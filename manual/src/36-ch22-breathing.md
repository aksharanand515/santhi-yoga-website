## Why breathing is special

Breathing is the only vital rhythm that runs automatically all our lives and can also be taken over at will. That dual control — automatic and voluntary — is why yoga has always treated the breath as the bridge between body and mind. This chapter describes the machinery; @ch:pranayama teaches the practices.

## The respiratory system

Air enters through the **nose** (or mouth), passes the **pharynx** and **larynx** — where the vocal folds form the glottis, whose partial closure produces the sound of ujjāyī — and travels down the **trachea**, which divides into the two main **bronchi** and then into ever smaller airways ending in the **alveoli**, tiny air sacs surrounded by capillaries where gas exchange occurs [@west2020].

The lungs themselves contain no muscle that could expand them. Each lung is covered by a thin membrane, the **pleura**, which lines the inside of the chest wall too; the thin film of fluid between the two layers links the lungs to the chest wall, so that when the chest expands, the lungs are drawn open with it.

::: keyconcept The nose first
Breathing through the nose warms, humidifies and filters the air, and slows the breath by increasing resistance [@west2020]. Almost all yogic breathing is done through the nose; the exceptions (śītalī, sītkārī, and some exhalations) are deliberate.
:::

## The muscles of breathing

### The diaphragm

The **diaphragm** is a dome-shaped sheet of muscle separating the chest from the abdomen. It arises from the lower six ribs and their cartilages, the xiphoid process and — through two muscular pillars, the **crura** — from the upper lumbar vertebrae, and inserts into its own central tendon [@moore2018]. It is supplied by the phrenic nerves (C3–C5).

When the diaphragm contracts, its dome moves downward and flattens, enlarging the chest from top to bottom. Because the abdominal organs below it cannot be compressed, they are pushed down and forward — the belly rises — and, as the abdomen resists, the diaphragm’s continued contraction lifts and widens the lower ribs [@detroyer2011]. In quiet breathing the dome moves perhaps one to two centimetres; in a maximal breath it moves several centimetres [@west2020].

!fig(diaphragm)

### The intercostal muscles and the ribs

Between the ribs lie the **intercostal muscles**. The **external intercostals** are active in inspiration, helping to raise the ribs; the **internal intercostals** (their interosseous part) assist forced expiration [@moore2018]. The ribs move in two ways:

- The **upper ribs** move mainly in a **“pump-handle”** fashion: as they rise, the sternum moves forward and up, increasing the front-to-back depth of the chest.
- The **lower ribs** move mainly in a **“bucket-handle”** fashion: they swing up and out, widening the chest from side to side.

!fig(ribs)

### Accessory muscles

When more air is needed — or when breathing becomes habitually tense — **accessory inspiratory muscles** join in: the scalenes and sternocleidomastoid lift the upper ribs and sternum; the pectoralis minor and others assist when the arms are fixed [@west2020]. Habitual reliance on these muscles at rest (“clavicular” or upper-chest breathing) is associated with tension in the neck and shoulders and is one reason students are taught full, diaphragmatic breathing first.

### Muscles of exhalation

At rest, **exhalation is passive**: the diaphragm relaxes, and the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall returns them to their resting position. In forced or active exhalation — speaking loudly, coughing, kapālabhāti — the **abdominal muscles** (transversus abdominis, obliques, rectus abdominis) contract to push the abdominal contents up against the diaphragm, and the internal intercostals draw the ribs down [@west2020].

## How air moves: pressure

Air moves from higher to lower pressure. When the chest expands, pressure in the lungs falls slightly below atmospheric pressure and air flows in; when it recoils or is compressed, pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out [@hall2021]. The pressure differences in quiet breathing are only a few centimetres of water — which is why small changes in posture and tension can make breathing noticeably easier or harder.

## Abdominal pressure and posture

The diaphragm is not only a breathing muscle. Together with the abdominal wall and the pelvic floor it forms a pressurised cylinder around the abdominal organs, and it contracts to raise **intra-abdominal pressure** when we lift, push or stabilise the trunk — even while it continues to breathe [@hodges2000]. This is the physiological basis of the yogic locks (@ch:bandhas-mudras-mantra) and of the connection between steady breathing and a stable trunk in postures such as plank and crow.

## Lung volumes

!fig(spirogram)

In quiet breathing we move only about half a litre of air in each breath (the **tidal volume**). A great deal more can be moved: the **inspiratory reserve volume** above it and the **expiratory reserve volume** below it. Some air — the **residual volume** — always remains in the lungs [@hall2021]. “Full yogic breathing” uses these reserve volumes deliberately, which is why it feels so different from ordinary breathing, and why it should never be forced.

## Gas exchange and the control of breathing

In the alveoli, oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out. The rate and depth of breathing are controlled by groups of neurons in the **brainstem** (medulla and pons), which generate the basic rhythm automatically [@hall2021].

Crucially, the strongest chemical drive to breathe is not a lack of oxygen but a rise in **carbon dioxide** (and the accompanying acidity), sensed mainly by **central chemoreceptors** in the brainstem. Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies respond to falling oxygen and rising carbon dioxide [@hall2021]. This has two major practical consequences:

1. **Breath-holding.** The urge to breathe during retention comes chiefly from rising CO₂, not falling oxygen.
2. **Over-breathing.** Breathing faster or more deeply than the body needs — as can happen in rapid breathing practices — washes out CO₂ (**hypocapnia**). This makes the blood more alkaline and constricts blood vessels in the brain, producing light-headedness, tingling in the fingers and around the mouth, and sometimes cramps or anxiety [@west2020].

::: safety Never over-breathe before holding the breath — especially in water
Rapid breathing before a breath-hold removes CO₂ and so delays the urge to breathe, while oxygen continues to fall. Consciousness can be lost without warning. This mechanism, described in underwater swimmers decades ago [@craig1961], has caused many drownings. **Never combine vigorous breathing and long retention, never practise breath-holding in or near water, and never practise intense prāṇāyāma while driving or standing.**
:::

## Breathing and the heart

Heart rate rises slightly with each inhalation and falls with each exhalation — **respiratory sinus arrhythmia** (RSA), a normal sign of a healthy, flexible autonomic nervous system [@lehrer2014].

!fig(rsa)

When breathing is slowed to around six breaths a minute (for example, about five seconds in and five seconds out), these oscillations of heart rate become larger and more regular, and they interact with the rhythms of blood pressure control (the **baroreflex**). Slow breathing at this rate has been shown in many small studies to increase heart rate variability and baroreflex sensitivity, and is associated with reports of calm and reduced anxiety [[S]] [@russo2017; @zaccaro2018; @lehrer2014]. In one study of yoga beginners, slow breathing with equal inhalation and exhalation increased baroreflex sensitivity most; adding ujjāyī did not increase the effect further [@mason2013].

::: evidence Exhalation and calm
Many teachers say that a longer exhalation “activates the parasympathetic system”. The idea has a physiological basis — heart rate slows during exhalation, and vagal influence on the heart is greater then — and a small randomised study found that daily cyclic breathing with long exhalations improved mood and reduced resting respiratory rate more than an equivalent period of mindfulness meditation [@balban2023]. But the evidence is still limited, effects are modest and individual responses vary. Present it as “tends to be calming” rather than as a switch.
:::

## Breathing and movement

In most postures the breath and the movement support each other mechanically:

- **Inhalation** accompanies **opening and extending** movements — lifting the arms, arching the spine, raising the chest — because inhalation itself extends the thoracic spine slightly and lifts the ribs.
- **Exhalation** accompanies **folding, twisting and lowering** — forward bends, twists, coming down — because exhalation draws the ribs down and the abdomen in, making space for flexion and rotation.

This is why the Sivananda sun salutation pairs each position with an inhalation or exhalation, and why cues such as “inhale to lengthen, exhale to fold” feel natural.

::: tip The breath as the teacher’s instrument
Watch the breath before you watch the posture. A student holding their breath, breathing through the mouth, or lifting the shoulders to breathe is working beyond their present capacity. “Can you breathe easily here?” is one of the most useful questions in teaching.
:::

## Breathing in prāṇāyāma, meditation and relaxation

Table: Connecting breathing physiology to yoga practice {#tab:breath-practice}

| Practice | Main mechanical feature | Physiological effect (current understanding) |
|---|---|---|
| Diaphragmatic breathing in relaxation | slow, abdominal, nasal | reduced accessory-muscle activity; tends to calm |
| Full yogic breath | uses reserve volumes; abdomen, lower ribs, upper chest | greater tidal volume; must be slow to avoid over-breathing |
| Ujjāyī | partial closure of the glottis | increases airway resistance, slows breathing, provides an audible focus |
| Kapālabhāti | rapid active exhalation by the abdominal muscles, passive inhalation | increases ventilation; can cause light-headedness through CO₂ loss |
| Nāḍī śodhana | slow, alternate-nostril breathing | slows breathing; lateralised effects unproven [@saoji2019] |
| Bhrāmarī | prolonged exhalation with humming | slows breathing; greatly increases nasal nitric oxide [@weitzberg2002] |
| Breath retention (kumbhaka) | apnoea | rising CO₂; blood-pressure changes; needs care |

::: takeaways
- The lungs are expanded by the diaphragm, intercostals and (when needed) accessory muscles; quiet exhalation is passive, forced exhalation uses the abdominal muscles.
- The diaphragm descends and flattens in inhalation, displacing the abdomen and widening the lower ribs; upper ribs move like a pump handle, lower ribs like a bucket handle.
- The diaphragm also contributes to intra-abdominal pressure and trunk stability.
- Carbon dioxide is the main chemical driver of breathing; over-breathing causes hypocapnia, and over-breathing before breath-holding is dangerous.
- Slow breathing near six breaths a minute enlarges respiratory sinus arrhythmia and increases baroreflex sensitivity; calming effects are plausible but modest.
- Inhalation supports extension and opening; exhalation supports folding, twisting and lowering.
:::
