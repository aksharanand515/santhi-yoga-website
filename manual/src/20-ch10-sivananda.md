## Swami Sivananda (1887–1963)

Swami Sivananda was born Kuppuswami on 8 September 1887 at Pattamadai, in present-day Tamil Nadu [[H]] [@dlshq2024]. He trained in medicine and for several years served as a doctor in Malaya, where he became known for his compassion towards poor patients. In 1923 he left medical practice to seek spiritual life, and in 1924 took monastic initiation (*sannyāsa*) at Rishikesh, in the Himalayan foothills, becoming Swami Sivananda Saraswati.

For years he lived austerely, meditating, nursing the sick and serving the wandering monks of Rishikesh. In 1936 he founded **The Divine Life Society**, and in 1948 the Yoga-Vedanta Forest Academy. He wrote more than two hundred books in simple English on every aspect of yoga and Vedānta — among them *The Science of Pranayama* (1935) — and sent his disciples to teach across India and the world [@dlshq2024; @sivananda1935]. He died (attained *mahāsamādhi*) in 1963.

Sivananda’s teaching is famous for its practicality and its breadth. He wrote for householders as well as monks, stressed daily discipline and selfless service, and taught that all the paths of yoga should be practised together. His short motto summarises the whole:

::: verse The motto of Swami Sivananda
**Serve · Love · Give · Purify · Meditate · Realise**
:::

His disciples founded several of the most influential modern schools, including Swami Vishnudevananda (Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Centres), Swami Satyananda (the Bihar School of Yoga), Swami Satchidananda (Integral Yoga), Swami Chidananda (who succeeded him as president of the Divine Life Society) and Swami Sivananda Radha.

## Swami Vishnudevananda (1927–1993)

Swami Vishnudevananda was born on 31 December 1927 in **Kerala** [[H]]. As a young man he encountered one of Sivananda’s pamphlets and went to Rishikesh, where he became a monk and soon a renowned teacher of Haṭha Yoga at the ashram. In 1957 Sivananda sent him to the West with the words, often quoted, “People are waiting.” He founded the first **Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Centre** in Montreal in 1959, published ***The Complete Illustrated Book of Yoga*** in 1960 [@vishnudevananda1960], and in 1969 led the first **Sivananda Yoga Teachers’ Training Course** — one of the first yoga teacher trainings offered in the West [@sivanandaorg2019]. In 1978 he inaugurated the Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Dhanwantari Ashram at **Neyyar Dam**, near Thiruvananthapuram, in his home state. Known also for his “peace flights” over conflict zones, he died in 1993.

## The five points of yoga

Vishnudevananda condensed the teachings of his master into five practical points for modern life [@vishnudevananda1960; @lidell1983]:

!fig(fivepoints)

1. **Proper exercise (āsana)** — keeps the body flexible and strong, and the spine supple, through postures held with attention rather than speed.
2. **Proper breathing (prāṇāyāma)** — full, rhythmic breathing, and specific practices to regulate the breath and calm the mind.
3. **Proper relaxation (śavāsana)** — conscious release of tension in body and mind, practised at the beginning, between postures and at the end of every class.
4. **Proper diet (vegetarian)** — a simple, fresh, vegetarian diet, in keeping with *ahiṃsā* and the sattvic ideal.
5. **Positive thinking and meditation (Vedānta and dhyāna)** — the discipline of the mind, leading to meditation and self-knowledge.

::: keyconcept The five points as a curriculum
The five points map neatly onto a whole life, not only a class: the first three happen on the mat, the fourth in the kitchen, the fifth everywhere. They also form a teaching sequence for beginners: most students arrive for the first point and discover the others gradually.
:::

## The Yoga of Synthesis

Sivananda taught that human beings have four main faculties — action, emotion, intellect and will — and that each should be developed through its own path: karma yoga, bhakti yoga, jñāna yoga and rāja yoga. Practised together they produce a balanced person; practised alone, each can become lopsided (@ch:four-paths). In a Sivananda ashram the day includes all four: selfless work, devotional chanting, study and lecture, and meditation, as well as Haṭha practice.

::: note A typical ashram day
Sivananda ashrams follow a rhythm broadly like this: rising before dawn; morning *satsaṅg* (silent meditation, chanting and a short talk); a two-hour āsana and prāṇāyāma class; a late-morning meal; karma yoga (service); a lecture or study session; a second āsana class; an early evening meal; evening satsaṅg; and early rest. Timings vary between ashrams.
:::

## The Sivananda class

The traditional class — around 90 minutes, sometimes two hours — follows an order designed to prepare, practise and integrate [@lidell1983]:

Table: The structure of a traditional Sivananda class {#tab:siv-class}

| Stage | Practice | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening prayer or chant; relaxation in śavāsana | arrive; let the body and mind settle |
| 2 | Prāṇāyāma: kapālabhāti and anuloma viloma (alternate-nostril breathing) | energise and balance; steady the mind before movement |
| 3 | Sūrya namaskār (several rounds) | warm the whole body; link breath and movement |
| 4 | Leg raises (single and double) | strengthen the abdomen and lower back; prepare for headstand |
| 5 | The twelve basic postures, with relaxation between them | the core of the practice |
| 6 | Final relaxation (10–15 minutes) | integrate; systematic relaxation; pratyāhāra |
| 7 | Closing chant | end with attention and gratitude |

## The twelve basic postures

At the heart of the class are twelve postures, practised in a fixed order [@vishnudevananda1960; @lidell1983]:

!fig(twelve)

The order is not arbitrary. The tradition explains it in terms of balancing the spine and the systems of the body, and some of its logic is also clear in modern terms [[I]]:

- **Inversions first** (headstand, shoulderstand, plough), while the student is fresh and attention is sharp — they demand the most concentration and carry the most risk if done tired.
- **Counterposes follow immediately**: fish after shoulderstand and plough to reverse the neck flexion.
- **Forward and backward bends alternate**: the seated forward bend is followed by three backbends of increasing intensity (cobra, locust, bow), each with rest in between.
- **The twist** follows the backbends, bringing the spine through rotation.
- **The arm balance** (crow, or peacock for advanced students) builds strength and concentration.
- **Standing postures come last** — standing forward bend and triangle — before the final relaxation.

This is the reverse of most modern styles, which begin standing and end inverted; neither order is “correct”, but the traditional order works well for the Sivananda method of long holds with rest between postures. Section II teaches each of the twelve in detail (@ch:twelve-asanas).

::: tip Teaching the sequence to beginners
Beginners do not attempt all twelve in full. A typical first-month class includes preparation for headstand (child’s pose, dolphin), half shoulderstand against the wall or with support, a modified plough, supported fish, and simpler versions of the rest. The *sequence* is preserved; the *forms* are adapted. This is exactly the Sivananda method.
:::

## Santhi Yoga School and the lineage

Santhi Yoga School teaches in this tradition. Its daily class follows the traditional structure — opening relaxation, prāṇāyāma, sūrya namaskār, the classical postures held with breath and attention, and a long final relaxation — and its teacher training rests on the five points, the Yoga of Synthesis and the twelve basic postures. At the same time, Santhi is **an independent school, not an official Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Centre**. We teach what we have received with gratitude, and we take responsibility for how we teach it — including the modern anatomy, safety and teaching methods in this manual.

::: takeaways
- Swami Sivananda (1887–1963), a physician turned monk, founded the Divine Life Society in 1936 and taught a practical, synthetic yoga through hundreds of books and many disciples.
- Swami Vishnudevananda (1927–1993), born in Kerala, founded the Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Centres from 1959, ran the first Sivananda teacher training in 1969 and founded the Neyyar Dam ashram in 1978.
- The five points: proper exercise, breathing, relaxation, diet, and positive thinking and meditation.
- The Yoga of Synthesis combines karma, bhakti, jñāna and rāja yoga.
- The Sivananda class moves from relaxation and prāṇāyāma through sun salutation and leg raises to the twelve basic postures and final relaxation; the twelve begin with inversions and end with standing poses.
:::
