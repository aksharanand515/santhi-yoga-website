## Yoga as a darśana

By the early centuries CE, Brahmanical thought had organised itself into schools, or *darśana*s (“ways of seeing”). Six were eventually recognised as orthodox: Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika (logic and natural philosophy), Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta (interpretation of the Veda) and **Sāṃkhya and Yoga**, which were paired as theory and practice. When Indian philosophers speak of “Yoga” as a school, they mean the system of the *Yoga Sūtra*.

## Sāṃkhya: the map beneath the practice

Sāṃkhya (“enumeration”) is one of the oldest Indian philosophies; its classical text is Īśvarakṛṣṇa’s *Sāṃkhyakārikā*, of the fourth or fifth century CE [[H]] [@larson1979]. It is a **dualism**: reality consists of two fundamentally different principles.

- **Puruṣa** is pure consciousness — the witness, the seer. It does not act, change or suffer. There are many puruṣas, one for each being.
- **Prakṛti** is nature or matter in the widest sense — everything that can be experienced, including the mind. Prakṛti is made of three strands, the **guṇas**: *sattva* (lucidity), *rajas* (activity) and *tamas* (inertia).

Unmanifest prakṛti evolves into twenty-three further principles (*tattva*s): the intellect (*buddhi* or *mahat*), the sense of “I” (*ahaṃkāra*), the mind (*manas*), the five organs of perception and five of action, the five subtle elements and the five gross elements.

!fig(samkhya)

The crucial and surprising point is that **the mind is on the side of matter**. Thinking, feeling, deciding and remembering are all movements of prakṛti. Consciousness itself is puruṣa, which only witnesses them. Suffering arises because puruṣa is confused with the movements of the mind it observes — as if the audience believed it was the film. Liberation is the discriminating recognition (*viveka*) that seer and seen are different.

::: keyconcept Why this matters in the practice hall
When a teacher says “observe your breath” or “notice the thought, and let it go”, they are applying Sāṃkhya-Yoga: the practice of standing back from the contents of experience as the witness of them. You can teach this without teaching the metaphysics — but you should understand where it comes from.
:::

## The Yoga Sūtra

The *Yoga Sūtra* consists of 195 aphorisms (196 in some editions) in four chapters called *pāda*s. A *sūtra* (“thread”) is a condensed statement meant to be memorised and explained by a teacher. Almost every sūtra is ambiguous without a commentary, and the text has always been studied with the commentary (*bhāṣya*) attributed to Vyāsa — which recent scholarship suggests may be Patañjali’s own [@maas2013]. Later commentators include Vācaspati Miśra (tenth century), King Bhoja (eleventh century) and Vijñānabhikṣu (sixteenth century) [@bryant2009; @white2014].

Table: The four chapters of the Yoga Sūtra {#tab:padas}

| Pāda | Sūtras | Theme |
|---|---:|---|
| 1 · *Samādhi pāda* | 51 | what yoga is; the mind and its fluctuations; practice and detachment; Īśvara; obstacles; kinds of samādhi |
| 2 · *Sādhana pāda* | 55 | the practice: kriyā yoga, the kleśas, suffering and its cause, the eight limbs (first five) |
| 3 · *Vibhūti pāda* | 55–56 | the inner limbs; saṃyama; the extraordinary powers and their dangers |
| 4 · *Kaivalya pāda* | 34 | the mind, karma and impressions; liberation (*kaivalya*) |

## The mind and its fluctuations (YS 1.1–1.16)

The text begins: *atha yogānuśāsanam* — “Now, the teaching of yoga” (1.1). The word *atha*, “now”, is traditionally read as marking a beginning that follows preparation: the student is ready. The definition follows (1.2), and then its consequence: “Then the seer abides in its own nature” (1.3). Otherwise, the seer takes on the form of the fluctuations (1.4).

*Citta* is the whole mind-field; *vṛtti*s are its movements or “turnings”. Patañjali lists five kinds (1.5–11), each of which may be afflicted (*kliṣṭa*) or unafflicted (*akliṣṭa*):

Table: The five vṛttis (YS 1.6–1.11) {#tab:vrittis}

| Vṛtti | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *pramāṇa* | valid cognition — through perception, inference or reliable testimony | seeing that the room is dark |
| *viparyaya* | error, a cognition not matching its object | mistaking a rope for a snake |
| *vikalpa* | conceptualisation without a real object; verbal construction | “the horns of a hare” |
| *nidrā* | sleep — a cognition whose content is absence | dreamless sleep |
| *smṛti* | memory — retention of experienced objects | remembering yesterday’s class |

The inclusion of sleep and memory is instructive: yoga does not aim at replacing “bad” thoughts with good ones, but at stilling *all* the movements — even pleasant ones — so that awareness can know itself.

How is this done? Through ***abhyāsa*** and ***vairāgya*** (1.12). Practice is steady effort to remain in stillness (1.13), and it becomes firmly grounded when cultivated **for a long time, without interruption and with devotion** (*dīrgha-kāla-nairantarya-satkāra*, 1.14). Detachment is the mastery of one who no longer thirsts for objects seen or heard of (1.15); its highest form is indifference even to the guṇas themselves (1.16).

::: verse Yoga Sūtra 1.14
स तु दीर्घकालनैरन्तर्यसत्कारासेवितो दृढभूमिः ।

*sa tu dīrgha-kāla-nairantarya-satkārāsevito dṛḍha-bhūmiḥ*

Practice becomes firmly grounded when it is cultivated for a long time, without interruption, and with devotion.
:::

## Īśvara (YS 1.23–1.29)

Among the means to samādhi Patañjali includes ***īśvara-praṇidhāna***, devotion or surrender to Īśvara, “the Lord” (1.23). Īśvara is a special puruṣa, untouched by affliction, action or its fruits (1.24), the teacher of even the ancient teachers (1.26). Its designator is the sacred syllable *Oṃ* (1.27), and its repetition with contemplation of its meaning is a practice (1.28) which brings inward-turned awareness and removes obstacles (1.29). Scholars debate how theistic Patañjali’s system is; in practice, the sūtras leave room both for devotional students and for those who understand Īśvara as an ideal of pure awareness [[I]] [@bryant2009].

## Obstacles and their remedies (YS 1.30–1.39)

Every practitioner meets obstacles. Patañjali names nine (1.30): illness, mental dullness, doubt, carelessness, laziness, sensual indulgence, false perception, failure to reach a stage of practice, and inability to maintain a stage once reached. They are accompanied by four symptoms (1.31): distress, dejection, trembling of the body and **irregular breathing**. The remedy is one-pointed practice (1.32), supported by four attitudes of mind:

::: verse Yoga Sūtra 1.33 · the four “immeasurables”
Friendliness towards the happy, compassion towards the suffering, gladness towards the virtuous, and equanimity towards the non-virtuous — through these the mind becomes clear.

*maitrī · karuṇā · muditā · upekṣā*
:::

These four attitudes are shared with Buddhism, where they are called the *brahmavihāra*s. For teachers they are a practical ethic: friendliness towards the confident student, compassion for the struggling one, delight in another’s progress, and equanimity towards the difficult one.

## Kriyā yoga and the kleśas (YS 2.1–2.17)

The second chapter opens with a practical yoga for those not yet able to sustain deep meditation: ***kriyā yoga***, consisting of *tapas* (disciplined effort), *svādhyāya* (study and self-study, including recitation) and *īśvara-praṇidhāna* (2.1). Its purpose is to cultivate samādhi and weaken the afflictions (2.2).

!fig(kleshas)

The five **kleśa**s — ignorance, I-am-ness, attachment, aversion and clinging to life — are the roots of suffering (2.3). Ignorance (*avidyā*) is the field in which the others grow (2.4); it is defined as taking the impermanent for permanent, the impure for pure, suffering for happiness and the non-self for the Self (2.5). The kleśas may be dormant, weak, interrupted or fully active (2.4); in their subtle form they are removed by returning them to their source, and their active forms by meditation (2.10–11).

Patañjali then makes the remarkable statement that for the discerning, all is suffering (2.15) — because of change, anxiety and conditioning, and because the guṇas are always in conflict. What can be done is to prevent suffering that has not yet come (2.16). Its cause is the conjunction of the seer and the seen (2.17), and its removal is discriminating knowledge (2.26), for which the **eight limbs** are the means (2.28–29). These are the subject of the next three chapters.

## Saṃskāra: how practice changes the mind

Throughout the text runs the idea of ***saṃskāra***, a latent impression left by every experience. Impressions produce tendencies, tendencies produce further vṛttis, which leave further impressions. Practice works by laying down impressions of stillness, which in turn suppress the impressions of distraction (1.50; 3.9–10). This is a remarkably modern-sounding model of habit and learning, and it underlies the Sūtra’s insistence on long, uninterrupted practice [[I]].

## Powers and liberation (YS 3–4)

The third chapter describes *saṃyama* — concentration, meditation and absorption practised together on one object (3.4) — and the extraordinary powers (*siddhi*s) that the tradition says arise from it, such as knowledge of past and future or of other minds. These are traditional claims [[T]], and Patañjali himself warns that they are obstacles to samādhi even if they appear as powers to the outward-turned mind (3.37). The fourth chapter discusses the mind, karma and time, and closes with ***kaivalya***, the “aloneness” of puruṣa: the guṇas, having served their purpose, return to their source, and pure consciousness is established in its own nature (4.34).

::: tip Reading the Sūtras with students
Choose one sūtra per week. Write it on a board in transliteration and translation. Explain it in two sentences, connect it to the practice students have just done, and leave it. The sūtras were designed to be carried in memory and chewed slowly.
:::

::: takeaways
- Classical Yoga rests on Sāṃkhya dualism: consciousness (puruṣa) is distinct from nature (prakṛti), which includes the mind.
- The Yoga Sūtra has four chapters (samādhi, sādhana, vibhūti, kaivalya) and around 195 aphorisms, studied with commentary.
- The five vṛttis are valid cognition, error, conceptualisation, sleep and memory; they are stilled by long, uninterrupted, devoted practice and by detachment.
- Nine obstacles, with symptoms including irregular breathing, are met by one-pointed practice and the four attitudes of friendliness, compassion, gladness and equanimity.
- Kriyā yoga (tapas, svādhyāya, īśvara-praṇidhāna) weakens the five kleśas, rooted in avidyā.
:::
