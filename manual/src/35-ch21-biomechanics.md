::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- define mobility, stability, flexibility, strength, active and passive range of motion, end range and load tolerance;
- distinguish tensile loading from compression and explain how each limits movement;
- describe the mechanics of balance and the roles of proprioception and motor control;
- explain how the body moves and is loaded in forward bends, backbends, twists, lateral bends, inversions, arm balances, hip openers, standing and seated postures;
- use these principles to observe students and choose appropriate modifications.
:::

## Key concepts

### Mobility and stability

**Mobility** is the ability to move a joint or region through its available range. **Stability** is the ability to control position and movement — to resist unwanted motion. They are not opposites but partners: a joint needs enough mobility for the task and enough control to use that mobility safely. The shoulder and the lumbar spine illustrate the two sides of this balance (@ch:skeleton).

### Flexibility and range of motion

**Flexibility** usually means the range through which a joint can be moved, often passively. Two measures matter:

- **Passive range of motion (PROM)**: the range reached with external help — gravity, a strap, the hands, a teacher.
- **Active range of motion (AROM)**: the range reached and held by the person’s own muscles.

!fig(rom)

AROM is almost always smaller than PROM. The gap is range the student can reach but not control — and it is where many injuries in yoga happen, because load arrives at a position the muscles cannot support. A useful principle is to **build active control in the range you already have** before seeking more passive range.

### Strength

**Strength** is the capacity of muscles to produce force. In yoga it is expressed mostly as holding (isometric) and controlled movement (concentric and eccentric) against body weight (@ch:muscle-principles). Strength at the end of a range turns passive flexibility into usable mobility.

### Tension and compression

When a posture stops, something has limited it [@clark2016]:

- **Tension (tensile loading)** — tissues on the side being lengthened are pulled taut: muscles, fascia, ligaments, joint capsules, even nerves. Tension limits are felt as pulling or stretching, usually on the *opposite* side from the direction of movement.
- **Compression** — tissues on the side the body is moving towards are pressed together: soft tissue against soft tissue (belly against thighs), or bone against bone. Compression limits are felt as blocking, pinching or a hard stop, on the *same* side as the movement.

!fig(tension)

| | Tension limit | Compression limit |
|---|---|---|
| Where felt | on the side being lengthened | on the side moved towards |
| Quality | stretching, pulling | blocking, pinching, hard stop |
| Changes with practice? | often, gradually | soft-tissue compression sometimes; bone-on-bone compression, no |
| Teaching response | patience, breath, time, props | change the angle, use props, accept the limit |

### End range and load tolerance

**End range** is the last part of a joint’s range, where passive tissues become taut and the joint surfaces are least congruent. Loading a joint at end range — especially repeatedly, quickly, or with added force from a teacher or momentum — places the greatest demand on ligaments, capsules and discs.

**Load tolerance** (or capacity) is the amount of load a tissue can accept without irritation. It is not fixed: tissues adapt to loads they meet regularly and progressively, and lose capacity with disuse. Injury risk rises when load increases faster than capacity can adapt [@soligard2016] — the principle behind gradual progression.

!fig(load)

::: keyconcept The two questions of load
For any posture and any student, ask: **how much load**, and **where is it going?** Load that is shared across many joints and muscles, at moderate ranges, with steady breath, is well tolerated. Load concentrated in one joint, at end range, held with strain, is not — however beautiful the posture looks.
:::

### Balance, proprioception and motor control

A body is balanced when its **centre of mass** lies over its **base of support**. The wider and lower the base, the more stable the posture: triangle is more stable than tree, and tree more stable than standing on the toes. The nervous system maintains balance using three sources of information — vision, the vestibular system of the inner ear, and **proprioception**, the sense of position and movement from receptors in muscles, tendons, joints and skin [@proske2012] — and corrects small errors through the ankles, the hips and, if necessary, by stepping.

**Motor control** is the nervous system’s organisation of movement: which muscles fire, when, and how strongly. Much of what looks like “weakness” or “stiffness” in a beginner is really unfamiliar motor control, which improves rapidly with practice (@ch:nervous-system).

::: exercise Senses of balance
Stand in tree pose for 30 seconds with eyes open; then with the eyes closed; then with eyes open on a folded blanket. Notice how much you depend on each sense. Balance postures with the eyes closed train proprioception and the vestibular system — and are best taught near a wall.
:::

## How the body moves in each family of postures

### Forward bends

**Movement.** Flexion at the hips combined with flexion of the spine. In a well-organised forward bend, hip flexion dominates: the pelvis tilts forward over the thighs, and the spine follows.

**What limits it.** Chiefly tension in the hamstrings (and, with straight knees, the calves and the nerves at the back of the leg); compression of the belly against the thighs at the end.

**Loads.** Tension on the hamstrings and their attachment at the sitting bones; with the pelvis held back, flexion moves into the lumbar spine, increasing load on the discs and posterior ligaments. Seated forward bends load the spine less than standing ones held against gravity, but pulling with the arms can add considerable force.

**Teaching principles.** Sit on a blanket to allow the pelvis to tilt; bend the knees freely; fold from the hips with a long spine first, then allow the spine to round in a relaxed way; never pull or bounce. Tingling or electric sensations down the leg suggest nerve tension: bend the knees and reduce the stretch.

### Backbends

**Movement.** Extension of the spine, usually with extension at the hips (in bow, camel, wheel) and often flexion at the shoulders (in wheel).

**What limits it.** Tension at the front — hip flexors, abdominal wall, chest and shoulder muscles; compression at the back — the facet joints and spinous processes of the spine, especially in the lower lumbar region, which is the most mobile in extension.

**Loads.** Compression of the posterior elements of the lumbar spine; the neck when the head is dropped back under load.

**Teaching principles.** Lengthen before bending; distribute the extension — lift the sternum and upper back rather than pressing into the lower back; engage the legs and buttocks moderately so that the hips extend; keep the neck in line with the curve of the spine; approach gradually through cobra and locust before bow and wheel; counterpose with gentle flexion.

### Twists

**Movement.** Rotation of the spine in the transverse plane, often combined with some flexion or lateral flexion. The thoracic spine provides most rotation; the lumbar spine very little; the neck a great deal.

**What limits it.** Tension in the obliques, back muscles and rib-cage tissues; the facet joints of the lumbar spine.

**Loads.** Combined rotation and flexion under load is the combination most often associated with disc and spinal injury in biomechanical studies of the back [@mcgill2016]; seated twists with a lifted, lengthened spine keep flexion low.

**Teaching principles.** Sit tall (on a blanket if the lower back rounds); inhale to lengthen, exhale to turn; turn from the rib cage, not by levering the arms; let the pelvis move naturally in beginners rather than locking it; keep the neck’s rotation moderate; avoid deep, forced twists in students with osteoporosis [@sinaki2013].

### Lateral bends

**Movement.** Lateral flexion of the spine in the frontal plane, as in triangle and seated side bends.

**What limits it.** Tension on the lengthened side — quadratus lumborum, obliques, latissimus dorsi, intercostals; compression on the shortened side of the ribs and waist.

**Teaching principles.** Lengthen the spine first; keep the trunk in the frontal plane (not collapsing forward); emphasise length of the upper side rather than depth of the bend.

### Inversions

**Movement.** The body is upside down; the joints may be in neutral (headstand) or in strong flexion (shoulderstand and plough, in which the neck flexes towards its end range).

**Loads.** In headstand, part of the body weight passes through the head and cervical spine; in shoulderstand and plough, body weight rests on the shoulders and upper back while the neck is strongly flexed. Inversion also raises pressure in the veins of the head and the pressure inside the eyes [@baskaran2006; @jasien2015].

**Teaching principles.** Build strength and shoulder mobility first; in headstand, bear most weight through the forearms and shoulders and enter slowly and with control [@hector2015]; in shoulderstand, use folded blankets under the shoulders so that the neck flexes less and the cervical spine is not pressed into the floor; never turn the head while inverted; screen for contraindications (@ch:safety).

::: evidence What we know about inversions and the neck
Case reports describe neck injuries and rare strokes associated with inversions and extreme neck positions in yoga [@cramer2013ae]. A laboratory study of experienced practitioners found that the technique used to enter and hold headstand altered the load on the head and neck [@hector2015]. Neither shows that inversions are dangerous for healthy, well-prepared students; both support the traditional insistence on preparation, props and control.
:::

### Arm balances

**Movement.** Body weight is carried on the hands with the elbows bent (crow) or straight, the wrists extended, and the centre of mass brought over the hands by leaning forward.

**Loads.** Wrist extension under load; the shoulder girdle and elbows; the rotator cuff.

**Teaching principles.** Spread the fingers and press through the whole hand, especially the knuckles of the index finger and thumb (@fig:hand); keep the shoulder blades protracted (“push the floor away”); build time on the hands gradually; teach a controlled way out; place a cushion in front of the head for beginners.

### Hip openers

**Movement.** Mainly external rotation, abduction and flexion of the hip (lotus, butterfly, pigeon, cow-face).

**What limits it.** Soft-tissue tension (adductors, deep rotators, gluteals, capsule) and, very often, the shape of the bones of the hip [@clark2016].

**Loads.** When the hip reaches its limit, the rotational force travels to the next joint — the knee — which tolerates twisting badly.

**Teaching principles.** Rotation comes from the hip, not the knee; keep the foot of a bent leg flexed (dorsiflexed) to stabilise the knee; lift the pelvis on a cushion; never force a leg into lotus or press on a student’s knee. Pain in the groin or a pinching at the front of the hip may reflect bony impingement, for which more stretching is not the answer [@griffin2016].

### Standing postures

**Movement.** The legs form the foundation; the trunk is positioned over them. Standing postures load the joints of the lower limb through their functional range and demand balance.

**Teaching principles.** Build from the ground up: feet, then knees, then pelvis, then spine, arms, head. Track the knee in line with the second and third toes; in bent-knee postures keep the knee roughly over the ankle; adjust stance width and length to the student’s proportions; keep the pelvis and spine organised rather than forcing an ideal shape.

### Seated postures

**Movement.** The pelvis rests on the floor with the hips flexed and often rotated.

**What limits it.** Hamstrings and hip rotators pull the pelvis into posterior tilt, rounding the lower back.

**Teaching principles.** Raise the pelvis on a folded blanket or cushion until the knees are level with or below the hips; this allows the pelvis to tilt forward and the spine to lengthen without effort — the foundation of comfortable meditation.

## A practical checklist for observing a posture

Table: The teacher’s observation checklist {#tab:observe}

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| 1. Is the breath steady? | held breath, gasping, strain in the face = too much |
| 2. Where is the foundation? | even weight on feet or hands; knees tracking; stable base |
| 3. Where is the movement happening? | is it shared (hips and spine) or concentrated (lower back, neck)? |
| 4. Where is the load going? | end-range joints; hyperextended knees or elbows; sagging shoulders |
| 5. Tension or compression? | ask the student where they feel it |
| 6. Active or passive? | can they hold the range with their own muscles? |
| 7. Sthira and sukha? | steady *and* easy — or one without the other? |

::: takeaways
- Mobility needs stability; active range needs building within passive range.
- Movement is limited by tension (felt on the lengthened side) or compression (felt on the side moved towards); bone compression does not change with practice.
- Load concentrated at end range is poorly tolerated; tissues adapt to gradually progressive load.
- Balance depends on keeping the centre of mass over the base of support, using vision, the vestibular system and proprioception.
- Each family of postures has characteristic movements, limits, loads and teaching principles; the observation checklist applies to all.
:::

:::

::: practice
1. Measure a partner’s active and passive straight-leg raise (with a strap) and discuss the gap.
2. Take a backbend and explore distributing the movement: first let it happen in the lower back, then consciously lift the chest. Compare sensations.
3. Use the observation checklist (@tab:observe) on three classmates in the same posture; compare your notes with a partner.
:::
