::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- practise and teach each of the twelve basic postures of the Sivananda sequence, with preparation, technique, breathing and alignment;
- describe the joints, muscles, spinal actions, planes and loads involved in each posture;
- recognise common mistakes and limitations and offer appropriate modifications, beginner and advanced variations and props;
- apply the contraindications and precautions for each posture, and know when hands-on help is and is not appropriate;
- distinguish, for each posture, the effects traditionally attributed to it from what modern evidence supports.
:::

## How to use the posture monographs

The twelve basic postures are the heart of the Sivananda class (@ch:sivananda). Each is presented in a monograph with the same structure, so that you can compare them and later use the same framework for any posture you teach:

1. **Names, meaning and facts** — Sanskrit (IAST and Devanāgarī), English, an approximate pronunciation guide, the family of postures, level, hold and counterpose.
2. **Purpose** and **preparation**.
3. **Technique** step by step, **breathing** and **alignment**.
4. **Anatomy** — joints, muscles and their actions, spinal action and planes, and the **biomechanics** of how the posture loads the body.
5. **Common mistakes** and **common student limitations**.
6. **Contraindications and precautions**.
7. **Modifications, beginner and advanced variations** and **props**.
8. **Teaching** — safe cues and hands-on guidance.
9. **Transitions** and **counterpose**.
10. **Effects traditionally attributed** and **what modern evidence does and does not support**.

The illustrations show one way the posture may look; they are not a template every body must match (@ch:skeleton). Where a figure carries coloured overlays, **terracotta** marks muscles that are lengthening under load and **blue** marks muscles that are working; these are schematic locations.

::: safety Before teaching any inversion
Inversions come first in the Sivananda sequence and carry the highest demands. Before teaching headstand, shoulderstand or plough to any group, ask about neck problems, high blood pressure, heart disease, glaucoma and other eye conditions, pregnancy, osteoporosis, recent head injury and dizziness. Offer the supported alternatives (dolphin, half shoulderstand on blankets, legs up the wall) without making anyone feel singled out.
:::

## The sequence at a glance

!fig(twelve)

A brief relaxation in śavāsana (or makarāsana after the prone postures) follows each posture. In a 90-minute class the twelve take roughly 50–60 minutes, including relaxations; in a shorter class some are shortened or replaced by their preparations, but the order is kept.

!allasanas()

## Completing the sequence: final relaxation

After triangle, students lie down for the final relaxation described in @ch:preparation — tensing and relaxing the body, autosuggestion from the feet to the head, and relaxation of the mind — for ten to fifteen minutes. This is not an optional extra. It is the stage in which the effects of the practice are absorbed, and for many students it is the part of the class they value most.

::: takeaways
- The twelve basic postures follow a fixed order: inversions (headstand, shoulderstand, plough) with fish as counterpose; forward bend; three backbends of increasing depth (cobra, locust, bow); twist; arm balance; standing forward bend; triangle.
- Each posture should be taught with preparation, clear technique, breath, alignment and a planned way in and out.
- Every posture has modifications; preparations are not lesser versions but the right versions for many students.
- Inversions demand screening for contraindications; the neck is protected by forearm strength in headstand and blankets in shoulderstand and plough.
- Traditional effects should be taught as traditional; modern evidence for individual postures is limited, and some traditional claims (such as increased blood flow to the brain) are not supported by physiology.
:::

1. Which of the twelve is most challenging for you? Is the limit strength, mobility, bone shape, fear or understanding?
2. Which posture’s traditional description moves you most, and how will you share it honestly?
3. What would a class of the twelve look like for a group of students in their seventies?
:::

::: practice
1. Teach each of the twelve postures to a partner using only the “safe teaching cues” in its monograph plus one cue of your own.
2. For three postures, demonstrate the main posture, the beginner variation and one prop modification in sequence, explaining when you would offer each.
3. In groups of three (teacher, student, observer), practise spotting headstand at a wall with consent; the observer gives feedback on the teacher’s position and communication.
:::
