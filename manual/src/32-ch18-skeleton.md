## The skeleton at a glance

The adult skeleton typically has 206 bones: 80 in the **axial skeleton** (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) and 126 in the **appendicular skeleton** (the limbs and the girdles that attach them) [@moore2018]. In yoga the axial skeleton is the axis around which postures are organised; the appendicular skeleton supplies the base of support and the levers.

## The spine

!fig(spine)

### Regions and curves

The vertebral column usually consists of 7 cervical, 12 thoracic and 5 lumbar vertebrae, the sacrum (5 fused vertebrae) and the coccyx (3–5 small fused segments) [@moore2018]. In a newborn the whole spine forms a single forward-concave curve (the **primary** curve). As the child lifts its head and later stands, **secondary** curves develop in the neck and lower back. The adult spine therefore has four curves: cervical **lordosis**, thoracic **kyphosis**, lumbar **lordosis** and sacral kyphosis.

The curves are not defects to be flattened; they allow the spine to absorb load like a spring and to balance the head over the pelvis. Their depth varies considerably between healthy people. The cue “flatten your back” is anatomically misleading; “lengthen the spine” or “let the spine find its natural curves” is closer to the truth.

### The vertebra and the motion segment

A typical vertebra has a weight-bearing **body** in front and a **vertebral arch** behind, which encloses the vertebral foramen through which the spinal cord passes. Processes project from the arch: a **spinous process** backward (the bumps you feel along the back), two **transverse processes** sideways, and four **articular processes** forming the **facet (zygapophyseal) joints** with the vertebrae above and below [@bogduk2005].

!fig(vertebra)

Two adjacent vertebrae and the tissues between them form a **motion segment**: the intervertebral disc in front, two facet joints behind, and the ligaments and muscles around them. Spinal nerves leave the canal through the **intervertebral foramina** between each pair of vertebrae.

### The intervertebral discs

Each disc has a tough outer ring of fibrocartilage, the **annulus fibrosus**, and a gel-like centre, the **nucleus pulposus**. Discs make up roughly a quarter of the length of the column above the sacrum and allow movement between vertebral bodies while transmitting load [@moore2018]. They have almost no blood supply in adults and are nourished by diffusion, assisted by the changes in pressure that come with movement and rest.

::: evidence Spinal loading and posture
In a frequently cited study, pressure within a lumbar disc was measured with a sensor in a single healthy volunteer during daily activities [[S]] [@wilke1999]. Pressure was lowest lying down, similar in relaxed standing and relaxed sitting, roughly doubled when bending forward while standing, and much higher when lifting a weight with the back rounded. Such measurements help explain why loaded spinal flexion deserves care — but they come from very few people, and the human spine adapts to the loads it regularly meets. Imaging studies also show that disc bulges and “degenerative” changes are extremely common in people with no back pain at all, rising with age [@brinjikji2015]; findings on a scan do not by themselves predict pain.
:::

### How the regions move

The shape and orientation of the facet joints, the thickness of the discs and the presence of the ribs give each region its own pattern of movement [@neumann2017; @bogduk2005]:

Table: Movement of the spinal regions {#tab:spinal-regions}

| Region | Freest movements | Most limited | Yoga relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cervical** | flexion, extension, rotation (about half of neck rotation occurs between C1 and C2), lateral flexion | — (most mobile region) | vulnerable in inversions and deep backbends; shoulderstand and plough flex it strongly |
| **Thoracic** | rotation, lateral flexion | extension and flexion (limited by ribs and facet orientation) | where most twisting *should* happen; often stiff in extension — the true work of backbends |
| **Lumbar** | flexion and extension | rotation (only a few degrees in total) | backbends tend to concentrate here; do not “twist from the lower back” |
| **Sacrum** | small nutation / counternutation at the SI joints | — | moves with the pelvis; see below |

::: keyconcept Share the movement
The most common pattern of strain in yoga is movement concentrating where the spine is already most mobile — the lower neck and lower lumbar spine — while the stiffer thoracic spine does little. Good teaching distributes the movement: lengthen before bending, lift the chest in backbends, rotate the rib cage in twists, and keep the neck in line with the rest of the spine.
:::

## The pelvis and sacroiliac joints

!fig(pelvis)

The pelvis is a ring formed by the two **hip bones** (each fused from ilium, ischium and pubis) and the **sacrum**. In front, the hip bones meet at the cartilaginous **pubic symphysis**; behind, each meets the sacrum at a **sacroiliac (SI) joint**. The SI joints are strong, irregular joints bound by some of the body’s strongest ligaments; they move only a little — a few degrees at most — but that small movement matters for shock absorption [[S]] [@vleeming2012].

### Pelvic tilt

The pelvis sits on the heads of the femurs and can rotate forward and backward on them. This rotation, **pelvic tilt**, directly changes the lumbar curve, because the sacrum is the base of the spine.

!fig(pelvictilt)

- **Anterior tilt** (ASIS forward and down) increases the lumbar lordosis. It happens in the lift of the chest in cobra and at the start of a forward bend when the pelvis “leads”.
- **Posterior tilt** (ASIS back and up; tailbone under) flattens the lumbar curve. It happens when tight hamstrings pull the sitting bones down in a seated forward bend, forcing the lower back to round.

Understanding pelvic tilt explains why a student with tight hamstrings is taught to sit on a folded blanket in paścimottānāsana: raising the pelvis lets it tilt forward, so the fold can come from the hips rather than from a rounded lower back.

## The rib cage

Twelve pairs of ribs articulate with the thoracic vertebrae. Ribs 1–7 attach directly to the sternum by their own costal cartilage (“true” ribs); ribs 8–10 attach indirectly through the cartilage above (“false” ribs); ribs 11–12 end free in the abdominal wall (“floating” ribs) [@moore2018]. The rib cage protects the heart and lungs, stiffens the thoracic spine, and moves with every breath (@ch:breathing). Because the ribs connect to the thoracic vertebrae, a stiff rib cage limits thoracic extension and rotation — one reason breathing practice and backbends support each other.

## The shoulder girdle

!fig(shoulder)

The arm attaches to the trunk only through the **clavicle** at the sternoclavicular joint. The shoulder complex has four parts: the sternoclavicular joint, the acromioclavicular joint, the **glenohumeral joint** (the ball-and-socket joint between the humerus and the shallow glenoid of the scapula) and the **scapulothoracic** “joint” — the gliding of the scapula over the rib cage [@neumann2017].

The glenohumeral joint is the most mobile joint in the body: its socket is small relative to the head of the humerus and deepened only by a rim of cartilage, the labrum. That mobility is bought at the cost of stability, which depends largely on muscles, especially the rotator cuff (@ch:muscle-atlas). When the arm rises overhead, roughly two degrees of glenohumeral movement accompany every one degree of upward rotation of the scapula — the classic **scapulohumeral rhythm** [@neumann2017].

::: tip The shoulders in weight-bearing
In plank, downward dog, crow and headstand the shoulder girdle carries body weight. Two cues protect it: keep the shoulder blades **broad and gently protracted** (not collapsing between them) and keep the shoulders **away from the ears** (not shrugged or sagging). Students who have trouble raising the arms fully overhead often compensate by arching the lower back — watch for it in hasta uttānāsana and headstand.
:::

## The hip joint

The hip is a deep ball-and-socket joint: the femoral head sits well inside the acetabulum, held by a thick capsule and strong ligaments, making it far more stable — and less mobile — than the shoulder. Its movements are flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and internal and external rotation.

The shape of the hip varies strikingly between people [@clark2016; @neumann2017]. The angle between the femoral neck and shaft averages about 125° but ranges widely; the femoral neck is usually angled slightly forward (anteversion, averaging roughly 15°), again with wide variation; and the depth and direction of the socket differ. These differences — present from birth, not the result of practice — mean that for some people lotus or wide splits are impossible without injury, however long they stretch, because **bone meets bone**.

## The knee

The knee is essentially a hinge between femur and tibia, with a little rotation available when it is bent. Two C-shaped **menisci** deepen the flat tibial plateau; the **cruciate ligaments** (anterior and posterior) prevent forward and backward sliding of the tibia; the **collateral ligaments** limit sideways bending [@moore2018]. The kneecap (patella) glides in a groove on the femur within the quadriceps tendon.

!fig(knee)

The knee is the joint most at risk when a student forces the hip. In lotus, if the hip does not rotate outward sufficiently, the twisting force goes to the knee — and the knee, a hinge, cannot rotate safely under load. **The rule is: rotation comes from the hip; the knee is protected.**

## The ankle and foot

Each foot has 26 bones [@moore2018]. The **talocrural (ankle) joint** allows dorsiflexion and plantarflexion; the **subtalar** joint below it allows inversion and eversion. The foot’s arches — medial longitudinal, lateral longitudinal and transverse — are shaped by bone and ligament and supported dynamically by muscle.

!fig(foot)

In standing postures the foot is the foundation. Cueing weight evenly through three points — heel, big-toe mound and little-toe mound — gives a stable base. Arch height varies greatly between healthy people; a low arch is not in itself a problem to be corrected.

## The elbow, wrist and hand

The **elbow** combines a hinge (between humerus and ulna) with a pivot (between radius and ulna) that allows pronation and supination. Many people, especially the hypermobile, can hyperextend the elbow; in weight-bearing postures they should keep a micro-bend so that load is carried by muscle rather than by the joint’s end range.

The **wrist and hand** contain 27 bones: 8 carpals, 5 metacarpals and 14 phalanges [@moore2018]. The wrist was not designed as a weight-bearing joint in the way the ankle was. Plank, crow and other arm balances place it in strong extension under load (@fig:hand).

!fig(hand)

## Bones, variation and teaching

::: keyconcept Tension, compression and the shape of bones
When a movement stops, the limit is either **tension** — tissues on the opposite side being pulled — or **compression** — tissues or bones meeting on the same side [@clark2016]. Tension can often change with practice; compression by bone cannot. A teacher who assumes every limit is “tightness” will push some students into bone-on-bone compression. Ask: *Where do you feel it?* Sensation on the side being stretched suggests tension; a hard, blocked feeling on the side the body is folding towards suggests compression.
:::

This is the foundation of the approach to alignment taken in this manual: **alignment serves the student’s body; the student’s body does not serve an ideal image**. The illustrations in this book show one possible shape of each posture, not the correct one.

::: takeaways
- The spine’s four curves are normal and variable; the cervical and lumbar regions are most mobile, the thoracic region most limited in flexion–extension but freest in rotation.
- Discs and facet joints make up each motion segment; loaded flexion raises disc pressure, but imaging changes are common without pain.
- The pelvis tilts on the femoral heads, changing the lumbar curve; tight hamstrings cause posterior tilt in forward bends.
- The shoulder trades stability for mobility and relies on muscle; the hip is deep and stable, and its bone shape varies widely.
- The knee is a hinge that must be protected from twisting; the foot and hand are foundations whose loading can be taught.
- Movement limits come from tension or compression; bone shape sets a limit no amount of stretching can change.
:::
