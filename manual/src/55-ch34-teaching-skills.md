::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- construct clear, economical verbal cues and use different types of cue for different purposes;
- decide when and how to demonstrate, and demonstrate effectively;
- observe a class systematically, identify what matters most and give helpful feedback;
- use your voice with appropriate volume, pace, pitch, tone and silence, and care for it;
- communicate with presence and warmth, verbally and non-verbally.
:::

## Verbal cueing

A cue is any instruction that helps a student do or notice something. Good cues are **clear, brief, timed and purposeful**.

!fig(cuestructure)

### Building a cue

A reliable structure: **breath → body part → action/direction → (what stays still)**. “Inhale, lift the arms forward and up, keeping the shoulders soft.” Then **stop talking** and let students do it.

### Types of cue

Table: Types of verbal cue {#tab:cue-types}

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Instructional | move students into and out of postures | “Step the right foot back into a lunge.” |
| Breath | coordinate movement and breath | “Inhale here… exhale, fold.” |
| Alignment | organise the body safely and effectively | “Knee over the ankle.” |
| Safety / options | protect and include | “If your wrists complain, come onto your forearms.” |
| Action (external focus) | create an effective action with an image | “Press the floor away.” |
| Awareness / inquiry | turn attention inward | “Where do you feel this most?” |
| Energetic / imagery | evoke a quality | “Lengthen as if drawn up by a thread from the crown.” |
| Philosophical | connect practice to meaning | “Steady and easy — sthira, sukha.” |

Balance your cues. Beginners need mostly instructional, breath and safety cues; experienced students benefit from awareness and inquiry cues. External-focus action cues are often more effective for learning movement than internal anatomical cues [@wulf2013].

### Principles of good cueing

- **One cue at a time**, then pause.
- **From the ground up**: feet, legs, pelvis, trunk, arms, head — the foundation first.
- **Positive phrasing**: say what to do (“keep the knee over the ankle”) rather than what not to do (“don’t let your knee collapse”).
- **Timed with the breath**: instruct the movement on the breath that performs it.
- **Specific**: “lift the chest” rather than “open up”.
- **Consistent language**: use the same words for the same action every time; students learn your vocabulary.
- **Economical**: remove filler words (“just”, “kind of”, “go ahead and”, “I want you to”).
- **Left and right**: say them clearly; if you demonstrate facing the class, mirror (use your left when you say “right”) and say so.

::: tip Cue less, see more
Record yourself teaching a posture and count the words. Then teach it again with half as many. Most new teachers discover that the shorter version is clearer, and that the silences let them see the room.
:::

## Demonstration

Demonstration is powerful — people copy what they see more than what they hear — but it has costs: while demonstrating, you cannot observe; students may copy your body rather than find their own; and a teacher who demonstrates everything performs rather than teaches.

**When to demonstrate:** introducing a new posture or transition; showing a modification; clarifying a confusing instruction; with beginners and language differences.

**When not to:** in postures students already know; during holds, when you should be observing; when your version would be discouraging or unsafe to copy.

**How to demonstrate well:**

- Stand where everyone can see — raise yourself (on a stage or at the front) and turn side-on for sagittal-plane postures.
- Demonstrate the **version you want most students to do**, often the modified one; show variations as equals.
- Demonstrate **partially** — just the feet, just the transition — rather than the whole pose.
- Use a **student demonstration** (with consent) to show how a pose looks in a different body.
- Demonstrate, then **teach verbally while you walk around**.

## Observation

Observation is the skill that makes teaching responsive. It can be trained.

!fig(classroom)

### How to observe

1. **Scan the whole room** first — is everyone breathing, following, safe?
2. **Look at foundations** — feet and hands, then knees and hips.
3. **Look for the pattern, not the individual** — if six people do the same thing, your instruction was unclear; re-cue the class.
4. **Then look at individuals** — especially beginners, anyone who has told you of an injury, and anyone struggling.
5. **Move around**: see the room from the sides and back; different planes are visible from different angles.
6. **Watch faces and breath** as closely as shapes.

The observation checklist in @tab:observe applies to every posture.

### Giving feedback

- **Prioritise**: safety first, then the foundation, then refinements. Choose the one thing that matters most.
- **Address the group first** with a general cue; individualise only if needed.
- **Be specific and positive**: “Good — now let the knee move over the ankle,” rather than “Wrong”.
- **Be discreet**: go to the student and speak quietly, rather than calling out their name across the room.
- **Check the effect**: did the cue help? If not, try a different one, a demonstration or a prop.

## Voice

Your voice is your main instrument. In a two-hour class, you may speak for an hour or more.

Table: Qualities of the teaching voice {#tab:voice}

| Quality | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Volume | loud enough for the back of the room without strain; lower for relaxation — but still audible |
| Pace | slower than conversation; slower still in relaxation and meditation |
| Pitch | use your natural pitch; avoid a “yoga voice” that sounds unnatural |
| Tone | warm, steady, confident; match the tone to the part of the class (clear and energetic in standing postures; soft and steady in relaxation) |
| Articulation | clear consonants, especially for international students |
| Silence | planned pauses after each instruction; long silences in holds and relaxation |

### Caring for the voice

- Breathe from the diaphragm and support the voice with the breath rather than the throat.
- Warm up the voice before teaching (humming, bhrāmarī, gentle chanting).
- Drink water; avoid shouting over fans, traffic or music — reduce noise or use a microphone.
- Rest the voice between classes; seek professional help for persistent hoarseness.

## Communication and presence

Much of teaching is non-verbal. Students read your posture, facial expression, movement around the room and attention. Presence — being fully with the class, not in your notes, your phone or your own practice — is felt immediately.

- **Learn names** and use them.
- **Make eye contact** at the start and end of class.
- **Listen** — before class, after class and in the pauses.
- **Use humour** lightly, and never at a student’s expense.
- **Be yourself**: authenticity is more trustworthy than a borrowed persona.

::: cue Cueing tāḍāsana from the ground up
Stand with the feet together or hip-width apart… Feel the weight in the heels, the base of the big toes and the base of the little toes… Let the legs be strong, the kneecaps lifting softly… Let the pelvis rest level… Lengthen up through the spine… Let the shoulders soften down, the arms hang heavy… The crown of the head rises… Breathe… Stand.
:::

::: takeaways
- A clear cue combines breath, body part and direction, is given once, and is followed by silence.
- Use different types of cue for different purposes; cue from the ground up; phrase positively; be specific and economical.
- Demonstrate to introduce and clarify; do not demonstrate during holds when you should be observing.
- Observe systematically: the room, foundations, patterns, individuals, faces and breath; move around the room.
- Give prioritised, specific, positive and discreet feedback, and check its effect.
- Care for your voice; teach with presence and authenticity.
:::

::: reflect
1. Which of your verbal habits (filler words, “just”, over-talking) do you notice most?
2. When do you demonstrate because students need it, and when because you want to be seen?
3. What does your voice sound like when you are nervous? What helps?
:::

::: practice
1. Teach tāḍāsana and trikoṇāsana using only one cue at a time with a full breath of silence after each.
2. In groups of three, one teaches, one practises, one observes the teacher and counts filler words, demonstrations and cues given while not looking at students.
3. Practise observing a line of five classmates in downward dog for 30 seconds; list what you saw, prioritised by importance.
:::
