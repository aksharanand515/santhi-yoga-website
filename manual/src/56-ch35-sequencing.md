- choose appropriate counterposes;
- place rest and integration effectively, including the final relaxation.

## Why sequence matters

The same postures, in a different order, make a different class. Sequencing determines whether the body is ready for what is asked of it, whether the nervous system is stimulated or settled, whether the class feels coherent, and — often — whether it is safe. The Sivananda sequence is a carefully tested answer to the sequencing question (@ch:sivananda). Understanding *why* it works lets you teach it well and design other sequences when needed.

## Principles

1. **Preparation before demand.** Warm the body and prepare the specific regions a posture needs before asking for depth or load.
2. **Simple before complex; familiar before new.** Build from the known.
3. **Progression in intensity**, rising to a peak and then descending.
4. **Balance.** Move the spine in all its directions (flexion, extension, lateral flexion, rotation) and work both sides of the body equally.
5. **Counterpose.** After a strong movement in one direction, return the body gently towards neutral or the opposite.
6. **Rest and integration.** Short rests between postures; a long relaxation at the end.
7. **Fit to the students, the time of day and the setting** — a Kerala afternoon at 34 °C calls for a different class from a cool dawn.

## Warm-up and preparation

A warm-up raises body temperature, increases blood flow to muscles, mobilises joints through gentle range, and turns attention to the body and breath. In the Sivananda class, relaxation and prāṇāyāma settle the mind; sūrya namaskār warms the whole body; leg raises prepare the abdomen and hip flexors (@ch:preparation).

A good warm-up is **specific** as well as general. Look at the joints and muscles the main postures will use (@ch:mapping) and include movements that prepare them:

Table: Specific preparation for posture families {#tab:prep}

| Main work | Prepare with |
|---|---|
| Inversions | shoulder mobility (arm circles, cow-face arms), shoulder strength (dolphin, plank), trunk strength (leg raises), neck mobility (gentle rotations) |
| Backbends | spinal extension (cat–cow, sphinx, cobra), hip-flexor length (lunge), shoulder flexion (arms overhead), thoracic mobility |
| Forward bends | hamstring warm-up (leg raises, supine hamstring stretch), hip mobility, gentle spinal flexion |
| Twists | spinal mobility in all directions, hip mobility |
| Arm balances | wrist mobility and loading, plank, core strength, squats |
| Standing balances | foot and ankle mobility, leg strength, simpler balances |
| Hip openers | hip circles, gentle external rotation, adductor warm-up |

## Peak-pose sequencing

A **peak pose** is the most demanding posture of a class, towards which the whole sequence builds. Designing a peak-pose class:

1. **Choose the peak** suitable for the group.
2. **Analyse it** with the mapping table: joints, movements, muscles, loading.
3. **List what it requires** — strength, mobility, balance, confidence.
4. **Build preparatory postures** that develop each requirement, from simpler to closer approximations of the peak (“component poses”).
5. **Offer the peak with options** (@ch:levels).
6. **Counterpose and integrate**.

## Counterposes

A counterpose returns the body towards balance after a strong action. It should be **gentler** than the pose it follows and should move the main joints **in the opposite direction** without jolting.

Table: Common counterposes {#tab:counterposes}

| After | Counterpose | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulderstand and plough (neck flexion) | fish (neck and upper-back extension) | reverses cervical and thoracic flexion |
| Backbends | child’s pose; gentle forward bend; knees to chest | gentle spinal flexion |
| Deep forward bends | gentle backbend (cobra, bridge, tabletop with cow) | gentle spinal extension |
| Twists | twist to the other side; neutral spine | symmetry |
| Headstand | child’s pose, then rest | allows circulation and the neck to settle |
| Arm balances | wrist flexion and release; child’s pose | wrists and shoulders |
| Hip openers (external rotation) | gentle internal rotation (windscreen-wiper knees) | balance hip rotation |
| Standing sequences | rest lying down | lowers heart rate |

Counterposes are not an absolute rule. A gentle backbend does not require an immediate forward bend; the need increases with the intensity and duration of the preceding posture.

## Rest and integration

Rest is part of the practice, not a gap in it.

- **Between postures**: a few breaths in śavāsana, makarāsana or child’s pose lets heart rate and breath settle and effects be felt — the hallmark of the Sivananda method.
- **Final relaxation**: allow about 10–15 minutes in a 90-minute class, and at least 5 minutes in a 45–60-minute class. Never cut it short to fit in more postures.
- **After relaxation**: a transition to sitting, perhaps a short meditation, chant or silence, before students leave.

::: keyconcept Ending well
Students remember the end of a class more than the middle. A calm, unhurried ending — a long relaxation, a moment of silence, a closing word — integrates the practice and sends students out more steady than they came in.
:::

## The energetic shape of a class

!fig(classarc)

A well-sequenced class has a recognisable arc: arrival, warming, building, peak, descent and rest. The Sivananda class places its demanding inversions early and its most restful posture at the end, with a rhythm of effort and rest throughout.

::: tip Sequencing for the time of day
Morning classes can include more stimulating practice (kapālabhāti, sun salutations, backbends); evening classes more calming practice (forward bends, longer holds, lengthened exhalations, bhrāmarī). In the heat of a Kerala afternoon, reduce the number of fast rounds, lengthen rests, and add cooling breaths.
:::

::: takeaways
- Sequencing determines readiness, safety, coherence and the effect of a class.
- Core principles: preparation, simple to complex, progression, balance of spinal directions and sides, counterpose, rest and integration, fit to context.
- Warm-ups should be general and specific to the postures to come.
- Peak-pose classes analyse the peak and build its requirements through component poses.
- Counterposes are gentler movements in the opposite direction; rest and a long final relaxation are essential.
:::
