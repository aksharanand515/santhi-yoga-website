::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- describe what distinguishes beginner, intermediate and advanced students — beyond flexibility;
- teach an effective first class and beginners’ course;
- challenge experienced students safely and meaningfully;
- use tiered options, layering and group organisation to teach a mixed-level class well;
- decide what to teach when a class is more varied than planned.
:::

## What “level” means

Level is not flexibility. A flexible dancer in her first yoga class is a beginner; a stiff sixty-year-old who has practised daily for ten years is not. Level is better understood as a combination of:

- **familiarity** with the practices and the class structure;
- **body awareness** — knowing what one is doing and feeling;
- **self-regulation** — knowing when to rest, modify or stop;
- **strength, mobility and stamina** for the practices;
- **steadiness of breath and attention**.

Table: Characteristics and needs at each level {#tab:levels}

| | Beginner | Intermediate | Advanced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical experience | first weeks to months | regular practice for a year or more | many years of steady practice |
| Needs | clear, simple instructions; safety; confidence; understanding the structure | refinement; consistency; deeper understanding; more challenge | subtlety; inner focus; variations; guidance in pranayama and meditation |
| Common risks | doing too much; copying neighbours; not knowing when to stop | plateau; habits; over-confidence in inversions and backbends | overuse from repetition; ego; neglect of foundations |
| Teacher’s focus | foundation, breath, relaxation, the “why” | alignment within the student’s body, holds, transitions, prāṇāyāma | stillness, longer holds, advanced variations, self-practice |

## Teaching beginners

The first class shapes whether someone ever returns. Make it welcoming, clear and a little easier than you think necessary.

- **Before class:** greet each newcomer; ask about health, injuries and experience; show them the props and where to place their mat (near the middle, where they can see you and others).
- **Explain the structure** in a sentence: “We start lying down, then breathing, sun salutations and postures, with rest between, and a long relaxation at the end.”
- **Teach fewer things, more clearly.** Two or three rounds of sun salutation, simplified; six or eight of the twelve basic postures in preparation form.
- **Demonstrate more; talk less** — and walk around to check understanding.
- **Normalise rest and modification** from the start.
- **Finish on time**, with a proper relaxation, and ask afterwards how it felt.

::: tip A beginners’ course
Many schools teach beginners in a closed course of four to eight weeks, building the class piece by piece: week 1 relaxation and breathing; week 2 sun salutation; week 3 leg raises and inversions preparation; and so on, until the full class is familiar. Students who learn the structure first practise more safely in open classes later. Appendix G includes a beginners’ class plan.
:::

## Teaching experienced students

Experienced students need challenge, but more depth rather than more difficulty:

- longer holds and slower transitions;
- advanced variations for those who are ready, with preparation built into the sequence;
- refinement of breath, bandhas and dṛṣṭi (gaze);
- more prāṇāyāma and meditation;
- the freedom to practise with fewer instructions.

Watch for **overuse**: experienced students often have the most repetitive practices. Vary sequences and include strength and rest.

## Mixed-level classes

Most public classes are mixed. The key is **one class, several paths** through it.

### Tiered options

Teach the foundation of each posture to everyone, then offer tiers: “Stay here — or, if it suits you today, take the next step…” The foundation remains valuable for everyone; advanced students are not waiting, and beginners are not rushed.

!sequence(padahasta_beginner=Tier 1/knees bent, hands down; ardha_uttanasana=Tier 2/half lift, flat back; padahastasana=Tier 3/full fold, hands by ankles)

### Layering

Build a posture in layers, each one complete in itself: *feet → legs → pelvis → spine → arms → gaze → breath*. Students stop at the layer that is right for them.

### Organising the room

- Place beginners where they can see you and a steady student.
- Place experienced students where their practice will not distract beginners (e.g. at the back or sides) — and where you can still see them.
- Pair or group for partner work by experience when needed.

### Timing

Give the class a common rhythm — everyone enters and leaves postures together — so that no one is left behind or held up. Where variations take longer (e.g. headstand), give a clear “stay in child’s pose or dolphin until everyone comes down”.

::: keyconcept Teach the whole room
In a mixed class, the most important skill is not knowing advanced variations; it is **seeing** the whole room and **pacing** the class so that every student has a meaningful practice. When in doubt, teach to the least experienced student present, and offer the extras.
:::

## When the room surprises you

You planned an intermediate class and five newcomers walk in; or you planned gentle yoga and the room is full of athletes. Have a **flexible plan**: a core sequence that can be simplified or extended; spare postures in both directions; the confidence to drop your plan. The students in front of you are always more important than the plan in your notebook.

::: takeaways
- Level combines familiarity, body awareness, self-regulation, capacity and steadiness — not flexibility alone.
- Beginners need a warm welcome, the class structure, few clear instructions, demonstration and permission to rest.
- Experienced students need depth, refinement, variety and care for overuse.
- Mixed classes are taught as one class with several paths: tiered options, layering, room organisation and a common rhythm.
- Always teach the students in front of you, not the plan.
:::
