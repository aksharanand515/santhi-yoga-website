## Āsana

### What Patañjali says

Patañjali devotes only three sūtras to posture:

::: verse Yoga Sūtra 2.46–2.48
स्थिरसुखमासनम् ॥ प्रयत्नशैथिल्यानन्तसमापत्तिभ्याम् ॥ ततो द्वन्द्वानभिघातः ॥

*sthira-sukham āsanam · prayatna-śaithilyānanta-samāpattibhyām · tato dvandvānabhighātaḥ*

Posture should be steady and comfortable. It is perfected by relaxation of effort and by absorption in the infinite. From that, one is no longer assailed by the pairs of opposites.
:::

*Āsana* literally means “seat” — both the act of sitting and the thing one sits on. In the Yoga Sūtra it refers mainly to the seated posture for meditation [[H]]. Vyāsa’s commentary names about a dozen such postures, including the lotus (*padmāsana*), the hero (*vīrāsana*) and the auspicious (*bhadrāsana*) [@bryant2009]. The purpose is stillness: a body that no longer demands attention, so that attention can turn inward. The “pairs of opposites” (*dvandva*) — heat and cold, pleasure and pain — no longer disturb the one whose seat is mastered.

The second sūtra (2.47) is often read as a teaching method: posture is perfected by **relaxing effort** — releasing unnecessary tension — and by **absorbing the mind in the infinite**, sometimes interpreted as the image of Ananta, the endless serpent who supports the world [[I]].

### What Haṭha Yoga added

In Haṭha Yoga, āsana became a practice in its own right with its own benefits. The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* places it first: “Being the first limb of Haṭha Yoga, āsana is described first. It brings steadiness, health and lightness of body” (1.17) [@akers2002]. It then describes fifteen postures, some seated, some strenuous, such as *mayūrāsana*, the peacock. Over the following centuries the repertoire grew into the hundreds (@ch:hatha), and in the twentieth century āsana came to dominate yoga as it is practised worldwide.

::: keyconcept Sthira and sukha in every pose
Every posture — headstand as much as seated meditation — can be judged by Patañjali’s two words. **Sthira**: is it stable, alert, well-founded? **Sukha**: is there ease, space and a steady breath? A pose that is strong but strained lacks sukha; a pose that is comfortable but slack lacks sthira. Much of the art of teaching is helping each student find both.
:::

::: tip Cueing sthira and sukha
- *Sthira*: “Press the feet down evenly… feel the ground under you… let the legs be strong.”
- *Sukha*: “Soften the jaw… let the eyes be quiet… can the breath move a little more freely?”
- *Relaxation of effort* (2.47): “Notice any effort you don’t need — in the face, shoulders, hands — and let it go.”
:::

## Prāṇāyāma

### Patañjali’s account

The word *prāṇāyāma* combines *prāṇa* (breath, vital force) with *āyāma* (extension, restraint) — or, by another division, *prāṇa* and *yama* (control) [[I]]. Patañjali defines it as the regulation or cessation (*viccheda*) of the flow of inhalation and exhalation, practised once posture is established (2.49). He analyses it precisely:

- It has three movements: **external** (after exhalation), **internal** (after inhalation) and **suspended** (stopping) (2.50).
- It is observed in terms of **place** (*deśa* — where the breath is felt, how far it reaches), **time** (*kāla* — duration) and **number** (*saṅkhyā* — count of breaths), becoming long and subtle (*dīrgha-sūkṣma*) (2.50).
- A fourth kind goes beyond the external and internal spheres (2.51).
- Through it the covering over the inner light is diminished (2.52), and the mind becomes fit for concentration (2.53).

Notice that Patañjali describes prāṇāyāma as becoming **long and subtle**, not forceful. The dramatic breathing techniques of Haṭha Yoga — breath retention with locks, rapid abdominal breathing — belong to a later tradition, which also warns emphatically against forcing: “Just as lions, elephants and tigers are tamed gradually, so the breath is brought under control gradually; otherwise it kills the practitioner” (HYP 2.15) [@akers2002].

### The link between breath and mind

The central yogic insight about breathing is that breath and mind move together. The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* puts it directly: “When the breath is unsteady, the mind is unsteady; when the breath is steady, the mind is steady” (2.2) [[T]]. Patañjali lists irregular breathing among the symptoms of a distracted mind (1.31) and recommends the exhalation and retention of breath as one of the means of steadying it (1.34).

::: evidence Breath and the nervous system
Modern physiology supports a two-way link between breathing and mental state [[S]]. Breathing is the only vital rhythm that is both automatic and easily controlled at will, and slow, regular breathing — around six breaths a minute — increases heart rate variability and baroreflex sensitivity and is associated with reduced subjective anxiety in many studies [@zaccaro2018; @russo2017]. The mechanisms and the size of effects are still being studied, and most trials are small; but the traditional claim that steadying the breath steadies the mind is broadly consistent with current evidence. The anatomy and physiology are covered in @ch:breathing and the practices in @ch:pranayama.
:::

## Pratyāhāra

**Pratyāhāra** is the fifth limb, a bridge between the outer and inner practices. Patañjali defines it as the senses imitating the nature of the mind, as it were, by disengaging from their objects (2.54); from it comes supreme mastery of the senses (2.55). The Bhagavad Gītā’s image is the tortoise drawing in its limbs (2.58).

Pratyāhāra is not sensory deprivation or suppression. The senses keep working; attention simply stops being dragged outward by them. Think of reading an absorbing book in a noisy café: the sound is there, but it does not take you. Pratyāhāra is that skill, developed deliberately.

### Pratyāhāra in practice

Table: Practical doorways into pratyāhāra {#tab:pratyahara}

| Practice | How it works |
|---|---|
| Closing the eyes in relaxation and between postures | removes the most dominant sense from the outer world |
| Internal focus in āsana (breath, sensation) | turns attention from appearance to experience |
| *Ṣaṇmukhī mudrā* (closing ears, eyes, nose and mouth with the fingers) | reduces sensory input and turns attention to inner sound |
| *Bhrāmarī* (humming breath) | draws attention to an internal sound and vibration |
| Yoga nidrā and systematic relaxation | rotates awareness through the body while outer attention withdraws |
| *Trāṭaka* (candle gazing) followed by the inner image | shifts attention from an outer object to an inner one |

::: exercise Sensing without being pulled
Sit for five minutes with the eyes closed. For one minute each, notice sounds, then bodily sensations, then smells, then the taste in the mouth, then the play of light behind the eyelids — simply registering each without following it. For the last minute, let all the senses be present at once while attention rests on the breath. What changed between the first and last minute?
:::

## The three limbs in a classical class

The traditional structure of the class taught in this course follows the logic of these three limbs:

1. It begins with **relaxation** and **prāṇāyāma** — kapālabhāti and alternate-nostril breathing — to steady breath and mind before moving.
2. **Āsana** follows, with short relaxations between postures so that each is absorbed.
3. The class ends with a long **final relaxation**, a guided practice of pratyāhāra in which awareness withdraws from the body part by part.

In this sense a well-taught classical class is not an exercise class with breathing added; it is a practice of the outer limbs in the service of the inner ones.

::: takeaways
- In the Yoga Sūtra, āsana is primarily the steady, comfortable seat for meditation (2.46); Haṭha Yoga developed it into a large practice with benefits of its own.
- Posture is perfected by relaxing effort and absorbing the mind (2.47); sthira and sukha are criteria for any pose.
- Prāṇāyāma, for Patañjali, regulates inhalation, exhalation and suspension, observed by place, time and number, becoming long and subtle.
- Tradition and modern physiology agree that breath and mental state are closely linked; slow breathing has measurable autonomic effects.
- Pratyāhāra is freedom of attention from the pull of the senses, not their suppression, and can be taught through concrete practices.
:::
