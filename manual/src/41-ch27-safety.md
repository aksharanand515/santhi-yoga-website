::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- apply the principles of safe teaching, including screening, informed choice and gradual progression;
- explain load management and distinguish useful discomfort from pain that means stop;
- summarise what research shows about the frequency and pattern of yoga-related injuries;
- recognise common problems of the shoulders, wrists, knees, hips, hamstrings, lower back, neck and sacroiliac region, and modify practice appropriately;
- know when and how to refer a student to a healthcare professional, and recognise emergencies;
- teach safely in inversions, deep backbends, deep twists, extreme hip positions and weight-bearing on the hands, and for pregnant students, older adults, hypermobile students and those with previous injuries.
:::

## How safe is yoga?

Yoga is, on the whole, a safe form of exercise. A systematic review of randomised trials found that adverse events in yoga were no more common than with usual care or other exercise, and serious adverse events were rare [@cramer2015]. But “safe on average” is not “safe for everyone in every class”. Surveys and cohort studies of recreational practitioners find that a meaningful minority experience new musculoskeletal pain each year, and that practice sometimes aggravates existing problems [@campo2018; @penman2012]. Emergency-department data from the United States suggest that yoga-related injuries rose as participation grew, particularly among older adults [@swain2016]. Case reports describe rarer serious events, including neck injuries and vascular problems associated with extreme neck positions and inversions [@cramer2013ae].

Surveys of practitioners and teachers consistently name the same regions: the lower back, neck, shoulders, wrists, knees and hamstrings [@wiese2019; @fishman2009; @mikkonen2008]. Common threads in the reported mechanisms are **forcing** (by the student or by a teacher’s adjustment), **end-range loading** that the tissues are not prepared for, **repetition** without variation or rest, and **ignoring pain**.

::: keyconcept The teacher’s share of safety
You cannot guarantee that no student will ever be hurt; you can make injury much less likely by screening, offering options, progressing gradually, observing closely, never forcing, and creating a culture in which modifying and resting are respected rather than seen as failure.
:::

## Principles of safe teaching

1. **Know your students.** Use a brief health questionnaire for new students (Appendix I) and ask the class, discreetly, about injuries and conditions at the start. Invite private disclosure.
2. **Informed choice.** Explain options clearly and let students choose. Adults have the right to make decisions about their own practice; your job is to make those decisions well informed.
3. **Progress gradually.** New postures, longer holds, more repetitions and higher intensity are introduced step by step, over weeks, not in one class.
4. **Offer, don’t impose.** Offer variations to the whole room (“you might choose… or…”) rather than singling out individuals.
5. **Watch the breath and the face.** A held breath, a grimace or a flushed face mean the student is beyond their present capacity.
6. **Never force.** Neither students nor teachers should force a body into a shape. Hands-on assistance follows strict principles of consent and restraint (@ch:adjustments).
7. **Prepare the environment.** Adequate space between mats, non-slip surfaces, props available, a clear exit, ventilation and — in Kerala — shade, air movement and drinking water.
8. **Teach the exit.** For every challenging posture, teach how to come out safely before teaching how to go deeper.

## Load management

Tissues adapt to the loads they meet regularly, and become irritated when load increases faster than they can adapt (@fig:load) [@soligard2016; @cook2009]. For a teacher, managing load means thinking about **how much, how often, how intensely and how suddenly**:

- Increase only one variable at a time — duration *or* depth *or* repetitions.
- Be especially careful with **repetition**: fifty chaturaṅgas a week are a different load from five.
- Plan lighter sessions and rest; on an intensive course, the second week is when overuse problems typically appear.
- Return to practice gradually after a break, illness or injury.

## Pain and discomfort

Pain is a protective output of the nervous system, influenced by context and expectation, not a direct measure of damage [@raja2020; @butler2013]. Discomfort is a normal part of learning postures — stretching, muscular effort, fatigue, the unfamiliar. Pain that signals stop has different qualities.

!fig(pain)

::: note A clinical example: pain monitoring
In some rehabilitation programmes, physiotherapists allow patients to exercise with mild pain as long as it settles quickly and does not worsen the next morning — the “pain-monitoring model” used, for example, in tendon rehabilitation [@silbernagel2007]. This shows that some pain during activity is not necessarily harmful. It is a clinical tool used under professional supervision; in a group class, the more cautious traffic-light approach is appropriate.
:::

### When a student should stop

A student should stop the posture or practice immediately, and not resume it in that class, if they experience:

- sharp, stabbing, burning, electric or shooting pain;
- numbness, tingling or weakness spreading into the arms or legs;
- pain in a joint (rather than a stretch in a muscle), or a “pop” or giving way;
- dizziness, light-headedness, visual disturbance or severe headache;
- chest pain, pressure or palpitations, or unusual breathlessness;
- nausea, confusion or feeling faint;
- in pregnancy: bleeding, fluid leakage, contractions, calf pain or swelling, or any of the above [@acog2020].

::: safety Emergencies
Call for emergency medical help immediately if a student shows signs of a **heart attack** (chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back, breathlessness, sweating, nausea), a **stroke** (face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty — act fast), **loss of consciousness**, a **severe allergic reaction**, **heat stroke** (confusion, hot skin, collapse) or a **serious injury**. Know the local emergency number, where the nearest hospital is, and where the first-aid kit is kept. Every teacher should hold a current first-aid certificate.
:::

### Heat and hydration

Kerala is hot and humid for much of the year. Exercise in heat raises core temperature and fluid loss, and humidity reduces the cooling effect of sweating [@hall2021]. Schedule demanding practice in the cooler hours, ensure shade and air movement, encourage students to drink water before and after class, allow rests, and recognise **heat exhaustion** (heavy sweating, dizziness, headache, nausea, weakness): stop, move to a cooler place, lie down with legs raised, sip water, and seek help if symptoms do not improve quickly.

## What teachers can address — and when to refer

Table: Scope of the yoga teacher with regard to pain and injury {#tab:refer}

| Teachers can | Teachers should refer when |
|---|---|
| ask about injuries and conditions, and adapt practice | pain is new, severe, worsening, or not settling within a few days |
| offer modifications, props and alternative postures | pain wakes the student at night or is present at rest |
| reduce range, load, repetitions or duration | there are neurological symptoms: numbness, tingling, weakness, changes in coordination |
| encourage gradual return to practice after clearance | a joint is swollen, hot, locked or giving way |
| give general information about evidence on yoga and pain | there is unexplained weight loss, fever, or a history of cancer with new back pain |
| suggest seeing a doctor or physiotherapist | there are bladder or bowel changes, or numbness in the saddle area (a medical emergency) [@finucane2020] |
| work alongside a student’s healthcare professional | the student is pregnant with complications, or has a serious medical condition |

A yoga teacher does not diagnose. “I can’t tell what is causing that, but it would be worth having it checked” is always an acceptable sentence (@ch:teacher-role).

## Region by region

Table: Common problems by region, what teachers may notice, and how to respond {#tab:regions}

| Region | What you may notice | Practice patterns involved | What to do in class | Refer if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Shoulders** | pain at the front or side of the shoulder with arms overhead or in chaturaṅga; clicking with pain | repeated chaturaṅga with the shoulders dipping below the elbows; collapsing in plank and down dog; forced binds | knees down; lower halfway only; strengthen the shoulder girdle; reduce repetitions; avoid binds | pain at night, weakness lifting the arm, pain after a fall, symptoms persisting over two weeks |
| **Wrists** | pain on the back of the wrist in plank, down dog, crow; tingling in the fingers | sudden increase in time on the hands; sinking into the heel of the hand | spread the load (@fig:hand); fists, forearms or blocks; wedge under the heel of the hand; build gradually | numbness or tingling in the hand (possible nerve compression), swelling, pain after a fall |
| **Knees** | pain inside or behind the knee in lotus, hero, deep squats; clicking with pain; swelling | forcing lotus; twisting the knee when the hip is limited; hyperextension in standing poses | never force the knee; cushion between heels and buttocks; half lotus or easy pose; micro-bend in standing poses | swelling, locking, giving way, pain after a twisting incident |
| **Hips** | groin pain or pinching at the front of the hip in deep flexion; pain with pigeon or lotus | forcing external rotation or deep flexion into compression | reduce depth; change the angle; sit higher; respect bony limits [@griffin2016] | persistent groin pain, catching or locking, pain walking |
| **Hamstrings** | deep pain at the sitting bone, worse sitting and in forward bends | aggressive forward bending; pulling with the arms; long holds on a sensitised tendon | bend the knees; reduce depth; avoid pulling; strengthen (bridge, slow lowering) [@goom2016] | pain persisting for weeks, bruising after a sudden “pop” |
| **Lower back** | pain in forward bends or backbends; stiffness; pain after class | loaded flexion (standing folds with straight legs), deep backbends concentrated in the lumbar spine, twisting while slumped | bend the knees; lengthen before bending; smaller range; gentle movement usually helps more than rest [@qaseem2017] | red flags (see @tab:refer), leg symptoms, pain after trauma, severe or worsening pain |
| **Neck** | pain in shoulderstand, plough, headstand or fish; headache after inversions | weight on the head in headstand; neck pressed flat in shoulderstand; dropping the head back under load; turning the head while inverted | forearm strength first; blankets; supported fish; no head turning | pain or tingling into the arms, dizziness, visual symptoms, severe headache |
| **Sacroiliac region** | one-sided pain at the back of the pelvis, over the SI joint, in twists or asymmetric standing poses | forcing the pelvis “square” in asymmetric poses; deep twists with the pelvis fixed; hypermobility | let the pelvis move with the twist; shorter stances; symmetrical strengthening [@vleeming2012] | pain persisting, pain in pregnancy, pain after a fall |

## Special situations

### Inversions

Screen for neck problems, uncontrolled high blood pressure, heart disease, glaucoma and other serious eye conditions, recent head injury, dizziness and vertigo, and pregnancy in students not already practising inversions [@baskaran2006; @jasien2015]. Build strength first; use blankets for shoulderstand and plough; teach headstand at a wall with a controlled entry [@hector2015]; never allow turning of the head; keep holds short for beginners.

### Deep backbends

Build through cobra and locust before bow, camel and wheel; distribute extension along the spine; keep the neck in line; practise with fresh (not fatigued) muscles; counterpose gently; avoid for students with acute back pain aggravated by extension.

### Deep twists

Sit tall; turn from the rib cage; avoid levering with the arms; let the pelvis move; avoid combining deep rotation and flexion in students with osteoporosis or acute disc problems [@sinaki2013].

### Extreme hip mobility

Lotus, splits and deep hip openers are where bone shape most clearly sets limits. Never press on a student’s knees or pelvis; teach that the knee is protected by keeping the foot flexed and by sitting high; accept that some students will never do full lotus — and do not need to.

### Weight-bearing through the hands

Plank, down dog, crow and wheel load the wrists in extension (@fig:hand). Warm the wrists, distribute load through the whole hand, increase time on the hands gradually over weeks, and offer forearm, fist or block variations.

### Pregnancy

For women with uncomplicated pregnancies, physical activity including modified yoga is recommended by obstetric guidelines [@acog2020; @mottola2018]. A study of healthy women in late pregnancy found 26 yoga postures, including supine and some inverted positions, were well tolerated without changes in fetal heart rate [@polis2015]. General precautions [[C]]:

- Obtain the student’s confirmation that her pregnancy is uncomplicated and that she has discussed exercise with her midwife or doctor.
- Avoid overheating (no hot yoga), deep twists that compress the abdomen, lying on the front, strong abdominal work, breath retention and forceful breathing (kapālabhāti, bhastrikā), and deep backbends.
- After the first trimester, prolonged lying flat on the back may cause dizziness in some women; offer side-lying or an inclined position.
- Avoid postures with a risk of falling; use a wall for balances.
- Joint laxity increases in pregnancy: avoid pushing to end range.
- Stop and seek care for warning signs such as vaginal bleeding, fluid leakage, regular painful contractions, dizziness, chest pain, headache, calf pain or swelling [@acog2020].

Teaching pregnant students beyond these general principles requires specialist prenatal training.

### Older adults

Yoga-based exercise improves balance and mobility in adults over 60 [@youkhana2016], and exercise that challenges balance reduces falls [@sherrington2019]. But older students are more likely to have osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, high blood pressure and reduced balance. In spinal **osteoporosis**, repeated or loaded spinal flexion (e.g. forward bends, plough) has been associated with vertebral compression fractures [@sinaki2013]: emphasise upright postures, gentle extension, standing balance with support, and leg strength. Offer chairs and walls, allow extra time for transitions, and help students up and down from the floor safely.

### Hypermobility

Some students have joints that move well beyond the typical range; in a small number this is part of a hereditary connective-tissue disorder such as hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome [@malfait2017]. Hypermobile students often look “advanced” but may have poor end-range control, joint pain, and a tendency to hang into ligaments. Teach them to work **away from end range**: micro-bends in elbows and knees, strength and stability rather than further stretch, and awareness of joint position. Praise control, not depth.

### Previous injuries

After an injury or surgery, students should follow their clinician’s advice. In class: ask what they have been told they can and cannot do; begin below their previous level; increase load gradually; and pay special attention to the injured region and its neighbours. Pain that increases over successive classes is a reason to reduce load and refer.

::: tip The health conversation
At the start of every class with new students: “Is there anything I should know — an injury, pregnancy, a health condition? You can tell me now, or quietly after I finish speaking.” Then listen, adapt, and follow up after class.
:::

::: takeaways
- Yoga is generally safe, but injuries occur, most often in the lower back, neck, shoulders, wrists, knees and hamstrings, commonly through forcing, end-range loading, repetition and ignoring pain.
- Safe teaching rests on screening, informed choice, gradual progression, offering options, observation, a safe environment and teaching the exit.
- Load should increase gradually; discomfort is normal, but sharp, neurological, joint or systemic symptoms mean stop.
- Teachers adapt and refer; they do not diagnose. Know the red flags and emergency responses.
- Inversions, deep backbends and twists, extreme hip positions and weight-bearing on the hands need preparation and options; pregnancy, older age, hypermobility and previous injury each need specific adaptations.
:::
