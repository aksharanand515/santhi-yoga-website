## The word “yoga”

The Sanskrit word *yoga* comes from the verbal root *yuj*. Traditional Sanskrit grammar actually lists two roots of that form: *yujir yoge*, “to yoke, to join”, and *yuj samādhau*, “to concentrate, to be absorbed” [@bryant2009]. Both meanings are alive in the tradition. The first gives the image, familiar from the Upaniṣads, of harnessing the senses like horses to a chariot so that they pull in one direction. The second points to the meditative absorption (*samādhi*) that Patañjali places at the summit of practice. The popular translation “union” is not wrong, but it is only half of the story, and in classical Sāṃkhya-Yoga the goal is better described as *separation* — the discrimination of pure awareness from everything it observes (@ch:yoga-sutras).

In ordinary Sanskrit, *yoga* also means simply a method, a means, an application or a connection: astronomers speak of the *yoga* of two planets, physicians of the *yoga* of medicines. When the texts use the word for a spiritual discipline, the everyday sense of “a disciplined method” is never far away.

## Classical definitions

No single definition covers everything that has been called yoga over three thousand years. It is more useful to know several and to know where each comes from.

Table: Five classical definitions of yoga {#tab:definitions}

| Source | Definition | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| *Kaṭha Upaniṣad* 2.3.11 | “The firm holding of the senses” (*sthirām indriya-dhāraṇām*) | restraint of the senses; the oldest explicit definition |
| *Bhagavad Gītā* 2.48 | “Evenness of mind is called yoga” (*samatvaṃ yoga ucyate*) | equanimity in success and failure |
| *Bhagavad Gītā* 2.50 | “Yoga is skill in action” (*yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam*) | acting without attachment to results |
| *Bhagavad Gītā* 6.23 | “The severing of the connection with suffering is known as yoga” | freedom from suffering |
| *Yoga Sūtra* 1.2 | “Yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind” (*yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ*) | the mind itself; the basis of classical Yoga |

::: verse Yoga Sūtra 1.2
योगश्चित्तवृत्तिनिरोधः

*yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ*

Yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind.
:::

Notice what these definitions have in common. None of them mentions posture, flexibility or health. All of them describe a change in the relationship between a person and their own mind and senses. The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā*, the most influential text of physical yoga, agrees: it opens by presenting Haṭha Yoga as “a stairway” for those who wish to climb to Rāja Yoga, the yoga of meditation (HYP 1.1–2) [@akers2002]. Postures were, from the start of the Haṭha tradition, a means rather than an end.

::: keyconcept Yoga as means and as goal
The word *yoga* refers both to a set of **methods** (ethical discipline, posture, breath, concentration) and to the **state** those methods aim at (a still, clear mind; freedom). A good teacher keeps both in view: the methods are taught in detail, but always as steps towards something larger than the method.
:::

## The purpose of yoga

### The traditional answer

Every classical school of Indian philosophy begins from the fact of suffering (*duḥkha*) and asks how it can be ended. Patañjali puts it with characteristic economy: *heyaṃ duḥkham anāgatam* — “suffering that has not yet come is to be avoided” (YS 2.16). The traditional purpose of yoga is therefore liberation: *kaivalya*, the “aloneness” or independence of pure awareness in Patañjali’s system, or *mokṣa*, release, in Vedānta [[T]]. Health, calm and strength are welcomed along the way as supports for the journey, not as its destination. The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* says that success comes to the one who practises, “whether young, old, very old, sick or weak” (HYP 1.64) — a statement about perseverance, not a promise of physical results [@akers2002].

### Why students actually come

Surveys of modern practitioners consistently find that most people begin yoga for exercise, flexibility and stress relief; a spiritual motive often grows later, if at all [@penman2012]. There is nothing wrong with this. Swami Vishnudevananda deliberately began with the body, teaching that proper exercise, breathing, relaxation, diet and positive thinking form a practical path that anyone can follow (@ch:sivananda). But a teacher who knows only the modern purposes teaches a thinner practice, and a teacher who scorns them loses the students who most need to begin.

::: tip Meeting students where they are
A student who comes for a stiff back and leaves ten weeks later sleeping better, a little kinder to themselves and curious about the breath has been well taught. You do not need to lecture on *kaivalya* in a beginners’ class — but you should know why the relaxation at the end matters more than the headstand.
:::

## What yoga is — and is not

Yoga developed within the religious and philosophical cultures of South Asia; Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions all have yoga practices and yoga literature [[H]] [@samuel2008]. It is therefore neither true that yoga is “just exercise” nor that practising yoga requires adopting a particular religion. People of many faiths, and of none, practise it seriously. The Sivananda tradition, in which this school teaches, has always welcomed students of every background while chanting in Sanskrit and honouring its Hindu roots. As a teacher you should be able to do both: speak about the tradition knowledgeably and respectfully, and never pressure a student to believe anything.

::: myth “Yoga is five thousand years old.”
You will hear this claim in many classes. The evidence does not support it. The earliest texts that describe yoga as a practice date from the second half of the first millennium BCE, and interpretations of older Indus Valley seals as depicting yogis are speculative [[H]] [@samuel2008]. The traditions that became yoga are certainly ancient, and many practitioners regard the teachings as timeless — but that is a traditional claim [[T]], not a historical date. Chapter 2 sets out what the evidence does show.
:::

## Four kinds of knowledge

Throughout this manual you will see small labels in the text. They exist because four different kinds of statement are routinely mixed together in yoga teaching, and much confusion — and some harm — comes from mixing them.

1. **Historical evidence** [[H]] tells us when and where a text, practice or idea appeared. It comes from manuscripts, inscriptions, archaeology and the methods of historians and philologists. It can be revised when new manuscripts are found.
2. **Traditional teaching** [[T]] is what a living tradition teaches about practice and its effects, handed down through texts and teachers. It deserves to be transmitted accurately and respectfully. It is not made true or false by laboratory experiment; it is a different kind of knowledge, validated within the tradition by practice and experience.
3. **Interpretation** [[I]] is a reading of a text or a practice by a commentator or teacher. Commentaries on the Yoga Sūtras disagree with one another; so do modern teachers. Interpretation should be labelled as such.
4. **Scientific evidence** [[S]] comes from systematic observation and measurement — anatomy, physiology, clinical trials. It is always provisional and must be cited so others can check it.

::: keyconcept The rule of labels
When you teach, say which kind of statement you are making. “In the tradition, twists are said to massage the abdominal organs” is honest. “Twists detoxify your liver” is a scientific claim that has no evidence behind it and should not be made.
:::

## How to judge a research claim

Much of Section II discusses research. You do not need to be a scientist to use it well, but you do need a few principles.

Table: A simplified hierarchy of evidence {#tab:evidence}

| Level | Type of study | What it can tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest | Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomised trials | whether an effect is consistent across many trials |
| | Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) | whether an intervention causes an effect compared with a control |
| | Cohort and case–control studies | associations in real populations; cannot prove cause |
| | Case reports and case series | that something *can* happen (useful for rare harms) |
| Weakest | Expert opinion, mechanism, tradition | ideas and hypotheses to be tested |

Keep five cautions in mind:

- **Small studies are fragile.** Many yoga trials have a few dozen participants; their results often shrink or vanish in larger studies.
- **“Yoga” is not one thing.** A trial of twelve weeks of gentle Hatha is not evidence about hot power yoga, and vice versa.
- **Control groups matter.** Yoga compared with doing nothing will almost always look good. Yoga compared with other exercise is a fairer test.
- **Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.** That no one has tested a traditional claim does not make it false; it makes it untested.
- **Mechanism is not outcome.** A plausible mechanism (“the pose stretches the psoas, which attaches to the lumbar spine…”) does not show that the pose relieves back pain. Only outcome studies can show that.

::: evidence Where yoga research is strongest
The best-studied use of yoga is chronic non-specific low back pain, where systematic reviews find small-to-moderate improvements in pain and function compared with no exercise, and results broadly similar to other exercise [@wieland2022; @cramer2013lbp]. Clinical guidelines include yoga among the recommended non-drug options for chronic low back pain [@qaseem2017]. Evidence for anxiety, depression and balance in older adults is encouraging but more mixed and generally of lower quality [@cramer2018; @cramer2013dep; @youkhana2016]. Evidence for many specific claims made about individual postures is absent.
:::

## Yoga in Kerala

You are studying in a region with a remarkable concentration of traditions related to yoga. The great Advaita Vedānta teacher Ādi Śaṅkara is traditionally held to have been born at Kaladi, about forty kilometres north-east of Kochi [[T]]; Kerala is home to *kaḷarippayaṯṯŭ*, the martial art whose training shares with Haṭha Yoga an emphasis on breath, flexibility and the body’s vital points; and Āyurveda has been continuously practised here for centuries. Swami Vishnudevananda, who carried the Sivananda teachings to the West, was born in Kerala in 1927 and in 1978 inaugurated the Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Dhanwantari Ashram at Neyyar Dam, near Thiruvananthapuram [@sivanandaorg2019]. Studying here is an opportunity to see yoga as part of a living culture rather than as an exported product.

## How this course works

The course follows an old pattern: **practise, study, teach**. Each morning you practise; you then study what you practised; and in the afternoons you begin to teach it. The *Yoga Sūtra* names three elements of “the yoga of action” (*kriyā yoga*) — *tapas*, disciplined effort; *svādhyāya*, study, including study of oneself; and *īśvara-praṇidhāna*, surrender to something greater than oneself (YS 2.1). A teacher-training course is an intense exercise in all three.

::: exercise Your working definition
On the first evening, write a definition of yoga in no more than twenty-five words. Seal it in an envelope. On the last day of the course, write a new definition before you open the envelope, then compare the two. Most students find that their second definition is shorter.
:::

::: takeaways
- *Yoga* comes from the root *yuj*, meaning both “to yoke” and “to be absorbed”; it names both a set of methods and the state they aim at.
- The classical definitions — Kaṭha Upaniṣad, Bhagavad Gītā, Yoga Sūtra — describe a transformation of mind and senses, not physical skill.
- The traditional purpose of yoga is freedom from suffering; modern students usually arrive for health and calm, and both motives deserve respect.
- Distinguish historical evidence, traditional teaching, interpretation and scientific evidence — and say which you are offering.
- Judge research claims by study type, size, control group and whether the “yoga” studied resembles what you teach.
:::
