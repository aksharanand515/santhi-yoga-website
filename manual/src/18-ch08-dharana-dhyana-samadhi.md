::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- define dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi according to Yoga Sūtra 3.1–3.3 and explain how they differ;
- explain *saṃyama* and why Patañjali calls these three the “inner limbs”;
- distinguish the main kinds of samādhi described in the Yoga Sūtra;
- describe what a beginner can realistically expect from concentration practice;
- speak about samādhi to students honestly and without exaggeration.
:::

## From outer to inner

The first five limbs prepare the ground. The last three — concentration, meditation and absorption — are the practice of yoga in the strict sense defined in YS 1.2: the stilling of the mind. Patañjali calls them “inner” (*antaraṅga*) relative to the first five (3.7), and yet “outer” relative to the seedless samādhi that is the final goal (3.8). Everything is relative to the next step.

## Dhāraṇā: concentration

> *deśa-bandhaś cittasya dhāraṇā* — “Concentration is the binding of the mind to a place” (YS 3.1).

The “place” may be a point in the body (the navel, the heart, the space between the eyebrows, the tip of the nose), an external object (a candle flame, an image), a sound (a mantra) or an idea. Vyāsa gives examples of bodily places: the navel, the lotus of the heart, the light in the head, the tip of the nose, the tongue [@bryant2009].

In dhāraṇā the mind is placed on the object and keeps wandering away; it is brought back again and again. This is the stage at which almost all practitioners spend most of their time — and it is not a failure. Each return is the practice (@fig:attention).

## Dhyāna: meditation

> *tatra pratyayaikatānatā dhyānam* — “Meditation is the continuous flow of the same awareness towards that object” (YS 3.2).

When concentration becomes steady, the effort of returning falls away. Attention flows towards the object without interruption, like oil poured from one vessel to another — a traditional image. The meditator is still aware of meditating: there is a subject, an object and the act of attending.

## Samādhi: absorption

> *tad evārtha-mātra-nirbhāsaṃ svarūpa-śūnyam iva samādhiḥ* — “Absorption is when the object alone shines forth, as if empty of its own form” (YS 3.3).

In samādhi the sense of a separate meditator falls away. Only the object remains in awareness. The phrase “as if empty of its own form” refers to the mind: it becomes so transparent to its object that it is as if it were not there.

## Saṃyama

Practised together on one object, the three are called ***saṃyama*** (3.4). From its mastery comes the light of insight (*prajñā*) (3.5), which is applied in stages (3.6). The third chapter of the Sūtra describes many applications of saṃyama and the powers traditionally said to result [[T]]; as we saw, Patañjali also warns that they are obstacles to the final goal (3.37).

| | Dhāraṇā | Dhyāna | Samādhi |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | binding the mind to one place (3.1) | continuous flow of awareness to it (3.2) | the object alone shines, mind as if empty (3.3) |
| Effort | frequent, deliberate return | effortless continuity | effort transcended |
| Sense of meditator | strong | present | absent |
| Everyday analogy | repeatedly bringing a puppy back to its bed | the puppy lying quietly | — (no ordinary analogy is adequate) |

## The kinds of samādhi

Patañjali distinguishes two broad kinds [@bryant2009]:

- ***Samprajñāta*** (“with cognition”), also called *sabīja*, “with seed”: absorption in an object. It is accompanied, in progressively subtler stages, by gross thought (*vitarka*), subtle reflection (*vicāra*), bliss (*ānanda*) and the pure sense of “I-am” (*asmitā*) (1.17).
- ***Asamprajñāta*** (“beyond cognition”), also *nirbīja*, “seedless”: the stilling of even the last impressions, in which only latent impressions remain and then dissolve (1.18, 1.51). This leads to *kaivalya*.

Later Vedānta speaks of *savikalpa* samādhi (with distinctions) and *nirvikalpa* samādhi (without distinctions); these terms are often used interchangeably with Patañjali’s, though the two systems do not map onto each other exactly [[I]].

::: keyconcept Samādhi is not a special feeling
It is easy to confuse samādhi with pleasant or unusual experiences in meditation — lights, bliss, a sense of floating. Patañjali’s own list of obstacles includes *bhrānti-darśana*, false perception (1.30). Traditional teachers consistently advise students not to seek or cling to experiences, but to continue practising. As a teacher, receive students’ reports of experiences calmly and without inflating them.
:::

## What a beginner can expect

Honesty is the first duty of a meditation teacher. In the first months, a student can expect:

- a great deal of mind-wandering, which is normal and is the material of practice;
- moments — seconds, then longer — of steady attention;
- growing awareness of how busy the mind is (which may feel like the practice is making things worse, but is usually the opposite);
- improved ability to notice and step back from thoughts and emotions in daily life;
- occasional drowsiness, restlessness, boredom or strong emotion.

::: evidence What research says about beginners
Meta-analyses of structured meditation programmes find moderate benefits for anxiety, depression and pain compared with no treatment, and effects generally similar to other active treatments such as exercise [[S]] [@goyal2014]. Researchers caution that the field has often overstated its findings, and that terms such as “mindfulness” and “meditation” cover very different practices [@vandam2018]. Meditation can also occasionally bring difficult experiences — anxiety, re-experiencing of trauma, disturbing perceptual changes — which teachers should know how to meet (@ch:meditation-practice) [@britton2021].
:::

::: tip Teaching the inner limbs in a posture class
You cannot teach samādhi; you can teach dhāraṇā, and create the conditions in which the rest may happen. In a posture class, a single point of focus held through a pose (the breath at the nostrils, the soles of the feet, a drishti) is dhāraṇā. Long holds with a quiet room invite dhyāna. Final relaxation is where many students first glimpse deep absorption. Guard the silence.
:::

::: takeaways
- Dhāraṇā binds the mind to one place; dhyāna is its uninterrupted flow; in samādhi the object alone shines and the sense of a separate meditator falls away.
- Together, practised on one object, they are called saṃyama.
- Samādhi with seed (samprajñāta) has stages of vitarka, vicāra, ānanda and asmitā; seedless samādhi (asamprajñāta) leads to liberation.
- Unusual experiences in meditation are not samādhi and should not be sought or inflated.
- Beginners spend most of their time in dhāraṇā; teachers should set honest expectations and know that meditation can occasionally be difficult.
:::

1. Practise ten minutes of dhāraṇā on the breath at the nostrils each morning for a week, noting in your journal roughly how many times you returned.
2. Teach a partner a five-minute concentration practice on a candle flame (trāṭaka), including setting up, timing, closing and the after-image (see @ch:meditation-practice for safety).
3. Role-play: a student reports seeing “blue light” in meditation and asks if it means they are awakening. Respond in under one minute.
