::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- describe anatomical position and use directional terms correctly, including in inverted postures;
- name the three cardinal planes and their axes and identify the plane of common yoga movements;
- classify joints by structure and describe the movements available at each type of synovial joint;
- name and locate the skeletal landmarks most useful for teaching;
- describe the main connective tissues and how each responds to load.
:::

## Why a shared language matters

“Lift your hips” means different things in cobra, shoulderstand and downward dog. Anatomical language exists to remove that ambiguity. A teacher who uses it well — mostly in training, observation and conversation with healthcare professionals rather than in class cues — can describe precisely what a student is doing, communicate with physiotherapists and doctors, and read the anatomy literature. In class you will translate it back into plain words.

## Anatomical position

All anatomical description refers to one reference posture, **anatomical position**: standing upright, facing forward, feet together and pointing forward, arms by the sides with the palms facing forward [@moore2018]. Every directional term keeps its meaning relative to this position, whatever position the body is actually in. In headstand, the head is still **superior** to the feet; in forward bend, the chest is still **anterior** to the spine.

!fig(directions)

Table: Directional terms {#tab:directions}

| Term | Meaning | Example in yoga |
|---|---|---|
| Superior / inferior | towards the head / towards the feet | “draw the shoulder blades inferiorly” = down the back |
| Anterior (ventral) / posterior (dorsal) | towards the front / back | the hamstrings are posterior thigh muscles |
| Medial / lateral | towards / away from the midline | the big toe is on the medial side of the foot |
| Proximal / distal | closer to / further from the trunk (on a limb) | the knee is proximal to the ankle |
| Superficial / deep | nearer to / further from the surface | transversus abdominis is deep to the obliques |
| Ipsilateral / contralateral | same side / opposite side | in triangle to the right, the right hand reaches down on the ipsilateral side |
| Cranial / caudal | towards head / tail (used for the trunk) | the coccyx is the most caudal part of the spine |
| Palmar / dorsal (hand); plantar / dorsal (foot) | palm and sole / back of hand and top of foot | in cobra the dorsal surface of the feet rests on the floor |

## Planes and axes

Movement is described in three cardinal **planes**, each perpendicular to the others, with an **axis** of rotation perpendicular to each plane [@neumann2017]:

!fig(planes)

Table: Planes, axes and yoga movements {#tab:planes}

| Plane | Axis | Typical movements | Yoga examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sagittal** (divides left and right) | mediolateral (side to side) | flexion, extension | forward bends, backbends, lunges, sun salutation |
| **Frontal / coronal** (divides front and back) | anteroposterior (front to back) | abduction, adduction, lateral flexion | triangle, side bends, star, arms rising sideways |
| **Transverse / horizontal** (divides upper and lower) | longitudinal (vertical) | rotation | twists, hip rotation in lotus and pigeon |

Real postures combine planes. Triangle is mainly a frontal-plane posture, yet the front hip also flexes (sagittal) and the trunk often rotates (transverse). Naming the planes helps you see what each part of the body is doing.

## Joints

A joint (articulation) is where two or more bones meet. Joints are classified by structure [@moore2018]:

- **Fibrous joints** — bones joined by dense connective tissue with little or no movement (e.g. the sutures of the skull).
- **Cartilaginous joints** — bones joined by cartilage, allowing limited movement (e.g. the intervertebral discs, the pubic symphysis).
- **Synovial joints** — bones separated by a joint cavity with lubricating synovial fluid, enclosed in a capsule and reinforced by ligaments. These are the freely movable joints of yoga practice.

Table: Types of synovial joint {#tab:joint-types}

| Type | Movement | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ball-and-socket | movement in all three planes | hip, shoulder (glenohumeral) |
| Hinge | mainly one plane (flexion/extension) | elbow (humeroulnar), knee (with slight rotation), ankle (talocrural) |
| Pivot | rotation about a long axis | atlanto-axial joint (turning the head), proximal radioulnar joint |
| Condyloid (ellipsoid) | two planes (flexion/extension, abduction/adduction) | wrist (radiocarpal), knuckles (metacarpophalangeal) |
| Saddle | two planes plus some rotation | base of the thumb (carpometacarpal) |
| Plane (gliding) | small sliding movements | facet joints of the spine, between carpal bones, acromioclavicular joint |

## Joint movements

!fig(jointmoves)

Table: Movement terms used in this manual {#tab:movements}

| Movement | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Flexion | decreasing the angle between segments (in the sagittal plane) | folding at the hips in paścimottānāsana |
| Extension | increasing the angle; returning from flexion | straightening the knees; backbending the spine |
| Hyperextension | extension beyond the neutral or anatomical position | the lumbar spine in bow; “locked” knees or elbows |
| Abduction / adduction | moving away from / towards the midline | legs apart in triangle / legs together in tāḍāsana |
| Internal (medial) / external (lateral) rotation | rotating a limb inward / outward about its long axis | the front thigh externally rotates in warrior II |
| Circumduction | circular movement combining the above | arm circles |
| Lateral flexion | side-bending of the spine | the trunk in triangle |
| Rotation (spine) | turning of the trunk about its long axis | half spinal twist |
| Pronation / supination | turning the palm down / up (forearm rotation) | palms down in plank / palms up in savāsana |
| Dorsiflexion / plantarflexion | lifting the top of the foot / pointing the toes | heels towards floor in downward dog / pointed feet in headstand |
| Inversion / eversion | turning the sole inward / outward | balancing on the outer foot |
| Elevation / depression (scapula) | lifting / lowering the shoulder blade | shrugging / drawing shoulders down |
| Protraction / retraction (scapula) | sliding forward around the ribs / drawing back | pushing the floor away in plank / squeezing the shoulder blades |
| Upward / downward rotation (scapula) | turning the glenoid cavity up / down | arms overhead / hands behind the back |
| Nutation / counternutation (sacrum) | small nodding of the sacrum forward / backward relative to the ilia | occurs in forward and backward bending |

::: tip Translating anatomy into cues
“Flex your hips” becomes “fold from the hip creases”. “Depress and retract the scapulae” becomes “draw the shoulder blades gently down and back”. Use anatomical language to *think* and plain language to *teach*. Many students also respond better to external images (“press the floor away”) than to internal instructions (“contract your triceps”) (@ch:nervous-system).
:::

## Landmarks you can feel

These bony landmarks can be felt through the skin and are the teacher’s reference points for observing alignment. Learn to find each on yourself and, with consent, on a partner.

Table: Palpable landmarks for teachers {#tab:landmarks}

| Landmark | Where | Why it matters in teaching |
|---|---|---|
| C7 spinous process | most prominent bump at the base of the neck | reference point for neck position in shoulderstand; “lengthen the back of the neck” |
| Spine and acromion of the scapula | ridge across the shoulder blade; tip of the shoulder | observing shoulder position in plank, headstand |
| Inferior angle of the scapula | lower point of the shoulder blade | scapular upward rotation in arm raises |
| Sternum and xiphoid process | breastbone and its lower tip | “lift the sternum” in backbends; hand position in CPR |
| Iliac crest | top rim of the pelvis at the waist | pelvic level in standing poses |
| ASIS (anterior superior iliac spine) | front “hip points” | pelvic orientation in lunges, triangle, forward bends |
| PSIS (posterior superior iliac spine) | “dimples” at the low back | sacroiliac region; pelvic tilt |
| Ischial tuberosities | “sitting bones” | seated postures; origin of the hamstrings |
| Greater trochanter | outer bony prominence of the upper thigh | hip rotation; lying on the side |
| Patella and tibial tuberosity | kneecap and bump below it | knee tracking over the foot |
| Medial and lateral malleoli | ankle bones | foot alignment; lotus safety |
| Olecranon | point of the elbow | elbow position in crow, chaturaṅga, headstand |

## The body’s connective tissues

Yoga loads several kinds of tissue, which adapt differently [@neumann2017]:

- **Bone** — a living, remodelling tissue that strengthens in response to regular loading and weakens with disuse. Relevant to older students and osteoporosis (@ch:safety).
- **Articular (hyaline) cartilage** — the smooth covering of joint surfaces; has no blood supply and is nourished by movement and loading of the joint.
- **Fibrocartilage** — tough cartilage of the menisci, intervertebral discs and pubic symphysis.
- **Ligaments** — dense connective tissue joining bone to bone; they limit movement and contribute to joint position sense. They are not designed to be stretched: a chronically overstretched ligament does not reliably return to its original length.
- **Tendons** — join muscle to bone; they adapt to load over weeks to months — more slowly than muscle.
- **Muscle** — contractile tissue that adapts rapidly in strength and in tolerance to stretch.
- **Fascia** — sheets and wrappings of connective tissue around and within muscles and organs. Fascia is an active research area; many popular claims about “fascial release” and “storing emotions in fascia” go well beyond the evidence [[S]].

::: keyconcept Stretch muscles, respect ligaments
In a well-taught posture the sensation of stretch is felt in the *belly* of a muscle, not at a joint. Sensation at the back of the knee, in the front of the shoulder joint, at the sacroiliac joints or at the sitting bones is a signal to back off and modify, not to go deeper.
:::

::: takeaways
- All anatomical terms refer to anatomical position, even when the body is inverted.
- The three planes — sagittal, frontal, transverse — and their axes describe flexion/extension, abduction/adduction/lateral flexion and rotation.
- Synovial joints are classified as ball-and-socket, hinge, pivot, condyloid, saddle and plane; each type allows characteristic movements.
- Palpable landmarks (C7, ASIS, PSIS, ischial tuberosities, greater trochanter, malleoli and others) are the teacher’s reference points.
- Bone, cartilage, ligament, tendon, muscle and fascia each respond differently to load; ligaments should not be deliberately stretched.
:::
