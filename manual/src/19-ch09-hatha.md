## Origins

Haṭha Yoga — the yoga that uses the body, breath and vital energy as its primary means — took shape between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries CE [[H]] [@mallinson2017]. Its earliest surviving text, the *Amṛtasiddhi* (eleventh century), teaches techniques for controlling the vital essence (*bindu*) and breath and comes from a Buddhist tantric environment. The *Dattātreyayogaśāstra* (thirteenth century) is the first text to call its methods *haṭha yoga*; the Nāth yogis, a Śaiva order tracing its lineage to Matsyendra and Gorakṣa, became the tradition most closely associated with it.

The word *haṭha* means “force” or “obstinacy” — the forceful, effortful nature of the methods [@birch2011]. Later texts gave it an esoteric etymology: *ha* stands for the sun and *ṭha* for the moon, and Haṭha Yoga is the union of these two currents — the right and left channels, piṅgalā and iḍā — in the central channel, suṣumṇā [[T]]. The etymology is later than the name, but it expresses the tradition’s own understanding of its aim.

### The logic of Haṭha practice

The traditional rationale runs roughly as follows [[T]]: mind and breath are bound together; vital energy (*prāṇa*) ordinarily flows through the left and right channels; when prāṇa is made to enter the central channel through posture, breath retention, locks and seals, the mind becomes still, the dormant power (*kuṇḍalinī*) rises, and the practitioner attains Rāja Yoga — the state of samādhi. The *Pradīpikā* insists that Haṭha and Rāja Yoga need each other: “Without Haṭha, Rāja Yoga cannot be attained; without Rāja Yoga, Haṭha is fruitless” (2.76) [@akers2002].

## The Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā

The ***Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā*** (“Light on Haṭha Yoga”) was compiled by **Svātmārāma** in the fifteenth century, drawing on at least twenty earlier texts [[H]] [@mallinson2017]. It became the best-known Haṭha text and is the foundation of the Haṭha practice taught in this course. It has four chapters (*upadeśa*s). Verse numbering differs slightly between editions; this manual follows @akers2002; the Bihar School edition with extensive commentary is @muktibodhananda1998.

### Chapter 1: preparation and āsana

The first chapter describes the ideal practitioner and place of practice — a small hut in a peaceful, well-governed land, free from stones, fire and water, with a small door (1.12–13) — and the conditions for success and failure:

::: trad Six things that destroy yoga, and six that bring success · HYP 1.15–16
**Yoga is destroyed by:** overeating, over-exertion, too much talking, adhering to rules rigidly, too much company, and unsteadiness (fickleness).

**Yoga succeeds through:** enthusiasm, courage, patience, knowledge of the truth, determination, and giving up excessive company.
:::

Āsana is presented as the first limb, bringing “steadiness, health and lightness” (1.17). Fifteen postures are described: *svastikāsana*, *gomukhāsana*, *vīrāsana*, *kūrmāsana*, *kukkuṭāsana*, *uttāna-kūrmāsana*, *dhanurāsana*, *matsyendrāsana*, *paścimottānāsana*, *mayūrāsana*, *śavāsana*, and the four principal seated postures *siddhāsana*, *padmāsana*, *siṃhāsana* and *bhadrāsana* [@akers2002]. Siddhāsana is praised as the foremost (1.35–43). Several of the twelve basic postures appear here in early form: *dhanurāsana* (bow), *matsyendrāsana* (the ancestor of the half spinal twist), *paścimottānāsana* (forward bend) and *śavāsana*. The chapter closes with guidance on moderate diet (*mitāhāra*), recommending pleasant, nourishing food eaten while leaving a quarter of the stomach empty [[T]].

### Chapter 2: prāṇāyāma and cleansing

The second chapter begins with the link between breath and mind (2.2) and the importance of purifying the channels through alternate-nostril breathing. It warns that correctly practised prāṇāyāma removes disease while incorrect practice creates it (2.16–17), and that the breath must be tamed gradually, as a lion or elephant is tamed (2.15).

For those with excess fat or phlegm, six cleansing practices are recommended (2.21–22) [[T]]:

Table: The six cleansing practices (ṣaṭkarma), HYP 2.22 {#tab:shatkarma}

| Kriyā | Traditional description | Taught in a general class? |
|---|---|---|
| *Dhauti* | swallowing and withdrawing a long strip of cloth to clean the stomach | **No** — specialist practice under direct supervision |
| *Basti* | drawing water into the colon by abdominal control | **No** |
| *Neti* | cleansing the nasal passages with water (*jala neti*) or a thread (*sūtra neti*) | jala neti may be taught with hygiene precautions (sterile or boiled water) |
| *Trāṭaka* | gazing steadily at a small object until the eyes water | yes, with eye-health precautions |
| *Nauli* | isolating and rotating the rectus abdominis muscles | only to experienced students on an empty stomach; many contraindications |
| *Kapālabhāti* | rapid, forceful exhalations like the bellows of a blacksmith | yes, with screening and precautions (@ch:pranayama) |

The chapter then describes eight **kumbhakas** — breath retentions, each with its own breathing pattern (2.44): *sūryabhedana*, *ujjāyī*, *sītkārī*, *śītalī*, *bhastrikā*, *bhrāmarī*, *mūrcchā* and *plāvinī*. Several of these are now practised as breathing techniques without long retention; @ch:pranayama teaches those suitable for general classes.

::: safety Not everything in a classical text belongs in a modern class
The Haṭha texts were written for renunciate practitioners working one-to-one with a teacher over years. Some practices they describe (swallowing cloth, internal washes, very long breath retention, the more esoteric mudrās) carry real risks and are **not** taught in general classes or on this course except as theory. Respecting a tradition includes respecting its insistence on direct, individual guidance for advanced practices.
:::

### Chapter 3: mudrās and bandhas

The third chapter teaches ten **mudrās** — “seals” — which are advanced techniques combining posture, breath retention and locks to direct prāṇa into the central channel and awaken kuṇḍalinī (3.6–7): *mahāmudrā*, *mahābandha*, *mahāvedha*, *khecarī*, *uḍḍīyāna bandha*, *mūla bandha*, *jālandhara bandha*, *viparītakaraṇī*, *vajrolī* and *śakticālana* [@akers2002]. The three locks and *viparītakaraṇī* — the inverted posture that is the textual ancestor of shoulderstand — are taught in this course; *khecarī* (turning the tongue back into the nasal cavity after gradually cutting its frenulum) and *vajrolī* (a urethral practice) are described historically only [[H]]. @ch:bandhas-mudras-mantra covers the practices taught.

### Chapter 4: samādhi

The final chapter describes the goal: samādhi, in which prāṇa dissolves in the central channel and the mind in its source. It gives many synonyms for this state — Rāja Yoga, *unmanī*, *amanaska*, *laya* and others — and teaches meditation on the inner sound (*nāda*), heard first as loud sounds like the ocean, drums and thunder, then as subtle sounds like bells, flutes and bees (4.84–86) [[T]]. It ends with the image of the yogi freed from all states, “like a dead man”, unaffected by the world — in the sense of utterly still, not lifeless.

## Haṭha Yoga today

The Haṭha tradition reaches a modern yoga class mostly through three routes: its postures, its breathing practices, and its attitude — that the body is not an obstacle to the spiritual life but its instrument. The classical class taught in this course preserves the Haṭha order of practice (āsana, prāṇāyāma, relaxation, meditation) and its emphasis on the breath, while teaching a set of postures suited to householders. It is worth remembering the *Pradīpikā*’s own balance: success comes not from wearing the robes or talking about yoga, but from practice (1.66) [[T]].

::: takeaways
- Haṭha Yoga developed between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries; *haṭha* means “force”; the sun–moon etymology is later and symbolic.
- Its rationale is to lead prāṇa into the central channel through posture, breath, locks and seals, as a stairway to Rāja Yoga.
- The Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā (15th century) has four chapters: āsana; prāṇāyāma and cleansing; mudrās and bandhas; samādhi and nāda.
- It lists six things that destroy and six that support yoga, fifteen āsanas, six ṣaṭkarmas, eight kumbhakas and ten mudrās.
- Many classical Haṭha practices are unsuitable for general classes; respecting the tradition means respecting its insistence on individual guidance.
:::
