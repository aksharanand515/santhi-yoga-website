## From guru to teacher

In the Indian traditions, spiritual knowledge was transmitted through the relationship between **guru** and **śiṣya** (disciple), often living together over years. A traditional (and popular) etymology explains *guru* as *gu*, darkness, and *ru*, that which removes it — the dispeller of darkness [[T]]. The chain of teachers through which a practice is handed down is called ***paramparā***, and much of the authority of a tradition rests on its unbroken lineage.

A modern yoga teacher, even one teaching in a traditional lineage, is usually not a guru in this sense. You will teach students for an hour, a week, perhaps a few years. They will pay you, give feedback, and leave if they are not served. This is not a loss; it is a different and honest relationship. The modern teacher’s authority rests on **competence, integrity and care** — and on transparency about what they know and do not know.

::: note A necessary caution
The history of modern yoga includes well-documented cases in which teachers used the authority of the guru role to exploit students sexually, financially and psychologically [@remski2019]. These cases have taught the yoga community that devotion to a teaching must never become unconditional obedience to a person. A good teacher welcomes questions, respects students’ autonomy and submits to ethical standards and accountability like any other professional (@ch:ethics).
:::

## What a teacher owes

### To students

1. **Safety.** Teach practices appropriate to the students in front of you; screen for relevant health conditions; offer modifications; know when to refer (@ch:safety).
2. **Honesty.** Represent your training and experience truthfully; distinguish tradition from evidence; make no health promises.
3. **Respect.** Treat every student with dignity regardless of body, ability, age, gender, ethnicity, religion or background; respect their right to decline any practice or touch.
4. **Competence.** Know what you teach, and teach within what you know.
5. **Boundaries.** Keep the relationship professional; never exploit the trust or power the role carries.
6. **Confidentiality.** Keep students’ health information and personal disclosures private.
7. **Presence.** Give your full attention during class — not to your phone, your own practice or your image.

### To the tradition

- Transmit teachings accurately, with sources, and name the lineage you learned from.
- Pronounce Sanskrit carefully, and use sacred sounds and symbols with respect.
- Neither exaggerate the tradition’s claims nor dismiss them.
- Acknowledge your teachers.

### To yourself

- Keep your own practice alive. A teacher who has stopped practising teaches from memory, not experience.
- Keep learning (@ch:career).
- Look after your body, voice and energy; teaching many classes a week is physically demanding.
- Seek supervision or mentoring when facing difficult situations.

## Adhikāra: meeting students where they are

The traditions teach that a practice should be given according to the student’s ***adhikāra*** — their fitness, readiness or eligibility to receive it [[T]]. The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* insists that advanced techniques be learned from a teacher; the Upaniṣads show teachers testing students before instructing them. In modern terms, adhikāra is the principle behind **progression and individualisation**: a beginner is not taught advanced prāṇāyāma; a student with glaucoma is not taught headstand; a student in acute grief may need relaxation rather than a vigorous practice.

## Scope of practice

A 200-hour yoga teacher is trained to teach yoga to generally healthy people in group and private settings. A yoga teacher is **not** a physician, physiotherapist, psychotherapist or dietitian, and should not diagnose, treat or advise on medical or psychological conditions.

Table: Within and outside a yoga teacher’s scope {#tab:scope}

| Within scope | Outside scope |
|---|---|
| teaching yoga practices and their traditional context | diagnosing any condition (“that sounds like a disc problem”) |
| screening for health conditions relevant to practice | prescribing exercises to treat a diagnosed condition, unless specifically trained and working with the student’s clinician |
| offering general modifications for common limitations | advising a student to reduce or stop medication |
| sharing general, evidence-based information about the benefits and risks of yoga | offering psychotherapy or trauma processing |
| recognising warning signs and referring to a healthcare professional | giving dietary prescriptions for medical conditions |

::: keyconcept “Refer, don’t diagnose”
When a student describes symptoms, your job is not to name them but to recognise whether they call for a professional opinion, adapt the practice meanwhile, and encourage the student to seek that opinion. “I can’t tell you what’s causing that, but it would be worth having it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist. Until then, let’s leave out deep forward bends.”
:::

## The teacher as student

The Sivananda tradition speaks of the teacher as an instrument through which the teaching flows. Practically, this means humility: every class teaches the teacher something. The invocation that opens this manual asks for protection and nourishment for teacher *and* student together, and that they work together with energy. That is the relationship this course hopes to form in you.

::: takeaways
- The traditional guru–śiṣya relationship differs from the modern teacher’s role; the modern teacher’s authority rests on competence, integrity and care.
- History shows that unquestioning obedience to teachers enables abuse; accountability and students’ autonomy are essential.
- Teachers owe students safety, honesty, respect, competence, boundaries, confidentiality and presence.
- *Adhikāra* — teaching according to readiness — is the traditional basis of progression and individualisation.
- A yoga teacher does not diagnose or treat; recognise, adapt and refer.
:::
