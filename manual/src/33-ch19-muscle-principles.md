## What a muscle is

A skeletal muscle is an organ made of thousands of long cells, the **muscle fibres**, bundled into fascicles and wrapped at every level in connective tissue that merges at each end into the tendon. Inside each fibre, repeating units called **sarcomeres** contain overlapping filaments of the proteins actin and myosin. When the nervous system signals a fibre to contract, myosin heads bind to actin and pull the filaments past each other — the **sliding-filament** mechanism — shortening the sarcomere and generating force [@hall2021].

A single motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it controls form a **motor unit**. The nervous system grades force by recruiting more motor units and by firing them faster. A muscle does not “switch on” all at once; fine control in balances and slow transitions depends on this grading.

Muscles can only **pull**. They produce movement by pulling on bones across joints, and they are arranged in opposing groups so that every movement can be reversed.

## Types of contraction

!fig(contraction)

- **Concentric** contraction: the muscle shortens while producing force — lifting into locust (back extensors), rising from a squat (quadriceps and gluteus maximus).
- **Isometric** contraction: the muscle produces force without changing length — holding the locust, holding plank, holding warrior II. Much of a classical class is isometric work.
- **Eccentric** contraction: the muscle lengthens while producing force, controlling a movement against gravity — lowering slowly from locust, lowering the legs in double leg raises, descending from plank to the floor.

Muscles can produce more force eccentrically than concentrically, and eccentric work is particularly effective for building strength and tendon capacity — but it also produces more post-exercise soreness in the unaccustomed [@hall2021]. Slow, controlled lowering is one of the most valuable and least used tools in a posture class.

::: tip Gravity decides which muscle works
To know which muscle is working, ask which way gravity is pulling. In lowering the legs from double leg raise, the hip flexors work *eccentrically* against gravity — the hip extensors are not what lowers the legs. In a standing forward bend, the hamstrings and back extensors control the descent eccentrically; once the torso hangs, they may largely relax and gravity does the work.
:::

## Roles muscles play

Table: Muscle roles {#tab:muscle-roles}

| Role | Definition | Example: lifting the arms overhead |
|---|---|---|
| Agonist (prime mover) | the main muscle producing a movement | anterior deltoid (shoulder flexion) |
| Antagonist | the muscle opposing the movement, which must lengthen | latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major (lower fibres) |
| Synergist | assists the agonist or neutralises unwanted actions | serratus anterior and trapezius rotating the scapula upward |
| Stabiliser (fixator) | holds a neighbouring segment steady so that the movement can happen | trunk muscles preventing the lower back from arching |

**Open and closed chains.** When the distal end of a limb is free (lifting the leg in leg raises) the chain is *open*; when it is fixed against the floor (standing, plank) the chain is *closed* and the same muscles may move the trunk on the limb rather than the limb on the trunk. In warrior I the hip flexors of the back leg act in a closed chain on the pelvis — which is why tight hip flexors tip the pelvis forward.

## Length and tension

A muscle produces its greatest force at intermediate lengths, where actin and myosin overlap optimally; at very short or very long lengths it is weaker (the **length–tension relationship**) [@hall2021]. This has two practical consequences:

- **Active insufficiency.** A muscle that crosses two joints cannot shorten fully over both at once. Try bending the knee strongly while the hip is extended: the hamstrings cramp or weaken because they are already short at the hip.
- **Passive insufficiency.** A two-joint muscle cannot be fully lengthened over both joints at once. The hamstrings limit a forward bend with straight legs far more than with bent knees. This is why bending the knees in paścimottānāsana or pāda hastāsana immediately allows the hips to flex further — and why it is a legitimate modification, not a cheat.

## Reflexes and the nervous control of stretch

Muscles contain **muscle spindles**, sensory receptors that detect changes in muscle length and its rate of change. When a muscle is stretched quickly, the spindles trigger a reflex contraction of the same muscle — the **stretch reflex**. This is one reason why bouncing into a stretch is counter-productive: the muscle resists. **Golgi tendon organs**, in the tendons, sense tension. The nervous system also coordinates opposing muscles through **reciprocal inhibition**: when an agonist contracts, its antagonist is reflexly inhibited, so that, for instance, actively contracting the quadriceps can help the hamstrings to yield [@kandel2021].

These reflexes are real and useful, but popular accounts exaggerate their role in long-term flexibility. Contract–relax (“PNF”) stretching, often explained by tendon-organ inhibition, appears to work largely through increased tolerance to stretch [@weppler2010].

## How flexibility actually changes

It is widely assumed that regular stretching makes muscles physically longer. The evidence suggests otherwise, at least in the short and medium term. Several weeks of stretching increase range of motion mainly by increasing **tolerance to the sensation of stretch** — the nervous system allows the muscle to go further before signalling “stop” — with little measurable change in the muscle’s mechanical properties [[S]] [@magnusson1996; @weppler2010]. Structural changes may occur over longer periods, and the question is not fully settled.

::: keyconcept Flexibility is partly a skill of the nervous system
Much of what students experience as “getting more flexible” is the nervous system learning that a range is safe. This is good news: it responds to calm, regular, unforced practice — to breath, relaxation and trust — exactly the qualities a classical class cultivates. It also means that forcing, which alarms the nervous system, tends to be counter-productive.
:::

### Stretching principles

Table: Evidence-informed principles for stretching {#tab:stretching}

| Principle | Guidance | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | regular practice (at least 2–3 days a week; daily is more effective) | @garber2011 |
| Duration | holding 10–30 seconds is effective for most adults; older adults may benefit from 30–60 seconds; about 60 seconds total per muscle group per session | @garber2011 |
| Intensity | to the point of mild tension or discomfort, not pain | @garber2011 |
| Before explosive activity | long static holds (over about 60 seconds) immediately before power or speed tasks can slightly reduce performance; shorter holds have little effect | @behm2011 |
| Warm tissue | stretching is more comfortable after a warm-up | general consensus |

Yoga postures are usually held longer than the minimum and combine stretching with isometric strength, balance and breathing, so they do not map neatly onto these guidelines — but the principles of regularity, moderate intensity and patience apply fully.

::: myth “More flexibility is always better.”
Flexibility beyond what one’s activities require has no demonstrated health benefit, and some researchers argue that flexibility should not be treated as a major component of fitness at all [@nuzzo2020]. For hypermobile students, *more* range can mean *less* stability (@ch:safety). The yogic aim is freedom of movement and ease in the body — not maximum range.
:::

## Strength in yoga

Muscle strength develops through **progressive overload** — gradually increasing the demand — and is **specific**: muscles become stronger in the ranges and types of contraction in which they are trained [@garber2011]. Yoga develops strength mainly through isometric holds and slow concentric and eccentric movement against body weight: chaturaṅga, plank, crow, locust and the leg raises of the classical class all build meaningful strength. Yoga is less effective for building maximal strength or bone density in the way that heavy resistance training does; for most students it is best seen as one part of a physically active life that also includes walking and strength training [@bull2020].

::: tip Strength and flexibility together
Build strength at the ends of the range you are gaining. In practice: after passively stretching the hamstrings in a forward bend, practise lifting the leg actively in leg raises. The nervous system is more willing to allow a range it can control.
:::

## Soreness

Unaccustomed exercise, especially eccentric work, commonly produces **delayed-onset muscle soreness** (DOMS), peaking one to three days later and resolving within about a week. It is normal after the first days of an intensive training course. Soreness that is sharp, localised to a joint, accompanied by swelling, or that worsens rather than eases should be reported (@ch:safety).

::: takeaways
- Muscle fibres shorten by the sliding of actin and myosin; force is graded by recruiting motor units; muscles can only pull.
- Contractions are concentric (shortening), isometric (holding) or eccentric (lengthening under control); gravity decides which muscles work.
- Muscles act as agonists, antagonists, synergists and stabilisers; two-joint muscles show active and passive insufficiency.
- The stretch reflex resists fast stretch; flexibility gains in the medium term come mainly from increased stretch tolerance.
- Regular, moderate, unforced stretching and progressive strength work are the evidence-based foundations; more flexibility is not always better.
:::
