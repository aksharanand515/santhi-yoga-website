- explain the power dynamics of the teacher–student relationship, including projection and dependence;

- write a personal code of conduct.

## Why ethics comes first

A yoga class places people in unusual positions: lying with eyes closed, in close physical proximity to a teacher, sometimes emotionally open, often trusting the teacher’s judgement about their bodies. That trust is the ground of good teaching — and the reason abuse of it is so damaging. The history of modern yoga includes prominent teachers who exploited students sexually, financially and psychologically, often protected by their communities’ reverence [@remski2019]. Ethics is not an optional module; it is the foundation on which everything else in this section stands.

## The yamas and niyamas as a professional code

Table: A professional ethic drawn from the yamas and niyamas {#tab:ethic}

| Principle | In professional practice |
|---|---|
| *Ahiṃsā* | put student safety and wellbeing first; no forcing; no humiliation; no discrimination |
| *Satya* | honest representation of training, qualifications and lineage; no exaggerated health claims; admit mistakes |
| *Asteya* | respect students’ time, money and ideas; credit sources; do not take students from other teachers dishonestly |
| *Brahmacarya* | no sexual or romantic involvement with current students; appropriate touch only with consent |
| *Aparigraha* | do not cultivate dependence; let students go; avoid possessiveness over students or methods |
| *Śauca* | clean, safe environment; clear intentions; transparent business practice |
| *Santoṣa* | contentment with an honest living; no pressure-selling |
| *Tapas* | commitment to preparation, continuing education and your own practice |
| *Svādhyāya* | reflective practice; seek supervision; learn from complaints and feedback |
| *Īśvara-praṇidhāna* | humility; the teaching is not about you |

## The teacher–student relationship

### Power

Teachers hold power: of knowledge, of position at the front of the room, of physical proximity and touch, and of the spiritual authority students may project onto them. Power is not in itself a problem — a teacher needs authority to keep a class safe — but it must be recognised, used for students’ benefit and never exploited [@farhi2006].

### Projection and dependence

Students sometimes see their teacher as wiser, kinder or more spiritually advanced than any human being is — a process psychologists call *projection* or *transference*. They may seek approval, personal advice or special attention, or develop romantic feelings. Teachers can also project onto students (seeing them as disciples, friends or admirers). Recognising this is the first step; maintaining steady, professional warmth is the second. A good teacher’s aim is to make students **less** dependent on them over time — more able to practise, discern and care for themselves.

::: keyconcept The direction of care
In a professional relationship, care flows from teacher to student. If you notice that you are relying on students for emotional support, friendship, admiration or romantic attention, that is a signal to seek support elsewhere — from peers, mentors or professionals.
:::

## Boundaries

Table: Professional boundaries in yoga teaching {#tab:boundaries}

| Boundary | Clear practice |
|---|---|
| **Sexual** | No sexual or romantic relationships with current students. If a genuine mutual attraction arises, the teaching relationship ends first — the student is referred to another teacher, and time passes — and the power imbalance is honestly considered. Sexualised comments, jokes or touch are never acceptable. |
| **Physical** | Touch only with consent, for a clear teaching purpose, on appropriate areas, and with the student free to decline at any time (@ch:adjustments). |
| **Emotional** | Listen with care, but do not act as a therapist; refer when a student’s needs exceed your role. |
| **Financial** | Transparent prices; no pressure-selling; no borrowing from or lending to students; declare conflicts of interest (e.g. commissions on products you recommend). |
| **Time** | Start and end on time; set clear availability for messages; protect your own rest. |
| **Digital** | Professional social-media presence; no photographs or videos of students without explicit consent; careful private messaging. |
| **Confidentiality** | Health information and personal disclosures stay private, except where there is a risk of serious harm. |

### Dual relationships

A *dual relationship* exists when a teacher has a second relationship with a student — friend, employer, landlord, business partner, family member. In small communities (and in Fort Kochi) these are often unavoidable. They are not forbidden, but they require extra care: keep roles distinct, be alert to conflicts of interest, and avoid situations in which a student could feel unable to say no.

### Gifts

Small gifts of gratitude are part of many cultures, including India’s, and can be graciously received. Large or repeated gifts, or gifts that seem to carry expectations, should be politely declined or discussed.

## Cultural respect

Yoga comes from Indian traditions and is being taught here in its land of origin to students from many countries. Respect means:

- learning and pronouncing Sanskrit carefully, and translating it;
- presenting practices with their context and lineage;
- not using sacred images, mantras or symbols as decoration or marketing;
- acknowledging the diversity of Indian traditions rather than presenting one as “the” yoga;
- welcoming students of all faiths without asking them to adopt a religious identity.

## When things go wrong

Mistakes will happen: a cue that hurt someone, a careless comment, a boundary blurred. The ethical response is to **acknowledge**, **apologise**, **repair** and **learn**. Schools should have a clear, accessible way for students to raise concerns, and teachers should welcome feedback rather than defending against it. If you observe another teacher behaving unethically — particularly anything involving sexual misconduct or abuse — it must be reported to the school’s leadership, and where the law requires, to the appropriate authorities.

::: exercise Ethical dilemmas
In small groups, discuss what you would do:

1. A student you teach weekly invites you for dinner, alone.
2. A student tells you in confidence that she is being harmed at home.
3. A studio asks you to advertise that your class “detoxes the liver and cures back pain”.
4. A student gives you an expensive gift after you helped them through a difficult time.
5. You notice a colleague repeatedly adjusting one student in a way that seems unnecessary and intimate.
6. A student asks you to photograph them in an advanced posture for their social media, while other students are in the background.

For each, name the principles in tension and decide on a course of action.
:::

::: takeaways
- The trust students place in teachers creates power, which must be recognised and used only for their benefit.
- The yamas and niyamas translate directly into a professional code of conduct.
- Projection and dependence are normal; the teacher’s aim is to make students more independent.
- Clear sexual, physical, emotional, financial, time, digital and confidentiality boundaries protect students and teachers.
- Mistakes are acknowledged, apologised for, repaired and learned from; misconduct by others is reported.
:::
