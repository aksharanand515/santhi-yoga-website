::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- read and use an asana–anatomy map: joints, movements, muscles, types of contraction, loading, limitations and modifications;
- compare postures across families by the demands they place on the body;
- use the maps to choose preparatory postures, counterposes and modifications when planning a class;
- construct a map for any posture not included here.
:::

## How to read the maps

Each row of the tables that follow summarises one posture in eight columns:

- **Joints** — the joints doing the most significant work or reaching the greatest range.
- **Movement** — what those joints are doing, in anatomical terms (@ch:anatomy-foundations).
- **Key muscles** — the principal muscles involved (@ch:muscle-atlas).
- **Muscle action** — C = concentric, E = eccentric, I = isometric, L = lengthening under load (@ch:muscle-principles).
- **Loading** — where the posture concentrates load, and any structure that deserves care (@ch:biomechanics).
- **Common limitations** — what most often limits students.
- **Modifications** — first-line options for those limitations.

The maps are deliberately simplified. Every posture involves more muscles than are listed, and individuals perform the same posture in different ways. Use the maps as a planning tool and a memory aid, not as a description of what must happen in every body.

::: tip Using the maps to plan
To prepare for a posture, look at its **joints** and **key muscles** and choose earlier postures that mobilise those joints and warm those muscles. To choose a counterpose, look at its **movement** and choose a posture that moves the main joints gently in the opposite direction. To prepare modifications, read the **limitations** column before class and have the props ready.
:::

## Mapping a new posture

::: exercise Build your own map
Choose a posture not included in these tables — for example *pārśvottānāsana* (intense side stretch), *ardha candrāsana* (half moon) or *gomukhāsana* (cow face) arms. Complete all eight columns, then check your map with a partner: do you agree on the main movement, the key muscles and where the load goes? Finally, test your “modifications” column by teaching the posture to someone with the limitation you listed.
:::

::: takeaways
- An asana–anatomy map summarises joints, movements, muscles, contraction types, loading, limitations and modifications for a posture.
- Maps help in choosing preparations (same joints and muscles), counterposes (opposite movements) and modifications (from the limitations).
- The maps simplify: bodies differ, and so do their ways of making a shape.
- Any posture can be mapped with the same eight questions.
:::

::: reflect
1. Which column of the maps is most useful to you as a new teacher? Why?
2. Which posture in the tables surprised you with its loading? How will that change how you teach it?
3. How could you use a simplified version of a map to help a student understand their own practice?
:::

::: practice
1. Using the tables, plan a sequence of five preparatory postures for wheel (ūrdhva dhanurāsana).
2. Choose a counterpose for each of the three prone backbends using the “movement” column, and justify each.
3. Map two postures you teach often that are not in the tables, and present them to your practice group.
:::

::: check

3. According to the maps, which posture loads the wrist in extension with the most body weight? (a) cat–cow (b) crow (c) cobra (d) bridge
4. Supported bridge places a block: (a) under the head (b) under the sacrum (c) under the feet (d) between the knees

:::
