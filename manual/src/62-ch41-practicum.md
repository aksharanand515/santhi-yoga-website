By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- describe the components of the practicum and how you will be assessed;

- prepare for the written and practical final examinations;

## The practicum

The **practicum** is where you learn to teach by teaching — to your fellow trainees, and in supervised settings. It runs through the whole course, growing in length and responsibility:

Table: Practicum progression (indicative; your course leader will confirm the schedule) {#tab:practicum}

| Week | Practicum task | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | teach one posture to a partner; teach the opening relaxation | pairs |
| 2 | teach sūrya namaskār and two basic postures; observe a senior teacher’s class | small groups |
| 3 | teach a 30-minute class segment; give and receive structured feedback | groups of 4–6 |
| 4 | teach a full class (45–60 minutes) as the final practical examination | whole group / public class |

Throughout, you will keep a **practicum log** (Appendix I) of every teaching session: date, length, content, feedback received, and your reflection.

## Observation

Observing experienced teachers — and fellow trainees — trains your eye. Use the **teacher observation sheet** (Appendix I) to note: structure and timing; cueing (clarity, economy, types); demonstration; observation and adjustment; use of voice and silence; inclusion and options; safety; atmosphere. Focus on what you can learn, not on judging.

## Giving and receiving feedback

Feedback is a gift when it is specific, balanced and kind.

**Giving feedback:**

- Start by asking the teacher how it felt to teach.
- Name **specific strengths** (“Your cue ‘press the floor away’ transformed my downward dog”).
- Offer **one or two specific suggestions**, phrased as possibilities (“You might try cueing the feet before the arms”).
- Base feedback on what you observed and experienced, not on personal preference.

**Receiving feedback:**

- Listen without defending or explaining.
- Ask for examples if something is unclear.
- Thank the giver.
- Decide afterwards what to act on.

## Assessment

You will be assessed on knowledge, practice, teaching and professional conduct. The components, used together to judge readiness to teach, are:

1. **Attendance and participation** in all sessions (absences agreed with the course leader and made up).
2. **Personal practice**: the ability to practise the twelve basic postures (with modifications appropriate to your body), sūrya namaskār, the prāṇāyāma taught and meditation.
3. **Assignments**: the teaching assignments at the end of each chapter form your portfolio.
4. **Practicum log and observation sheets**.
5. **Written examination**: philosophy, anatomy, safety, prāṇāyāma, meditation and teaching methodology.
6. **Practical teaching examination**: a full class taught to a group and assessed with the practicum assessment form.
7. **Professional conduct**: punctuality, ethics, care for others and for the school.

### What “ready to teach” means

The assessment does not ask for perfect postures or flawless classes. It asks whether you can teach a safe, clear, well-sequenced class to generally healthy beginners and mixed-level students; adapt it for common limitations; explain what you teach with honesty; and conduct yourself as a trustworthy professional.

## The final teaching examination

The practical examination is a full class (45–60 minutes, as assigned) taught to fellow trainees and/or members of the public, observed by one or more senior teachers.

Table: Practical examination criteria {#tab:exam-criteria}

| Criterion | What assessors look for |
|---|---|
| Preparation | a written lesson plan with intention, timings, options and safety notes |
| Opening | welcome, health check, clear orientation, opening relaxation |
| Sequencing | coherent, safe structure with preparation, counterposes and adequate rest |
| Instruction | clear, economical verbal cues timed with the breath; correct Sanskrit and English names |
| Demonstration | purposeful and appropriate, not continuous |
| Observation and adaptation | circulates, sees the room, responds to students, offers options |
| Safety | screens for contraindications; modifications; no forcing; appropriate use of touch with consent |
| Breath, relaxation and meditation | prāṇāyāma taught correctly; relaxation guided with appropriate voice and silence |
| Presence and voice | calm, audible, warm, authentic |
| Time management | starts and ends on time; relaxation protected |
| Reflection | honest self-assessment after the class |

The written examination and a sample of its questions are in Appendix K. The assessment forms are in Appendix I; the completion checklist is in Appendix L.

::: tip Preparing for the practical examination
Teach your exam class at least twice beforehand to classmates. Time it. Memorise the structure, not a script. Have a plan for beginners and a plan for anyone with an injury. On the day, arrive early, set up the room, breathe — and teach the people in front of you.
:::

## Graduation

On successful completion of all components you receive the Santhi Yoga School 200-hour certificate, traditionally presented in a closing ceremony. Graduation is an ending and a beginning: of your life as a teacher, and of a lifetime of study.

!photo(gbp-cover-graduation-ceremony-1280x720.jpg|A graduation ceremony at Santhi Yoga School.)

::: takeaways
- The practicum builds from teaching a single posture to teaching a full class, supported by a log, observation and feedback.
- Specific, balanced, kind feedback — given and received well — accelerates learning.
- Assessment covers attendance, personal practice, assignments, practicum, written and practical examinations, and professional conduct.
- “Ready to teach” means safe, clear, well-sequenced, adaptable, honest and professional — not perfect.
:::
