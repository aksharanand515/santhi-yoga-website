## Organisation

The **central nervous system** (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord. The **peripheral nervous system** (PNS) consists of the nerves that connect the CNS with the rest of the body: 12 pairs of cranial nerves and 31 pairs of spinal nerves [@kandel2021]. Functionally the PNS is divided into:

- the **somatic** nervous system, which carries sensation from the skin, muscles and joints and controls voluntary movement of skeletal muscle; and
- the **autonomic** nervous system (ANS), which regulates the internal organs, blood vessels and glands largely outside conscious control.

Nerves carry information in both directions: **afferent** (sensory) fibres towards the CNS and **efferent** (motor) fibres away from it. A great deal of what yoga trains is afferent — noticing, sensing, feeling.

## The autonomic nervous system

The ANS has two main branches, which generally have opposing effects on the organs they both supply [@hall2021]:

- The **sympathetic** branch, whose nerves leave the spinal cord in the thoracic and upper lumbar regions (T1–L2), prepares the body for action: heart rate and blood pressure rise, airways dilate, digestion slows, glucose is released.
- The **parasympathetic** branch, whose fibres leave the brainstem in four cranial nerves (III, VII, IX and X) and the sacral spinal cord (S2–S4), supports rest, digestion and repair: heart rate slows, digestion and secretion increase.

!fig(ans)

These are sometimes called “fight or flight” and “rest and digest”. The labels are useful but oversimplified: both branches are active all the time, their balance shifts continuously from moment to moment, and health depends on the **flexibility** of that balance rather than on one branch dominating. A third division, the **enteric** nervous system, is a network of neurons in the wall of the gut.

### The vagus nerve

The **vagus nerve** (cranial nerve X, “the wanderer”) runs from the brainstem through the neck to the heart, lungs and most of the abdominal organs. It carries about three-quarters of all parasympathetic fibres [@hall2021] — and most of its fibres are **sensory**, carrying information from the organs to the brain [@gerritsen2018]. The vagus slows the heart; this is why heart rate falls during exhalation, when vagal influence is greatest (@fig:rsa).

### Stress and allostasis

When we meet a challenge, the sympathetic system and the hormonal **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis** mobilise the body; cortisol and adrenaline rise. This response is healthy and necessary. Problems arise when it is triggered too often, too strongly or for too long, without recovery — a burden sometimes called *allostatic load* [@hall2021]. Much of what yoga offers a stressed modern student is a structured practice of **recovery**: slowing the breath, releasing muscular tension, resting in relaxation.

::: evidence Polyvagal theory: popular, contested
Many yoga trainings teach the **polyvagal theory**, which proposes that a specifically mammalian branch of the vagus supports social engagement and safety, and that the nervous system moves in a hierarchy of states [@porges2011]. The theory has been influential in trauma-informed practice. However, several of its core anatomical and evolutionary premises have been strongly challenged by researchers in autonomic physiology [@grossman2023]. It is reasonable to use its everyday language — feeling safe, feeling threatened, feeling shut down — as a description of experience; it is not reasonable to present its neuroanatomy as settled fact.
:::

## Sensing the body

### Proprioception

**Proprioception** is the sense of the position and movement of the body and its parts. It depends on muscle spindles, tendon organs, receptors in joints and skin, and the brain’s processing of their signals together with vision and the vestibular system [@proske2012]. Every alignment cue — “feel the weight in the heels”, “notice where your arms are” — is a proprioceptive instruction. Proprioception can be trained: balance practice improves it, and fatigue and injury impair it.

### Interoception

**Interoception** is the sense of the physiological condition of the body — heartbeat, breath, hunger, temperature, visceral sensations, the bodily feelings of emotion [@craig2002]. Contemplative practices such as body scanning, breath awareness and yoga nidrā cultivate attention to interoceptive signals, and researchers have proposed that improved interoceptive awareness is one of the ways such practices influence emotional regulation and health [@farb2015]. This is an active field; measures of interoception are still debated.

::: keyconcept Proprioception, interoception and pratyāhāra
Yoga turns attention from the external world to the body’s position (proprioception) and inner condition (interoception). In the language of the tradition, this is the beginning of pratyāhāra (@ch:limbs-outer). The two descriptions — traditional and physiological — illuminate each other without being the same thing.
:::

### Balance

Balance integrates three inputs — vision, the vestibular organs of the inner ear, and proprioception — with motor responses from the ankles, hips and stepping (@ch:biomechanics). Yoga-based exercise has been found to improve balance and mobility in adults over 60 [@youkhana2016], and exercise programmes that challenge balance reduce falls in older people [@sherrington2019] — one of the clearest practical benefits a teacher can offer older students.

## How movement is learned

Skilled movement is learned in recognisable stages [@fitts1967; @schmidt2019]:

!fig(motorlearning)

Two findings from motor-learning research are directly useful to yoga teachers:

1. **An external focus of attention often helps.** Instructions that direct attention to the effect of a movement or to the environment (“press the floor away”, “reach towards the wall”) often produce better performance and learning than instructions that direct attention to the body’s own mechanics (“contract your triceps”) [@wulf2013]. This does not mean internal cues are wrong — in yoga, internal awareness is often the point — but it explains why simple images frequently work better than anatomical instructions for learning a shape.
2. **Autonomy and confidence enhance learning.** Giving learners some choice and supporting their sense of competence improves learning [@wulf2016]. Offering options (“choose the variation that suits you today”) is good pedagogy, not merely kindness.

The nervous system is also **plastic**: it changes with practice. This is the physiological counterpart of the yogic teaching on saṃskāras — repeated experience lays down patterns that shape future experience (@ch:mind).

## Pain

Pain is defined by the International Association for the Study of Pain as “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage” [@raja2020]. Pain is produced by the brain as a protective response; it usually reflects threat to the tissues, but its intensity is also shaped by context, attention, expectation, mood and past experience [@butler2013]. This has two practical implications: pain always deserves to be taken seriously; and pain does not map neatly onto damage, so a teacher should neither dismiss it nor catastrophise about it (@ch:safety).

## Yoga and the nervous system: the evidence

Table: A summary of research on yoga and the nervous system {#tab:ns-evidence}

| Claim | What research suggests | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Slow breathing increases heart rate variability and baroreflex sensitivity | consistent acute effects in many small studies | moderate [@zaccaro2018; @russo2017] |
| Yoga reduces physiological markers of stress (e.g. cortisol, blood pressure) | reductions in some markers compared with controls in meta-analysis | low–moderate [@pascoe2017] |
| Yoga improves symptoms of anxiety and depression | benefits over no treatment; evidence quality mixed | low–moderate [@cramer2018; @cramer2013dep] |
| Yoga increases brain GABA | one small trial found increases after yoga compared with walking; mechanism hypotheses remain unconfirmed | preliminary [@streeter2010; @streeter2012] |
| Yoga “switches on” the parasympathetic system | an oversimplification; effects depend on practice type and person | — |
| Yoga improves balance in older adults | consistent small–moderate benefits | moderate [@youkhana2016] |

::: tip Speaking about the nervous system in class
Accurate and helpful: “Slowing the breath and lengthening the exhalation tends to calm the body.” Inaccurate and unhelpful: “This pose stimulates your vagus nerve and resets your nervous system.” Choose language that invites students to notice their own experience rather than language that promises a mechanism.
:::

::: takeaways
- The nervous system comprises the CNS and PNS; the PNS includes somatic and autonomic divisions.
- The sympathetic and parasympathetic branches both operate continuously; health reflects flexible balance. The vagus nerve carries most parasympathetic fibres, most of them sensory.
- Proprioception and interoception are trainable senses at the heart of posture and meditation practice.
- Motor learning progresses through stages; external-focus cues and learner autonomy aid learning.
- Pain is a protective output of the brain shaped by context, not a direct measure of damage.
- Evidence for yoga’s effects on the nervous system is promising in places but often preliminary; polyvagal theory is contested.
:::
