::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- list the eight limbs of yoga with their meanings and explain their relationship;
- define each of the five yamas and five niyamas, with the result Patañjali attributes to each;
- explain *pratipakṣa-bhāvanam*, the cultivation of the opposite;
- apply the yamas and niyamas to personal practice, to the conduct of a class and to professional life;
- discuss the yamas and niyamas with students without moralising.
:::

## The eight limbs

Patañjali presents the eight limbs (*aṣṭāṅga*) as the means to discriminating knowledge, which removes the impurities that obscure it (YS 2.28–29):

!fig(eightlimbs)

It is common to present the limbs as rungs on a ladder, to be climbed in order. This is partly right: Patañjali lists them in a logical sequence from outer to inner. But the word *aṅga* means “limb” — as of a body — and the limbs grow together. A beginner practises all eight in some form in the first class: behaving kindly (yama), arriving on time (niyama), sitting (āsana), breathing (prāṇāyāma), closing the eyes (pratyāhāra), focusing (dhāraṇā). The sequence describes increasing subtlety, not a waiting list [[I]].

## The yamas: restraints

The five **yamas** are restraints — primarily in our dealings with others (YS 2.30). Patañjali calls them the “great vow” (*mahāvrata*) when they are practised universally, unlimited by birth, place, time or circumstance (2.31). This is a demanding standard: ahiṃsā is not only for the practice hall, or for people we like, or for days when we feel calm.

Table: The five yamas (YS 2.30, 2.35–2.39) {#tab:yamas}

| Yama | Meaning | Result according to Patañjali |
|---|---|---|
| *ahiṃsā* | non-harming in thought, word and deed | in the presence of one established in it, hostility is abandoned (2.35) |
| *satya* | truthfulness | actions and their fruits come to rest on one’s word (2.36) |
| *asteya* | non-stealing | all jewels present themselves (2.37) |
| *brahmacarya* | “conduct in brahman”; sexual restraint, moderation of the senses | vigour is gained (2.38) |
| *aparigraha* | non-possessiveness, non-grasping | knowledge of the “how” of births arises (2.39) |

The results are traditional teachings [[T]]; their language is poetic, but their psychology is recognisable. People relax in the presence of someone who will not harm them. A truthful person’s word becomes reliable. Someone who does not grasp becomes clear-sighted about what drives their life.

### Ahiṃsā

Non-harming is the root of the other yamas; Vyāsa’s commentary says the others are practised for the sake of ahiṃsā [@bryant2009]. Traditionally it includes vegetarianism, which remains part of the Sivananda tradition’s “proper diet”. In the practice hall, ahiṃsā has a very direct meaning: **do not harm the body in the name of yoga**. Pushing through pain, forcing a student into a posture or practising prāṇāyāma aggressively are failures of ahiṃsā before they are failures of technique.

### Satya

Truthfulness is to be practised in the service of ahiṃsā: the tradition holds that truth should be spoken so that it is also beneficial and kind. For teachers, satya means honesty about what you know (“I don’t know — I will find out”), about your training and qualifications, and about the benefits of yoga. Exaggerated health claims in marketing are a breach of satya.

### Asteya

Non-stealing extends beyond property to time, credit and ideas. A teacher who starts late steals students’ time; one who presents another teacher’s sequence as their own takes credit that is not theirs.

### Brahmacarya

Literally “moving in brahman”, *brahmacarya* has meant celibacy for renunciates and faithfulness and moderation for householders. In modern professional terms its most important application is unambiguous: **a teacher must never exploit the trust of the teaching relationship for sexual or romantic purposes** (@ch:ethics). More widely, it means moderation — conserving energy rather than scattering it.

### Aparigraha

Non-possessiveness includes not grasping at achievements. A student fixated on “getting” a posture practises parigraha on the mat; a teacher who needs students to be “theirs” practises it in the studio.

## The niyamas: observances

The five **niyamas** concern one’s relationship with oneself (YS 2.32). The last three are identical with kriyā yoga (2.1).

Table: The five niyamas (YS 2.32, 2.40–2.45) {#tab:niyamas}

| Niyama | Meaning | Result according to Patañjali |
|---|---|---|
| *śauca* | cleanliness, purity of body and mind | detachment from one’s own body and from contact with others; purity of mind, cheerfulness, one-pointedness, mastery of the senses, fitness for self-realisation (2.40–41) |
| *santoṣa* | contentment | unexcelled happiness (2.42) |
| *tapas* | disciplined effort, austerity | perfection of body and senses through destruction of impurities (2.43) |
| *svādhyāya* | study, self-study, recitation | connection with one’s chosen deity (2.44) |
| *īśvara-praṇidhāna* | devotion to Īśvara; surrender | perfection of samādhi (2.45) |

### Śauca

Cleanliness of the body, the practice space and the mind. Haṭha Yoga develops external śauca into the six cleansing practices (@ch:hatha). For a teacher, śauca includes a clean, well-ventilated room, clean mats, personal hygiene, and a mind cleared of the day’s irritations before entering class.

### Santoṣa

Contentment is not complacency. It is the capacity to be at peace with what is while continuing to practise. The student who is content with a modified posture is practising santoṣa; so is the teacher content with a small class.

### Tapas

The word means “heat”. It is the willingness to endure discomfort for a worthwhile purpose — rising at dawn, holding a posture, keeping silence. Tapas must be balanced by ahiṃsā: the heat that transforms is not the heat that burns.

### Svādhyāya

Study of sacred texts, recitation of mantra, and — in modern readings — study of oneself [[I]]. This manual, your practice journal and your reflections on your own reactions in class are all svādhyāya.

### Īśvara-praṇidhāna

Surrender to something greater than oneself. For a devotional student this is surrender to God; for others, it may be a letting go of the ego’s need to control outcomes — which brings us back to the Gītā’s *niṣkāma karma*.

## Cultivating the opposite

When disturbing thoughts arise — thoughts of harming, lying or grasping — Patañjali advises ***pratipakṣa-bhāvanam***, “cultivating the opposite” (2.33). He explains that such thoughts, whether acted upon, caused in others or approved of, and whether mild, moderate or intense, bear the fruit of endless suffering and ignorance (2.34). Reflecting on this is itself the cultivation of the opposite. This is not the suppression of feeling but a deliberate turning of attention — remarkably close to modern cognitive approaches to emotion [[I]].

## The yamas and niyamas in the practice hall

Table: Applying the yamas and niyamas to teaching {#tab:yn-teaching}

| | In your own practice | In the class you teach |
|---|---|---|
| Ahiṃsā | practise without pain; rest when needed | offer options; never force; create a room where no one is shamed |
| Satya | be honest about your limits | make no claims you cannot support; admit what you do not know |
| Asteya | give full effort and attention | start and end on time; credit sources |
| Brahmacarya | moderation in effort, food, sleep | maintain clear professional boundaries |
| Aparigraha | let go of attachment to poses | let students progress at their own pace; do not compare them |
| Śauca | clean body, clean space, clear mind | clean room and props; hygiene; tidy preparation |
| Santoṣa | contentment with today’s practice | celebrate small progress; reduce comparison |
| Tapas | daily discipline | keep your own practice alive as a teacher |
| Svādhyāya | study and self-observation | keep learning; invite students to observe themselves |
| Īśvara-praṇidhāna | surrender the ego’s need to achieve | teach as an offering, not a performance |

::: tip Speaking about ethics without preaching
Students do not come to class to be told how to live. Let the yamas and niyamas show in *how you teach* — your punctuality, honesty and kindness — and speak about them lightly: “Today, see if you can practise without harming yourself even a little.” One sentence, lived, is worth an hour of instruction.
:::

::: takeaways
- The eight limbs are yama, niyama, āsana, prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi; they grow together like limbs of one body.
- The yamas — ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, brahmacarya, aparigraha — are a “great vow” when practised without exception (2.31).
- The niyamas — śauca, santoṣa, tapas, svādhyāya, īśvara-praṇidhāna — shape one’s relationship with oneself; the last three form kriyā yoga.
- Pratipakṣa-bhāvanam, cultivating the opposite, is Patañjali’s method for working with harmful thoughts.
- For a teacher the yamas and niyamas become a professional ethic: non-harm, honesty, punctuality, boundaries, humility and continued practice.
:::
