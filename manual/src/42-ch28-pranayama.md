::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- practise and teach breath awareness, diaphragmatic breathing, full yogic breathing, nāḍī śodhana (anuloma viloma), ujjāyī, bhrāmarī, kapālabhāti, bhastrikā and the cooling breaths;
- explain breath retention (kumbhaka), traditional ratios and the use of bandhas with retention, and how to progress safely;
- state the contraindications and precautions for each practice;
- distinguish the effects traditionally attributed to each practice from what physiological research shows;
- plan a prāṇāyāma sequence for beginners and for experienced students.
:::

## The place of prāṇāyāma

Prāṇāyāma is the fourth limb of Patañjali’s yoga (@ch:limbs-outer) and a central practice of Haṭha Yoga (@ch:hatha). In the classical class it comes near the beginning, after the opening relaxation and before the sun salutation, so that the breath and mind are steady before the body moves.

The traditional aim of prāṇāyāma is the regulation of *prāṇa* and, through it, of the mind — ultimately preparing for concentration and meditation (YS 2.52–53) [[T]]. The physiological mechanisms are described in @ch:breathing: breathing rate and depth influence blood gases, heart-rate rhythms and the autonomic nervous system, and attention to the breath is itself a form of concentration. Proposed mechanisms linking slow breathing to calm — for instance through pulmonary stretch receptors and vagal pathways — remain largely hypotheses [@jerath2006; @brown2005] [[I]].

## General guidelines

- **Empty stomach**: at least two to three hours after a meal (longer for kapālabhāti, bhastrikā and bandhas).
- **Posture**: a steady, comfortable seated posture with the spine upright — cross-legged on a cushion, kneeling, or on a chair. Lying down is suitable only for breath awareness and relaxed abdominal breathing.
- **Nose**: breathe through the nose unless the technique specifies otherwise.
- **Place**: clean, quiet, well ventilated, neither hot nor draughty.
- **No strain, ever.** The breath should never be forced, and the practice should end with the breath and mind calmer than at the start. Dizziness, tingling, breathlessness, headache or anxiety mean stop and return to normal breathing.
- **Progress slowly**: weeks, not days, between steps.

::: safety General contraindications and cautions for prāṇāyāma
Gentle slow breathing (awareness, diaphragmatic, full yogic breath without strain, nāḍī śodhana without retention, bhrāmarī) is suitable for almost everyone. **Rapid or forceful breathing** (kapālabhāti, bhastrikā) and **breath retention** (kumbhaka) carry more risk and are commonly advised against, or offered only in gentle forms, in [[C]]: pregnancy; uncontrolled high blood pressure; heart disease; history of stroke; epilepsy; glaucoma, retinal detachment or recent eye surgery; hernia and recent abdominal or chest surgery; lung disease or a history of pneumothorax; panic disorder or severe anxiety (where over-breathing can provoke symptoms); and during acute illness. **Never practise breath retention in water, while driving, or standing where a faint could cause a fall** [@craig1961].
:::

## 1. Breath awareness

**Technique.** Sit or lie comfortably. Without changing anything, notice the breath: where you feel it (nostrils, chest, abdomen), its rhythm, the pauses between inhalation and exhalation, the temperature of the air at the nostrils.

**Purpose.** Establishes attention on the breath and reveals habitual patterns (holding, shallow chest breathing, a rushed exhalation). It is the foundation of every other practice, and a meditation in itself.

**Teaching.** “Let the breath breathe itself. Your only job is to notice.”

## 2. Diaphragmatic (abdominal) breathing

**Technique.** Lying on the back with the knees bent or in śavāsana, place one hand on the abdomen and one on the chest. Inhale through the nose, letting the abdomen rise under the hand while the chest stays relatively quiet; exhale and let the abdomen fall. Continue for 3–5 minutes at a slow, comfortable pace.

**Physiology.** The diaphragm contracts in every inhalation; this practice lets its movement show in the abdomen and reduces the use of the upper-chest accessory muscles (@ch:breathing).

**Common difficulty.** “Reverse breathing” — the abdomen pulling in on inhalation. Slow down, relax the belly, and let the hand rise first.

## 3. Full yogic breath

**Technique.** Sitting upright (or lying), inhale slowly and let the breath fill the body in three overlapping waves: the abdomen expands, then the lower ribs widen, then the upper chest rises slightly. Exhale smoothly in reverse, or simply let all three fall together. Continue for 5–10 rounds at an unforced pace.

**Physiology.** Uses the inspiratory and expiratory reserve volumes (@fig:spirogram). Because it moves more air, it must be *slow*: a fast full breath is over-breathing.

**Cue.** “Belly — ribs — chest. Then let it all go.”

## 4. Nāḍī śodhana / anuloma viloma — alternate-nostril breathing

In the tradition taught here, alternate-nostril breathing with retention is called ***anuloma viloma***; the preparatory form without retention is often called *nāḍī śodhana*, “purification of the channels”. The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* describes it as the principal practice for purifying the nāḍīs (HYP 2.7–10) [[T]].

**Hand position.** Right hand in *Viṣṇu mudrā*: fold the index and middle fingers into the palm; the thumb closes the right nostril, the ring finger (with the little finger) closes the left. Left-handed students may use either hand.

**Technique (beginner, no retention).**

1. Close the right nostril; inhale through the left for a count of 4.
2. Close the left nostril; release the right and exhale through it for 4.
3. Inhale through the right for 4.
4. Close the right; exhale through the left for 4. This is one round.
5. Practise 5–10 rounds, then lengthen the exhalation (4 : 8) over the following weeks.

**Technique (classical anuloma viloma, with retention).** Inhale left for 4 counts; close both nostrils and retain for 16; exhale right for 8; inhale right for 4; retain 16; exhale left for 8 — one round at the classical **1 : 4 : 2** ratio taught in this course.

!fig(nadishodhana)

::: tip Progressing anuloma viloma
Suggested progression over weeks: 4:4 (no retention) → 4:8 → 4:4:8 → 4:8:8 → 4:12:8 → 4:16:8. Only move on when the current ratio is comfortable for ten rounds with no gasping on the inhalation that follows retention. Many students never need retention at all to benefit.
:::

**Physiology and evidence.** Alternate-nostril breathing is usually slow, and slow breathing has well-documented acute effects on heart-rate variability and baroreflex sensitivity [@russo2017; @zaccaro2018]. In a study of healthy volunteers, several weeks of slow breathing practices including alternate-nostril breathing were associated with increased parasympathetic markers [@pal2004]. Whether breathing through one nostril selectively stimulates one side of the brain or one branch of the autonomic system, as is sometimes claimed, has not been established [@raghuraj2008; @saoji2019].

## 5. Ujjāyī — the “victorious” breath

**Technique.** Sitting upright, breathe in and out through the nose while slightly narrowing the back of the throat (the glottis), as if whispering “haaa” with the mouth closed. The breath makes a soft, even sound, like distant waves. Keep the face and throat relaxed; the sound should be audible to you, barely to a neighbour.

**Uses.** Slows and smooths the breath; gives an audible anchor for attention; used in some postural styles throughout the practice. In the *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* it is one of the eight kumbhakas (HYP 2.51–53) [@akers2002].

**Physiology and evidence.** Partial glottic closure increases airway resistance and slows breathing. In yoga beginners, slow breathing increased baroreflex sensitivity; adding ujjāyī did not improve on slow breathing alone [@mason2013].

**Common mistake.** Constricting the throat forcefully, producing a harsh sound and tension in the neck and jaw.

## 6. Bhrāmarī — the humming-bee breath

**Technique.** Sit upright. Close the ears gently with the thumbs (or index fingers on the ear flaps) and rest the other fingers over the closed eyes (ṣaṇmukhī mudrā) or in the lap. Inhale through the nose; exhale slowly while making a steady, low humming sound like a bee, with the lips closed and the teeth slightly apart. Feel the vibration in the head. Repeat 5–10 times.

**Physiology and evidence.** The long exhalation slows breathing; humming increases nasal nitric oxide many times over [@weitzberg2002]. A systematic review of bhrāmarī reported short-term reductions in heart rate and blood pressure and improvements in some psychological measures, but the included studies were small and of limited quality [@kuppusamy2018].

**Cautions.** Very safe; avoid pressing hard on the eyes; not practised lying down.

## 7. Kapālabhāti — “skull-shining” breath

Traditionally one of the six cleansing practices (ṣaṭkarma) rather than a prāṇāyāma (HYP 2.35) [@akers2002], kapālabhāti is taught near the beginning of every class in this course.

**Technique.**

1. Sit upright with the hands on the knees. Take two normal breaths, then inhale moderately.
2. Exhale sharply through the nose by briskly contracting the lower abdominal muscles, then let the abdomen relax: the inhalation happens passively.
3. Continue rhythmically — about one exhalation per second for beginners — for 15–30 exhalations. This is one round.
4. After the last exhalation, breathe normally for several breaths (in the traditional practice, the student then inhales fully and retains the breath briefly; beginners omit this).
5. Practise three rounds, with rest between. Over weeks, increase gradually to 60 or more exhalations per round, and the pace slightly.

**Common mistakes.** Forcing the inhalation; moving the shoulders and chest; pulling the abdomen in on the inhalation; going too fast or too long, causing dizziness.

**Physiology and evidence.** Kapālabhāti raises ventilation well above metabolic need, so carbon dioxide falls: dizziness and tingling are signs of over-breathing (@ch:breathing). Acute studies of fast yogic breathing show mixed autonomic effects [@saoji2019]. A case report describes pneumothorax following kapālabhāti in a young man [@johnson2004]. The traditional claims — that it cleanses the frontal sinuses, energises and clears the mind — are traditional [[T]].

::: safety Kapālabhāti and bhastrikā
Contraindicated or modified [[C]]: pregnancy; menstruation (many traditional teachers advise against it); high blood pressure; heart disease; epilepsy; hernia; recent abdominal or chest surgery; lung disease or history of pneumothorax; glaucoma; panic disorder. Teach slowly, stop at the first sign of dizziness, and never practise before driving or in water.
:::

## 8. Bhastrikā — the bellows breath

**Technique.** Similar to kapālabhāti, but both inhalation and exhalation are active and forceful, like a blacksmith’s bellows. In the traditional form it is followed by a breath retention (HYP 2.59–67) [@akers2002].

**Teaching.** An intermediate practice. Teach only after kapālabhāti is well established, in short rounds (10–20 breaths) at a moderate pace, with full rest between. Contraindications as for kapālabhāti.

## 9. The cooling breaths: śītalī and sītkārī

**Śītalī.** Roll the tongue into a tube, protruding slightly from the lips; inhale slowly through the tongue; close the mouth and exhale through the nose. (Some people cannot roll the tongue; they practise sītkārī instead.)

**Sītkārī.** Teeth lightly together, lips parted; inhale slowly through the teeth with a hissing sound; close the mouth and exhale through the nose.

**Traditional use.** Cooling the body and calming the mind (HYP 2.54–58) [[T]] — welcome on a hot afternoon in Kerala. Physiologically, inhaling through a moist surface cools the mouth and the inhaled air; the slow pace also slows breathing. Avoid in cold weather, and with sensitive teeth (sītkārī).

## Breath retention (kumbhaka)

*Kumbhaka* means “pot” — the body held full (or empty) like a vessel.

- ***Antara kumbhaka***: retention after inhalation.
- ***Bāhya kumbhaka***: retention after exhalation.
- ***Kevala kumbhaka***: spontaneous cessation of breath in deep meditation, described as the goal of prāṇāyāma (HYP 2.71–74) [[T]].

### Ratios

Table: Common breathing ratios {#tab:ratios}

| Ratio (in : hold : out : hold) | Name / use | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 : 1 | equal breathing | beginners; calming and balancing |
| 1 : 2 | lengthened exhalation | beginners; winding down, before sleep |
| 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 | “box” or square breathing | beginners–intermediate; concentration |
| 1 : 2 : 2 | mild retention | intermediate |
| 1 : 4 : 2 | classical anuloma viloma | experienced practitioners |

### Bandhas with retention

In classical practice, *jālandhara bandha* is applied during antara kumbhaka, and *uḍḍīyāna* and *mūla bandha* may be added in bāhya kumbhaka (@ch:bandhas-mudras-mantra). Bandhas with retention are advanced practices, taught individually to healthy students with an established foundation.

::: evidence Breath retention
Holding the breath causes carbon dioxide to rise and oxygen to fall; the urge to breathe comes mainly from the rising CO₂ (@ch:breathing). Short retentions are harmless for healthy people. Long retentions, especially with straining against a closed glottis (a Valsalva-like manoeuvre), cause marked swings in blood pressure and heart rate — the reason for the cardiovascular contraindications. Over-breathing before retention can lead to loss of consciousness without warning [@craig1961]. There is little research on the long-term effects of the traditional ratios [@saoji2019].
:::

## Teaching prāṇāyāma

- **Set up** everyone in a comfortable, upright seat first; offer cushions and chairs.
- **Demonstrate** the technique, then guide it with a calm, steady count — or let each student count at their own pace once learned.
- **Observe**: shoulders rising, faces straining, gasping after retention and swaying are signs to simplify.
- **Sequence**: calming practices (diaphragmatic, nāḍī śodhana, bhrāmarī) suit the end of the day; energising practices (kapālabhāti) the morning. In the classical class, kapālabhāti precedes anuloma viloma.
- **Duration** for beginners: 5–10 minutes in total, increasing over months.
- **Finish** with a few natural breaths and a moment of stillness to notice the effects.

::: takeaways
- Prāṇāyāma regulates the breath to steady the mind; it is practised seated, on an empty stomach, through the nose, without strain.
- Slow practices (awareness, diaphragmatic, full breath, nāḍī śodhana, ujjāyī, bhrāmarī) suit almost everyone; fast practices (kapālabhāti, bhastrikā) and retention need screening and gradual progression.
- Anuloma viloma uses Viṣṇu mudrā and, in its classical form, a 1 : 4 : 2 ratio; beginners omit retention.
- Evidence best supports the acute autonomic effects of slow breathing; many specific traditional claims are untested.
- Never combine over-breathing with long retention, and never practise retention in water or while driving.
:::
