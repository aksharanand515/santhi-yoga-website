::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- describe the qualities, knowledge and skills of an effective yoga teacher;
- explain how adults learn and what this implies for teaching yoga;
- identify your own strengths and areas for growth as a teacher;
- set personal development goals for the course and your first year of teaching.
:::

## Three things a teacher brings

Ask a group of experienced students what made their best yoga teachers good, and their answers fall into three groups:

1. **Who the teacher is** — presence, authenticity, humility, warmth, integrity, a living practice.
2. **What the teacher knows** — the practices, the tradition, the body, safety, how people learn.
3. **What the teacher can do** — see what is happening in the room, communicate clearly, adapt on the spot, hold a group’s attention, create a sense of safety.

A teacher-training course can give you the second and begin the third; the first grows from your own practice and life. None is optional. Knowledge without presence is a lecture; presence without knowledge is charisma, and can be dangerous.

Table: Qualities of an effective yoga teacher {#tab:qualities}

| Domain | Qualities | What it looks like in class |
|---|---|---|
| Being | presence, authenticity, humility, warmth, patience, equanimity | attention on students, not on self; calm when things go wrong |
| Knowing | technique, anatomy, safety, philosophy, pedagogy | accurate cues; appropriate modifications; honest answers |
| Doing | observation, clear communication, adaptation, classroom management, sequencing | teaching the students who are present, not the plan alone |
| Practising | a personal daily practice | teaching from experience; continued learning |
| Ethics | honesty, boundaries, respect, confidentiality | safe, trustworthy relationships (@ch:ethics) |

## How adults learn

Most yoga students are adults, and adults learn differently from children. Theories of adult learning emphasise that adults [@knowles2015]:

- **need to know why** they are learning something;
- **bring experience** that is a resource — and sometimes an obstacle;
- are **self-directed** and resent being treated as passive;
- learn best when the material is **relevant** to their lives and problems;
- are motivated mostly **internally** — by growth, health, meaning.

Motor-learning research adds that learners improve faster with **external-focus cues**, **choice** and a sense of **competence** (@ch:nervous-system) [@wulf2016].

::: keyconcept Teach the person in front of you
Every principle above points the same way: good teaching starts from the student’s experience, explains why, offers choice, and builds confidence. The fixed sequence of the classical class is a strength — but within it, every instruction is adapted to the people on the mats.
:::

## The teacher’s own practice

A teacher’s practice is not a private hobby; it is the source of their teaching. The tradition taught here expects its teachers to maintain a daily practice of āsana, prāṇāyāma and meditation, study and service. In practical terms, a teacher’s practice:

- keeps the teacher’s knowledge embodied — you understand a posture from the inside;
- reveals what students experience, especially when you practise as a beginner (modified postures, new styles);
- sustains the energy and steadiness teaching requires;
- keeps the teacher humble — there is always more to learn.

## Common traps for new teachers

- **Teaching your own practice.** Sequencing the class you would enjoy rather than the class your students need.
- **Performing.** Demonstrating throughout the class; focusing on how you look rather than on what students need.
- **Over-talking.** Filling every silence with instruction or philosophy.
- **Correcting everything.** Giving five cues where one would do.
- **Imitating.** Adopting your teacher’s voice and phrases rather than finding your own.
- **Needing approval.** Measuring success by compliments and numbers.

::: tip Your first hundred classes
Expect your first hundred classes to feel awkward. Plan carefully, teach simply, ask for feedback, and keep notes after each class: *What worked? What would I change? What did I notice about the students?* Improvement comes from reflection, not from repetition alone.
:::

## A self-assessment

::: exercise Where are you now?
Rate yourself from 1 (beginning) to 5 (confident) in each area: personal practice; knowledge of the twelve postures; anatomy; safety and modifications; philosophy; clear verbal cueing; demonstration; observation; voice; sequencing; managing a group; comfort with silence; ethical boundaries. Choose your two lowest scores and write one concrete action for each for the coming week. Repeat the assessment at the end of the course.
:::

::: takeaways
- Good teachers bring being (presence, integrity), knowing (knowledge) and doing (skills), supported by practice and ethics.
- Adults learn best when they understand why, when their experience is respected, when they can choose and when they feel competent.
- A daily personal practice is the source of a teacher’s authenticity and understanding.
- New teachers commonly teach their own practice, perform, over-talk and over-correct; reflection after each class is the remedy.
:::

::: reflect
1. Describe your best yoga teacher in five words. Which of those qualities do you already have?
2. What does your teaching style risk becoming if you are not careful — performer, lecturer, drill sergeant, friend?
3. How will you protect your own practice when you are teaching many classes a week?
:::

::: practice
1. Teach a five-minute segment and ask your partner to note every time you gave an instruction without explaining why. Repeat, adding brief reasons.
2. Teach the same posture twice — once demonstrating throughout, once without demonstrating at all. Discuss the difference.
3. Interview an experienced teacher (at the school or elsewhere) about what they wish they had known in their first year.
:::
