## The inner instrument

Yoga and Vedānta analyse the mind as an “inner instrument” (*antaḥkaraṇa*) with four functions [[T]]:

Table: The fourfold inner instrument {#tab:antahkarana}

| Function | Sanskrit | Role | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mind | *manas* | receives sense impressions; doubts, wavers, weighs options | “Shall I get up for morning practice or not?” |
| Intellect | *buddhi* | decides, discriminates, judges | “I will get up — it matters to me.” |
| Ego | *ahaṃkāra* | the sense of “I” and “mine” that claims experiences | “*I* am the one who gets up early.” |
| Memory-store | *citta* | holds impressions and memories | remembering how good yesterday’s practice felt |

(In the Yoga Sūtra *citta* is used more broadly for the whole mind-field.) The model is not neurological; it describes how experience *feels* from the inside, and it gives teachers a vocabulary. When a student “can’t decide whether to come out of the pose”, that is manas; when they choose wisely, buddhi; when they cannot bear to be seen modifying, ahaṃkāra.

### Saṃskāras and vāsanās

Every experience leaves an impression (*saṃskāra*) in the mind. Impressions cluster into tendencies (*vāsanā*) that shape future perception and action. This is why the Yoga Sūtra insists on long, uninterrupted practice (1.14): meditation lays down impressions of stillness strong enough to counter the impressions of distraction [[T]]. Modern students recognise the idea immediately as a theory of habit.

## Two families of meditation

Across traditions, meditation methods fall broadly into two families, a distinction also used by researchers [@lutz2008]:

- **Focused attention** (*concentration*): attention is placed on one object — the breath, a mantra, a candle flame, a point in the body — and returned whenever it wanders. This is Patañjali’s *dhāraṇā* developing into *dhyāna*.
- **Open monitoring** (*mindfulness* or *choiceless awareness*): attention rests in awareness itself, noticing whatever arises — sensations, sounds, thoughts, emotions — without selecting or pursuing any of it. This resembles the witness practice of Vedānta.

The two support each other. Concentration steadies the mind enough to observe without being swept away; open awareness prevents concentration from becoming rigid.

## Mindfulness: origins and modern forms

The English word *mindfulness* was chosen by nineteenth-century translators for the Pāli *sati* (Sanskrit *smṛti*), which means “memory” or “remembering” — in practice, remembering to be present [[H]]. In the Buddhist *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, mindfulness is established on four foundations: the body, feelings (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral), states of mind, and mental objects. Classic introductions for modern readers include @hanh1975 and @gunaratana2011.

In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn developed **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)** at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center, an eight-week secular programme combining sitting meditation, body scan and gentle yoga [@kabatzinn1990]. MBSR and its offshoot, Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy, brought mindfulness into medicine and psychology and generated the large research literature discussed in @ch:meditation-practice.

::: note Mindfulness and the yoga tradition
Some teachers present mindfulness as a Buddhist import foreign to yoga; others present all meditation as “mindfulness”. Both are simplifications. Yoga and Buddhism developed in close dialogue, share much vocabulary (the Yoga Sūtra’s four attitudes of 1.33 are the Buddhist *brahmavihāras*) and differ in philosophy. It is accurate and respectful to name each practice’s origin.
:::

## Traditional guidance for meditation

Traditional teachers present meditation as the culmination of practice, with a strong emphasis on regularity and on **mantra**. Their practical advice, which echoes the Gītā’s instructions on the seat and posture of meditation (BG 6.10–14), can be summarised as follows [[T]]:

- **Place.** Keep a special, clean place for meditation; the atmosphere builds with use.
- **Time.** Practise at the same time daily; the hours around dawn (*brahma muhūrta*) and dusk are recommended.
- **Direction.** Face north or east (a traditional recommendation).
- **Posture.** Sit in a steady, comfortable cross-legged posture with the back, neck and head in a straight line.
- **Mind and breath.** Instruct the mind to be quiet for the period of practice; begin with deep breathing, then let the breath become slow and rhythmic.
- **Focal point.** Fix the mind on one point — the space between the eyebrows (*ājñā*) for intellectual temperaments, or the heart (*anāhata*) for emotional ones — and keep it there.
- **Object.** Use one object consistently: a personal mantra (*japa*), repeated mentally and coordinated with the breath, or an image.
- **Duration.** Start with twenty minutes and increase gradually; consistency matters more than length.
- **Letting go.** When the mind wanders, bring it back gently; do not fight thoughts.

This structure — same time, same place, same object, gradually increasing duration — is the backbone of a stable practice in any tradition.

::: keyconcept “Like a lamp in a windless place”
The Gītā’s image (6.19) is precise: the flame does not stop burning — the mind does not go dark — but it no longer flickers. Meditation is not blankness but steady, bright awareness.
:::

## Traditional and modern understandings

The traditional purpose of meditation is liberation — the stilling of the mind so that consciousness knows itself. Modern secular programmes aim at stress reduction, emotional regulation and wellbeing. The practices overlap heavily; the frameworks differ. A yoga teacher should be able to teach both honestly: offering a student with anxiety a simple breath-focused practice without spiritual claims, and offering a spiritually motivated student the full traditional framework.

::: evidence What meditation research does and does not show
Research suggests that regular meditation practice can produce modest improvements in anxiety, depression and perceived stress, and changes in attention and emotional regulation [[S]] [@goyal2014; @tang2015]. Many popular claims — about permanent brain changes, dramatic health effects or superiority over other treatments — go well beyond the evidence [@vandam2018]. A small but real proportion of meditators experience difficult effects such as anxiety or dissociation, particularly on intensive retreats [@lindahl2017; @britton2021].
:::

::: takeaways
- The yogic model divides the mind into manas, buddhi, ahaṃkāra and citta; saṃskāras and vāsanās explain how habits form and how practice changes them.
- Meditation methods fall into focused attention (concentration) and open monitoring (mindfulness), which support each other.
- Mindfulness translates the Buddhist *sati*; modern secular programmes such as MBSR date from 1979.
- Traditional guidance emphasises a regular time and place, a steady posture, rhythmic breath, one focal point and mantra.
- Research supports modest benefits; exaggerated claims should be avoided and possible difficulties recognised.
:::
