::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- state the origin, insertion and primary actions of the major muscles used in yoga;
- explain the role of each muscle in specific asanas, and whether it is typically lengthened or working;
- decide whether a student is more likely to need strengthening or lengthening of a muscle group, and how to offer each;
- correct common misconceptions about muscles taught in yoga classes;
- use the atlas as a reference when planning sequences.
:::

## How to use this atlas

Each card gives a muscle’s attachments and actions, followed by the information a yoga teacher actually needs: its role in practice, whether practice typically lengthens or strengthens it, the key asanas that involve it and a common misconception. The small figure beside each card shows the approximate location of the muscle on the manual’s figure, coloured **blue** where it is typically *working* and **terracotta** where it is typically *lengthening* in the postures named. These are location sketches, not dissections; consult @standring2021 or @moore2018 for detailed anatomical drawings, and @long2009 and @kaminoff2021 for yoga-oriented anatomical illustration.

!fig(hamstring_psoas)

Remember two principles from @ch:muscle-principles as you read. First, **no muscle acts alone**: every posture is produced by patterns of coordinated activity, and naming one muscle is always a simplification. Second, **“tight” is a sensation, not a diagnosis**: the feeling of tightness can reflect muscle length, stretch tolerance, protective tension, bone shape or fatigue.

## From muscles to movement

Table: Which muscle groups work and which lengthen in the twelve basic postures (simplified) {#tab:muscles-twelve}

| Posture | Mainly working | Mainly lengthening |
|---|---|---|
| Headstand | deltoid, serratus anterior, trapezius, rotator cuff, triceps (forearm press); trunk and hip muscles (balance); adductors | — (neck and shoulders loaded, not stretched) |
| Shoulderstand | back extensors and gluteals (to keep the body vertical); hip extensors; adductors | neck extensors, upper back, (pectorals with arms behind) |
| Plough | trunk muscles controlling the descent | hamstrings, back extensors, neck extensors |
| Fish | back extensors, rhomboids, triceps (elbow press) | pectorals, rectus abdominis, neck flexors, intercostals |
| Forward bend | quadriceps (knees straight), hip flexors (to fold actively) | hamstrings, gluteals, back extensors, calves |
| Cobra | back extensors, rhomboids, triceps (lightly) | rectus abdominis, hip flexors (mildly), pectorals |
| Locust | back extensors, gluteus maximus, hamstrings | hip flexors (mildly) |
| Bow | back extensors, gluteals, hamstrings (pulling feet into hands), rhomboids | quadriceps, hip flexors, rectus abdominis, pectorals, anterior deltoid |
| Half spinal twist | obliques (rotation), back extensors (upright spine) | obliques on the opposite side, deep hip rotators and gluteals of the top leg |
| Crow | serratus anterior, pectorals, triceps, forearm flexors, hip flexors, abdominals | wrist flexors (in extension) |
| Standing forward bend | quadriceps; hamstrings and back extensors eccentrically during descent | hamstrings, calves, back extensors |
| Triangle | obliques and quadratus lumborum of the lower side, gluteus medius, quadriceps | adductors, hamstrings of the front leg, lateral trunk of the upper side |

@ch:mapping gives the full asana–anatomy mapping tables for these and other postures.

::: takeaways
- The major muscles of yoga can be learned in six regions: trunk, hip, thigh, lower leg, shoulder and arm, and neck.
- For each, know the attachments and actions — and translate them into the postures in which the muscle works or lengthens.
- Many popular claims (the psoas storing emotions, twists detoxifying organs, stretching the IT band) have no scientific basis.
- The trapezius, serratus anterior and rotator cuff are central to safe weight-bearing and overhead postures.
- “Tight” describes a sensation with many possible causes; teach people, not muscles.
:::

::: practice
1. Choose six muscles from the atlas. For each, demonstrate one posture in which it works and one in which it lengthens.
2. In pairs, palpate (with consent) the belly of the quadriceps, hamstrings, gluteus maximus and triceps while your partner contracts and relaxes each.
3. Rewrite three cues from your own classes that name a muscle into cues that name an action or image instead, and compare how students respond.
:::

::: assignment
Choose one posture from the twelve. Using the atlas, prepare a “muscle map” of it: list the muscles working and lengthening, the type of contraction of each, and one cue that helps each key muscle do its job. Present it in three minutes, then teach the posture using only your three best cues.
:::
