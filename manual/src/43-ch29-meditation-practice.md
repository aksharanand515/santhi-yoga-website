::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- teach the fundamentals of a meditation practice: posture, place, time, attitude and duration;
- guide breath meditation, mantra meditation, trāṭaka, body scan and open-awareness (witnessing) practices;
- guide a structured meditation with appropriate language and silence;
- help students with common obstacles, including sleepiness, restlessness, doubt and difficult experiences;
- support students in establishing a daily practice, and teach meditation safely and ethically.
:::

## Fundamentals

Meditation practice rests on a few simple foundations, which the Sivananda tradition sums up as regularity of **place, time and practice** (@ch:mind).

### Posture

The aim is a posture in which the spine is upright and the body can remain still and comfortable for the whole session. Options, in order of increasing demand:

- sitting on a chair, feet flat, back away from the backrest or lightly supported;
- kneeling (vajrāsana) on a cushion or meditation bench;
- sitting cross-legged (sukhāsana) on a firm cushion high enough that the knees are at or below the hips;
- half lotus or siddhāsana; full lotus for those whose hips allow it without strain on the knees.

The hands rest on the knees (cin or jñāna mudrā) or in the lap (dhyāna mudrā). The head is balanced, chin slightly tucked; the eyes closed or softly lowered; the jaw, face and shoulders relaxed.

::: tip No one fails at posture
A student who meditates on a chair is not doing a lesser meditation. The *Bhagavad Gītā*’s instruction is a seat “neither too high nor too low” (6.11) and a body held steady (6.13); how a body achieves that varies. Discomfort that pulls attention every few seconds is the real obstacle — solve it with props.
:::

### Time and duration

Five to ten minutes daily is a better beginning than an hour once a week. Mornings (before the day’s activity) and evenings (before the evening meal) suit most people. The tradition regards the hours before dawn as especially favourable. Increase duration gradually — for example by five minutes a fortnight — towards twenty to thirty minutes.

### Attitude

- **Gentleness**: wandering is expected; return without self-criticism.
- **Consistency** over intensity.
- **Curiosity**: each session is different; there is no “good” or “bad” meditation.
- **Non-striving**: not chasing experiences, including calm.

## A structure for a meditation session

A clear structure helps beginners. A 20-minute guided session might be:

Table: A structure for a guided meditation session {#tab:med-structure}

| Minutes | Stage | Guidance |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–2 | Arriving | settle the posture; feel the contact with the floor or seat |
| 2–4 | Relaxing | scan and release the face, shoulders, hands, belly |
| 4–6 | Breath | a few slow, deep breaths, then let the breath find its own rhythm |
| 6–16 | Focus | the main practice (breath, mantra, trāṭaka image or witnessing) with minimal instruction and long silences |
| 16–18 | Widening | expand awareness to the whole body and surroundings |
| 18–20 | Closing | a few deeper breaths; move the fingers; open the eyes slowly; a moment of gratitude or dedication |

## Methods

### Breath meditation

**Technique.** Rest attention on the sensation of breathing at one place — the nostrils, the upper lip, the chest or the abdomen — without controlling the breath. When the mind wanders, notice where it went and return. Counting can help: count each exhalation from 1 to 10 and begin again at 1, or whenever the count is lost.

**Use.** The most universal method: requires no belief, is always available, and trains dhāraṇā directly.

### Mantra meditation (japa)

**Technique.** Repeat a mantra mentally, often coordinated with the breath — for example *Oṃ* on the exhalation, or *So* on the inhalation and *Ham* on the exhalation (*so’ham*, “I am That”). A mālā may be used to count repetitions (@ch:bandhas-mudras-mantra). When the mind wanders, return to the mantra.

**Use.** Central in the Sivananda tradition [@vishnudevananda1978]; gives a restless mind a steady, rhythmic object; can carry devotional meaning. Always translate and give context; offer a secular alternative (e.g. a word such as “peace”, or counting) for those who prefer it.

### Trāṭaka (steady gazing)

**Technique.** Place a candle at eye level about an arm’s length away in a dark, draught-free room. Gaze steadily at the flame, without straining, for 30 seconds to 2 minutes, blinking as little as comfortable. Then close the eyes and hold attention on the after-image at the space between the eyebrows until it fades. Repeat two or three times.

**Cautions.** Not for students with epilepsy triggered by flickering light, glaucoma, cataracts, recent eye surgery or eye infection, or those who find it distressing. Keep sessions short; never strain the eyes.

### Body scan and yoga nidrā

**Technique.** Move attention systematically through the body — toes to crown or the reverse — noticing sensation in each region without trying to change it. *Yoga nidrā* (“yogic sleep”), developed in the twentieth century by Swami Satyananda of the Bihar School, extends this into a structured practice with an intention (*saṅkalpa*), rotation of awareness through the body, breath awareness, opposite sensations and visualisation, practised lying down [@satyananda1976].

**Use.** Builds interoceptive awareness (@ch:nervous-system); deeply relaxing; a doorway for students who cannot sit still. It overlaps with the Sivananda final relaxation.

### Witnessing (open awareness)

**Technique.** After settling with the breath, let go of any single object. Rest as awareness itself, noticing whatever arises — sounds, sensations, thoughts, emotions — without following or pushing away. Labelling can help: silently note “thinking”, “hearing”, “feeling”, and return to open awareness.

**Use.** Corresponds to the witness (*sākṣī*) of Vedānta (@ch:vedanta) and to open-monitoring practices in research [@lutz2008]. Best introduced after some experience of concentration.

## Working with obstacles

Table: Common obstacles and practical responses {#tab:obstacles}

| Obstacle | Traditional name (YS 1.30) | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Sleepiness, dullness | *styāna*, *ālasya* | sit more upright; open the eyes slightly; meditate earlier in the day or after prāṇāyāma; shorter sessions |
| Restlessness, agitation | *vikṣepa* (distraction) | slow the breath; lengthen the exhalation; count breaths; walking meditation; mantra aloud |
| Doubt (“Am I doing it right?”) | *saṃśaya* | simple, clear instructions; remind that noticing wandering *is* the practice |
| Physical discomfort | *vyādhi* (illness) | adjust props; chair; shorter sessions; gentle movement before sitting |
| Craving experiences, disappointment | *bhrānti-darśana*, *alabdha-bhūmikatva* | drop expectations; practise for its own sake |
| Irregular practice | *anavasthitatva* | same time, same place, small duration; practice journal |
| Strong emotions or memories | — | open the eyes; feel the feet and hands; return to the breath or a neutral object; stop if needed and speak to the teacher |

!fig(attention)

::: safety When meditation is difficult
Most people find meditation calming, but a minority experience significant difficulties — anxiety, panic, dissociation, re-experiencing of traumatic memories, depersonalisation, disturbed sleep — especially with long or intensive practice [@lindahl2017; @britton2021]. Teachers should:

- keep sessions short for beginners and offer eyes-open and movement options;
- invite students to stop at any time and ground themselves (feel the feet, look around the room, name objects, breathe slowly with a long exhalation);
- avoid intensive retreat-style practice for students with active mental-health conditions unless they are under appropriate care;
- follow up individually with anyone who seems distressed; and
- refer to a mental-health professional when difficulties persist. Meditation is not a substitute for treatment.
:::

## Establishing a daily practice

Help students with concrete structures:

1. **Anchor** practice to an existing habit (after brushing the teeth; before breakfast).
2. **Start small** — five minutes — and keep it daily.
3. **Prepare the place** — a cushion, a corner, perhaps a candle or picture that marks the space.
4. **Use a timer**, so that attention is not on the clock.
5. **Keep a journal** of practice (Appendix J).
6. **Practise with others** when possible — a weekly group sustains a daily habit.
7. **Expect ups and downs**; missing a day is not failure — resume the next.

## Teaching meditation

### Language

- Use **invitational, present-tense** language: “Notice…”, “Let…”, “If you wish…”.
- Give **one instruction at a time**, then silence.
- Normalise wandering: “When you notice the mind has wandered, that noticing is a moment of awareness. Gently return.”
- Avoid promising experiences (“you will feel deep peace”) and avoid clinical claims (“this will cure your anxiety”).
- Keep your voice steady and warm, slower than normal speech.

### Silence

Beginning teachers talk too much. Plan silences and time them: 30 seconds, then a minute, then longer as the group settles. Silence is where the meditation happens.

### After the session

Invite brief sharing, especially with beginners: “What did you notice?” Receive all answers — boredom, sleepiness, restlessness, peace — with equal interest. Offer one practical tip, not a lecture.

::: cue A five-minute breath meditation
Sit comfortably, with the spine upright and the body at ease… Let the eyes close or rest gently on the floor… Feel the weight of the body, the contact with the ground… Now bring your attention to the breath at the nostrils — the cool air coming in, the warmer air going out… You don’t need to change the breath. Just feel it… *(silence, 30 seconds)*… When the mind wanders, as it will, notice where it has gone, and gently come back to the breath… *(silence, 1–2 minutes)*… Now let the attention widen to the whole body, breathing… *(silence)*… Take a slightly deeper breath… and when you are ready, open your eyes.
:::

::: evidence Meditation research: a brief summary
Meta-analyses of meditation programmes find moderate improvements in anxiety, depression and pain relative to no treatment, and effects comparable to other active treatments [@goyal2014]. Research identifies plausible mechanisms involving attention regulation, body awareness, emotion regulation and changes in self-perspective [@holzel2011; @tang2015]. Mind-wandering is common and associated with lower momentary happiness [@killingsworth2010]. Researchers warn against overstating findings, and note that adverse effects are under-reported [@vandam2018; @britton2021].
:::

::: takeaways
- A stable practice rests on a comfortable upright posture, a regular place and time, a short daily duration increased gradually, and a gentle, non-striving attitude.
- Core methods: breath meditation, mantra (japa), trāṭaka, body scan and yoga nidrā, and open awareness (witnessing).
- Obstacles listed by Patañjali — dullness, distraction, doubt, irregularity — have practical remedies.
- A minority of meditators have difficult experiences; teachers offer grounding, options, short sessions and referral.
- Teaching meditation relies on clear, invitational language and generous silence; claims should be modest.
:::
