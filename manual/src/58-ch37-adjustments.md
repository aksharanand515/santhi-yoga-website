::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- explain the purposes and risks of hands-on assistance in yoga;
- obtain, check and respect consent for touch at every stage;
- distinguish verbal, visual, prop-based and hands-on adjustments and choose between them;
- give safe, clear, appropriate hands-on assistance in the basic postures, using good body mechanics;
- recognise touch that is never appropriate.
:::

## Why touch — and why caution

Touch can be a powerful teaching tool. A light hand can show a student where to lengthen, reassure them in an unfamiliar position, or help them feel a part of the body they cannot sense. Touch is also one of the most frequently reported causes of yoga injury — especially when a teacher pushes a student deeper into a stretch [@fishman2009; @wiese2019] — and, when misused, a source of profound harm and violation [@remski2019]. Modern teaching has therefore moved from routine adjusting towards **assisting sparingly, with explicit consent, for a clear purpose**.

::: keyconcept Adjust or assist?
The word *adjustment* suggests the student is wrong and the teacher will fix them. *Assist* suggests the teacher is helping the student do what they are trying to do. The difference is not only in words: an assist follows the student’s movement and intention; it never overrides them.
:::

## The hierarchy of adjustments

Most corrections do not need touch. Work through this hierarchy, and use hands-on assistance only when the others will not do:

1. **Verbal cue to the group** (“everyone, knee over ankle”).
2. **Verbal cue to the individual**, quietly.
3. **Visual cue**: demonstrate, or point to where you want movement.
4. **Prop**: a block, strap, blanket or wall often solves the problem better than a hand.
5. **Hands-on assist**, with consent.

## Consent

Consent must be **informed, freely given, specific, ongoing and revocable**.

!fig(consent)

- **Ask at the start of class.** Explain that you sometimes offer hands-on assists, and offer an easy way to opt in or out: consent cards or chips placed at the front of the mat (turned one way for yes, the other for no), or a raised hand with eyes closed. Make “no” easy and without explanation.
- **Ask again before touching**: “May I place a hand on your back?” — and wait for a clear yes. Silence is not consent.
- **Tell them what you will do** and why.
- **Check during**: “How is that?” Stop immediately if the student says so, stiffens, holds their breath or seems uncertain.
- **Respect changes**: a student who said yes at the start may say no later, and vice versa.
- **Never assume** that previous consent or a friendly relationship implies consent now.

## Principles of safe touch

- **Purpose**: every touch has a clear teaching purpose that you could explain.
- **Areas**: use broad, neutral areas — shoulders, upper back, sacrum (with a flat hand), outer thighs, feet, hands. **Never touch** the chest, breasts, groin, buttocks or inner thighs.
- **Quality**: clear, steady, confident — not tentative, not heavy; use the flat of the hand; no stroking or lingering.
- **Direction**: guide (show direction) rather than push; never add force at the end of a student’s range.
- **Timing**: enter with the student’s breath; release slowly and clearly.
- **Your body**: stable stance, bent knees, spine neutral — you must be stable before you can steady anyone else.
- **Brevity**: a few breaths at most.

::: safety Never
- Never push a student deeper into a stretch, especially forward bends, twists and hip openers.
- Never adjust the neck or head in inversions, or lift a student into or out of headstand.
- Never use your body weight on a student’s back, pelvis or legs.
- Never touch without consent, or in a way you would not be comfortable explaining to the student, a colleague or a family member.
- Never adjust a student who has told you of an injury in that area, or a pregnant student, unless you are specifically trained.
:::

## Safe assists for the basic postures

Table: Examples of simple, safe assists (only with consent) {#tab:assists}

| Posture | Purpose | Assist | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tāḍāsana | grounding | hands lightly on the shoulders, drawing attention down | pressing down on the head |
| Child’s pose | release | flat hands on the lower back, following the exhalation, very light | pressing down with weight |
| Downward dog | length of the spine | hands on the sides of the pelvis guiding it up and back | pulling hard; pushing on the spine |
| Cobra | upper-back extension | a light hand between the shoulder blades inviting lift | pressing the pelvis down while the student pushes up |
| Seated forward bend | pelvic tilt | a hand on the lower back encouraging the pelvis to tilt forward | pushing the back down |
| Half spinal twist | rotation from the ribs | one hand in front of a shoulder, one behind the other, following the student’s turn | adding force at end range |
| Triangle | frontal-plane alignment | hands on the back of the pelvis and upper shoulder | pulling the upper arm |
| Headstand (at a wall) | safety | standing beside the student, hands ready near the hips or legs | lifting; standing in front |
| Śavāsana | release | adjusting a blanket; gently lengthening the legs from the heels (with prior consent) | touching the head or face |

## Hands-on in practice training

Learn assists by giving and receiving them in training, with feedback from the receiver. Always practise:

- asking and waiting for consent;
- stance and body mechanics;
- the lightest touch that communicates the intention;
- asking the receiver: *What did you feel? Was it clear? Was it comfortable?*

::: exercise The lightest touch
In pairs, one partner holds downward dog. The other, with consent, places the flat hands on the sides of the pelvis and guides it up and back — first with firm pressure, then with half the pressure, then with the lightest possible touch. Ask the receiver which communicated the direction most clearly. Most people choose the lightest.
:::

::: takeaways
- Touch can teach powerfully but is a common cause of injury and, when misused, of serious harm.
- Use the hierarchy: group cue, individual cue, visual cue, prop — hands-on only when needed.
- Consent is informed, freely given, specific, ongoing and revocable; ask at the start and before each touch.
- Safe touch has a clear purpose, uses neutral areas, guides rather than pushes, and never adds force at end range.
- Certain adjustments are never appropriate: pushing into stretches, adjusting the neck in inversions, using body weight, touching intimate areas.
:::
