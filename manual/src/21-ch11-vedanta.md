## What Vedānta is

*Vedānta* means “the end of the Veda”: the Upaniṣads, and the philosophy built on them. Its three foundational texts, the *prasthāna-traya* (“three points of departure”), are the **Upaniṣads**, the **Bhagavad Gītā** and the **Brahma Sūtras** (attributed to Bādarāyaṇa), a terse systematisation of Upaniṣadic teaching. Every Vedāntic school had to show that its reading of these three was coherent.

Table: Three schools of Vedānta {#tab:vedanta-schools}

| School | Founder (traditional dates) | Relation of self, world and brahman |
|---|---|---|
| **Advaita** (non-dualism) | Śaṅkara (c. 8th century) | the self is identical with brahman; the world of multiplicity is appearance |
| **Viśiṣṭādvaita** (qualified non-dualism) | Rāmānuja (11th–12th century) | souls and world are real, forming the “body” of brahman (God), on which they depend |
| **Dvaita** (dualism) | Madhva (13th century) | God, souls and world are eternally distinct; liberation is loving service of God |

Most modern teachers of “Vedānta” in yoga follow **Advaita**, while honouring devotion as a path. Śaṅkara, its greatest teacher, is traditionally said to have been born at Kaladi, on the Periyar river in central Kerala, and to have travelled the length of India before his early death [[T]]. Many works are attributed to him; scholars accept his commentaries on the Upaniṣads, Gītā and Brahma Sūtras as genuine and debate the authorship of others, such as the popular *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi* (“Crest-jewel of discrimination”) [[H]].

## Core ideas of Advaita

### Brahman and ātman

Brahman is described as ***sat-cit-ānanda***: being, consciousness and bliss. It is not an object that can be known, because it is the knower in every act of knowing. The Upaniṣadic method for pointing to it is negation — *neti, neti*, “not this, not this” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.3.6): whatever can be observed is not the observer. When everything observed has been set aside, what remains is the Self, *ātman*, which is not different from brahman.

### Superimposition and māyā

How, then, do we experience ourselves as limited individuals in a world of separate things? Śaṅkara’s answer is ***adhyāsa***, superimposition: the properties of one thing are mistakenly projected onto another. The classic example is the rope mistaken for a snake in dim light. The snake is not real — but the fear it causes is real, and it vanishes not when the snake is fought but when the rope is seen [[T]]. In the same way, the qualities of body and mind (“I am tired”, “I am anxious”) are superimposed on the Self, and the qualities of the Self (awareness, being) are lent to body and mind. ***Māyā*** is the power by which the one appears as many.

### Levels of reality

Advaita distinguishes three orders of reality [[I]]:

- **Absolute** (*pāramārthika*): brahman alone.
- **Empirical** (*vyāvahārika*): the everyday world, which is fully real for all practical purposes — the rope-snake does not apply to paying your rent.
- **Illusory** (*prātibhāsika*): dreams, mirages, the snake in the rope.

This distinction is often forgotten by enthusiastic students (“nothing matters, it is all māyā”). Advaita insists that ethics, duty and care for others remain binding at the empirical level, where we all live.

## The path of knowledge

Advaita teaches that liberation is not produced by action — it is the recognition of what already is. But the mind must be prepared for that recognition.

### The fourfold qualification

The student needs four qualifications (*sādhana-catuṣṭaya*), described in the *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi* and other works [@madhavananda1921]:

1. ***Viveka*** — discrimination between the eternal and the transient.
2. ***Vairāgya*** — dispassion towards the fruits of action, here and hereafter.
3. ***Ṣaṭ-sampatti***, the “six treasures”: *śama* (calm of mind), *dama* (restraint of the senses), *uparati* (withdrawal from distraction), *titikṣā* (forbearance), *śraddhā* (faith in teacher and teaching) and *samādhāna* (concentration).
4. ***Mumukṣutva*** — intense longing for liberation.

### Hearing, reflection, meditation

The method itself has three stages, from Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.5 (“the Self should be heard about, reflected upon and meditated upon”):

- ***Śravaṇa*** — hearing the teaching from a qualified teacher and the texts;
- ***Manana*** — reflecting on it until every doubt is resolved;
- ***Nididhyāsana*** — sustained contemplation until the understanding becomes direct and stable.

## Yoga and Vedānta compared

Table: Classical Yoga and Advaita Vedānta {#tab:yoga-vedanta}

| | Classical Yoga (Patañjali) | Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara) |
|---|---|---|
| Metaphysics | dualist: many puruṣas and one prakṛti | non-dualist: brahman alone is ultimately real |
| Cause of bondage | conjunction of seer and seen; avidyā | superimposition; avidyā |
| Means | eight limbs; stilling the mind (nirodha) | knowledge (jñāna) through hearing, reflection, meditation |
| Goal | *kaivalya*: aloneness of puruṣa | *mokṣa*: recognition that ātman is brahman |
| Role of samādhi | central | useful preparation; knowledge liberates |

Despite these differences, the two traditions have long been practised together. Vedāntins adopt the methods of Yoga to prepare the mind; many Yoga teachers adopt the non-dual vision of Vedānta. The phrase **Yoga Vedānta**, used by several modern teachers, expresses exactly this: the practices of yoga in the service of Vedāntic self-knowledge. In this framework, Haṭha and Rāja Yoga purify and steady the mind; Karma and Bhakti Yoga soften the ego; and Jñāna Yoga brings the final recognition.

::: keyconcept The witness
Both Yoga and Vedānta point to the **witness** (*sākṣī*): the awareness that observes sensations, thoughts and emotions without being any of them. The practical instruction “observe the breath” or “notice the thought” is the doorway to this teaching. You can offer the doorway to every student; what they find there is their own.
:::

::: tip Vedānta in a posture class
In final relaxation: “Notice that sensations come and go… thoughts come and go… and something in you notices them all. Rest as that.” Four sentences carry the heart of Vedānta without a single Sanskrit term. Offer it; do not insist.
:::

::: takeaways
- Vedānta rests on the Upaniṣads, the Bhagavad Gītā and the Brahma Sūtras; its main schools are Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita.
- Advaita teaches that the Self is brahman — being, consciousness, bliss — and that multiplicity arises through superimposition and māyā.
- Empirical reality remains fully binding in everyday life; the teaching is not an excuse to ignore ethics.
- The student is prepared by the fourfold qualification and liberated through hearing, reflection and meditation.
- “Yoga Vedānta” uses the practices of yoga to prepare the mind for Vedāntic self-knowledge.
:::
