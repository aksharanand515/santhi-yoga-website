::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- outline the main phases in the history of yoga, from the Vedic period to the present, with approximate dates;
- explain why claims about yoga in the Indus civilisation are contested;
- describe the contribution of the Upaniṣads, the śramaṇa movements, the epics, classical Yoga, tantra and Haṭha Yoga;
- explain how modern posture-based yoga developed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries;
- tell the history of yoga to students accurately, without either inflating or dismissing its antiquity.
:::

## Why history matters to a teacher

Students ask historical questions — “How old is yoga?”, “Did the ancient yogis do sun salutations?” — and many teachers answer them with myths. This matters for more than accuracy. A teacher who tells students that every posture is thousands of years old invites them to treat every instruction as sacred and unchangeable. A teacher who knows the real history can honour the ancient roots of yoga *and* explain why a posture may be adapted for a modern body. History, in other words, supports both reverence and good teaching.

Two warnings before we begin. First, **dates for early Indian texts are uncertain**: most were composed orally, transmitted for centuries before being written down, and edited over time. The dates below are the ranges most scholars currently accept, and they will be revised. Second, **the history of yoga is not one line**. Yoga developed in many streams — Brahmanical, Buddhist, Jain, Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava and others — which borrowed from and argued with one another.

## The Indus civilisation: a contested beginning

The mature phase of the Indus (Harappan) civilisation flourished in the north-west of the subcontinent from about 2600 to 1900 BCE [[H]]. Among thousands of small carved seals found at its cities, a few show a figure seated with the knees spread and the heels drawn together, sometimes with a horned headdress. When these seals were published in the 1920s and 1930s, the archaeologist John Marshall suggested the best-known of them depicted a “proto-Śiva” in a yogic posture. The idea has been repeated ever since.

Most scholars now treat this interpretation with great caution [@samuel2008]. The Indus script has not been deciphered, so we cannot read what the seals meant to the people who made them; the seated posture may represent many things; and there is a gap of more than a thousand years between the seals and the first texts that describe yoga. It is possible that practices ancestral to yoga existed in the Indus world. It is not demonstrated.

::: tip How to answer “How old is yoga?”
An honest answer runs like this: “The practices of disciplining the body, breath and mind that became yoga have roots going back at least three thousand years. The first texts that describe yoga clearly date from the last few centuries BCE. Many of the postures we practise today are much more recent — some only a century or two old.”
:::

## The Vedic period (c. 1500–500 BCE)

The **Vedas** are the oldest texts of the Indian tradition: collections of hymns, ritual formulae and speculation composed in an early form of Sanskrit. The oldest, the *Ṛgveda*, was composed roughly between 1500 and 1200 BCE [[H]] [@jamison2014]. The Vedic religion centred on *yajña*, the fire sacrifice, performed by priests with exact chanting.

The Vedas do not teach yoga in the later sense, but several threads that yoga would later weave together are already present:

- **Sacred sound.** Precise chanting, the power of the word (*vāc*) and the syllable *Oṃ* (prominent in later Vedic literature).
- **Tapas.** Literally “heat”, the ascetic effort by which seers and gods gain power and insight.
- **Ascetic figures.** A late hymn of the *Ṛgveda* (10.136) describes the *keśin*, a long-haired, silent sage (*muni*) who “flies with the wind” — an early image of an ecstatic ascetic.
- **The breath.** Vedic ritual speculation already treats *prāṇa*, breath, as a life-principle, and later Vedic texts begin to “internalise” the sacrifice: the fire is kindled within, and breathing itself becomes an offering.

## The śramaṇa movements and the Upaniṣads (c. 700–200 BCE)

In the middle of the first millennium BCE the Ganges plain saw the rise of towns, trade and a new religious type: the wandering renouncer, or **śramaṇa**, who left household life to seek liberation through austerity, meditation and insight. The Buddha (fifth century BCE on most estimates) and Mahāvīra, the Jain teacher, came from this milieu, and both taught systematic methods of meditation and ethical restraint [[H]] [@samuel2008]. Early Buddhist texts describe graded states of meditative absorption (*jhāna*) that closely parallel later yogic descriptions of concentration.

At the same time, Brahmanical thinkers composed the **Upaniṣads**, philosophical dialogues attached to the Vedas. The oldest — the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* and *Chāndogya* — probably predate the Buddha. They introduce ideas that became central to yoga: that the true Self (*ātman*) is identical with the ground of all being (*brahman*); that actions (*karma*) bind a person to rebirth; and that liberation comes through knowledge. Slightly later Upaniṣads describe yoga explicitly:

- The **Kaṭha Upaniṣad** gives the first clear definition of yoga as “the firm holding of the senses” (2.3.11) and the image of the Self as the lord of a chariot whose horses are the senses (1.3.3–9).
- The **Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad** instructs the practitioner to hold the body erect with chest, neck and head in line, to restrain the breath, and to practise in a clean, level place sheltered from wind (2.8–10). It lists the first signs of progress in yoga: lightness, health, steadiness, clear complexion, a pleasant voice (2.13) [@olivelle1996].
- The **Maitrī (Maitrāyaṇīya) Upaniṣad** lists a six-limbed yoga: breath control, withdrawal of the senses, meditation, concentration, reasoning (*tarka*) and absorption (6.18) — five of Patañjali’s later eight limbs, in a slightly different order.

## The epics and the Bhagavad Gītā (c. 400 BCE–400 CE)

The great Sanskrit epic, the **Mahābhārata**, grew over many centuries, roughly between 400 BCE and 400 CE [[H]]. Among its many teachings is a long section on liberation (*Mokṣadharma*) in which Sāṃkhya and Yoga appear as paired paths, and the **Bhagavad Gītā**, a dialogue of 700 verses between the warrior Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa on the battlefield. The Gītā broadened the meaning of yoga dramatically: alongside meditation it taught *karma yoga*, the yoga of action performed without attachment to its fruits, and *bhakti yoga*, the yoga of devotion (@ch:gita).

## Classical Yoga: Patañjali (c. 325–425 CE)

The *Yoga Sūtra* attributed to Patañjali is a compact collection of 195 or 196 aphorisms which systematised yoga as a philosophy of mind and a path to liberation. Traditionally the sūtras are studied together with a commentary (*bhāṣya*) attributed to Vyāsa. Philipp Maas has argued that sūtras and commentary form a single work, the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra*, composed around 325–425 CE [[H]] [@maas2013]; older estimates range more widely, from the second century BCE onwards. Patañjali’s yoga shares its metaphysics largely with **Sāṃkhya** and much of its meditative vocabulary with Buddhism. Chapters 5 to 8 study it in detail.

## Tantra (from c. 500 CE)

From around the middle of the first millennium CE, a large family of traditions now called **tantra** developed within Śaivism, Vaiṣṇavism, Buddhism and Jainism. Tantric texts introduced or elaborated much of what modern students think of as “yogic”: detailed maps of a subtle body with channels (*nāḍī*) and centres (*cakra*), the coiled power *kuṇḍalinī*, extensive use of *mantra*, visualisation of deities, and ritual gestures (*mudrā*) [[H]] [@samuel2008; @flood1996]. Tantra is often misunderstood in the West as primarily sexual; in the texts themselves, sexual ritual is a minor and often esoteric element within vast systems of ritual, meditation and philosophy.

## Haṭha Yoga (c. 1000–1800 CE)

**Haṭha Yoga** emerged in the early second millennium. The earliest text to teach its characteristic methods, the *Amṛtasiddhi*, dates from around the eleventh century and comes from a Buddhist tantric milieu; many later Haṭha texts are associated with the Nāth yogis, who trace their lineage to Matsyendra and Gorakṣa [[H]] [@mallinson2017]. The word *haṭha* means “force”, referring to the effortful methods of the practice; the popular explanation that *ha* means “sun” and *ṭha* “moon” appears only in later texts [@birch2011].

Haṭha Yoga’s distinctive contribution was to use the body — posture, breath retention, locks (*bandha*), seals (*mudrā*) and cleansing practices (*ṣaṭkarma*) — to control the vital energy and bring about the states of meditation earlier traditions pursued more directly. The classic compendium, Svātmārāma’s ***Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā*** (fifteenth century), gathered earlier teachings into four chapters; it describes fifteen postures. Later texts expanded the repertoire: the *Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā* (c. 1700) describes thirty-two āsanas, and an eighteenth-century text, the *Haṭhābhyāsapaddhati*, describes over a hundred, many of them strenuous and dynamic [@mallinson2017]. Chapter 9 studies this tradition.

::: history The 84 āsanas
The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* says that Śiva taught 84 postures (HYP 1.33), and some Haṭha texts add that there are as many postures as there are species of living beings — traditionally 8,400,000. The numbers are symbolic — 84 is an auspicious number in Indian tradition — and lists of “the 84 postures” differ from text to text. The idea is best understood as “there are countless postures, and a few are essential”, which is exactly how the *Pradīpikā* goes on to name four seated postures as the most important.
:::

## Yoga in the modern world (from c. 1850)

Modern yoga was shaped by encounters between Indian traditions and the colonial, scientific and global world.

- **Swami Vivekananda** (1863–1902) addressed the World’s Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893 and published *Raja Yoga* (1896), presenting Patañjali’s yoga as a universal science of mind. He was notably dismissive of Haṭha Yoga [@vivekananda1896; @demichelis2004].
- **Scientific research** on yoga began in India in the 1920s, notably at Kaivalyadhama, founded by Swami Kuvalayananda at Lonavla in 1924.
- **Physical culture.** In the early twentieth century Indian reformers, influenced in part by European gymnastics and physical-culture movements, developed exercise systems that drew on yogic postures. The Raja of Aundh popularised a systematic *sūrya namaskār* in the 1920s and 1930s, and **T. Krishnamacharya** (1888–1989) taught a dynamic posture practice at the Mysore palace in the 1930s whose students included B. K. S. Iyengar, K. Pattabhi Jois and his son T. K. V. Desikachar [@singleton2010].
- In 2014 the United Nations General Assembly proclaimed 21 June as the **International Day of Yoga**, first observed in 2015.

## Continuity and change

Historians such as Mark Singleton have shown that much of today’s posture practice — especially flowing sequences, standing postures and the dominance of āsana in a class — is a modern development, drawing on Haṭha Yoga but also on twentieth-century physical culture [@singleton2010]. Some practitioners find this unsettling. It need not be. The goals of the practice — steadiness of body, regulation of breath, one-pointed attention, freedom from suffering — are ancient. The forms in which those goals are pursued have always changed, and the *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* itself was a work of gathering and innovation. What matters for a teacher is to know which is which: to teach a modern posture as a modern posture, and an ancient teaching as an ancient teaching.

::: keyconcept Old aims, changing forms
Yoga’s aims — a steady body, a regulated breath, a focused mind and ultimately freedom — are ancient. Its forms have changed in every era. Teach both honestly.
:::

::: takeaways
- Early Indus seals are sometimes presented as evidence of yoga; the interpretation is speculative.
- Threads of yoga appear in the Vedas (sacred sound, tapas, breath); the first explicit teachings on yoga come in the Upaniṣads and early Buddhist and Jain texts.
- The Bhagavad Gītā broadened yoga to include action and devotion; Patañjali systematised it as a discipline of mind around 325–425 CE.
- Tantra contributed the subtle body, cakras, kuṇḍalinī and much mantra practice; Haṭha Yoga (from c. 1000 CE) made the body a primary instrument.
- Modern posture-based yoga developed in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, shaped by reformers, physical culture and teachers such as Krishnamacharya.
:::
