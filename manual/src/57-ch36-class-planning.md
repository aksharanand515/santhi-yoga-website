::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- plan a class from its intention and its students, not only from a list of postures;
- write a clear lesson plan with timings, sequence, cues, options and safety notes;
- plan a series of classes that progress over weeks;
- adapt a plan in the moment and reflect on it afterwards.
:::

## Planning begins with questions

Before choosing a single posture, answer five questions:

1. **Who** is coming? (Level, age, health, language, numbers.)
2. **What** is the setting? (Time of day, length, temperature, space, props, noise.)
3. **Why** — what is the intention of this class? (A foundation class; strength; calm; a peak pose; a philosophical theme.)
4. **What** will students do? (Sequence, breathing, relaxation, meditation.)
5. **How** will I know it worked? (What do I hope students will feel, understand or be able to do?)

## The shape of a lesson plan

A lesson plan is a tool for thinking and a safety net while teaching. It should be clear enough to glance at mid-class.

Table: Elements of a lesson plan {#tab:plan-elements}

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Header | date, time, length, place, expected students, level |
| Intention | one sentence: the purpose of the class |
| Theme (optional) | a teaching point, quality or idea woven through |
| Sequence | sections with timings; postures in order; breath; holds; repetitions |
| Key cues | the two or three most important cues for each main posture |
| Options | modifications and variations; props needed |
| Safety notes | contraindications to screen for; postures needing particular care |
| Transitions | how students move between postures and sections |
| Closing | relaxation script outline; closing words |
| Reflection | space to write after class |

## Planning a series

Students learn best when classes build on one another. A four- or six-week series might:

- introduce one element of the classical class each week (for beginners);
- work towards a peak pose over several weeks;
- follow a theme — the five points, the eight limbs, the koshas — one aspect per week.

Each class in a series should stand on its own for students who miss a week.

## In the moment

No plan survives the first five minutes unchanged. Adapt when:

- the students differ from what you expected;
- the room is too hot, crowded or noisy;
- students look tired, rushed or restless;
- something is not working — a cue, a sequence, a pace.

Adaptation is a skill, not a failure. Have spare postures in both directions (easier and harder), and be ready to shorten sections rather than rush.

::: tip Time keeping
Put timings in your plan and a clock where you can see it without looking at your wrist. The most common timing error is running over in the postures and cutting the final relaxation. Protect the relaxation first.
:::

## After class: reflection

Write a few lines immediately after each class. Over months these notes become your most valuable teaching resource. Useful prompts:

- What was the best moment? Why?
- Where did students look confused, strained or bored?
- Which cue worked best? Which didn’t?
- Did the timing work?
- What will I do differently next time?

::: takeaways
- Plan from the students, setting and intention, not only from postures.
- A lesson plan includes intention, sequence with timings, key cues, options, safety notes, transitions and closing.
- Series of classes should progress while each class stands alone.
- Adapting the plan in the moment is a skill; protect the final relaxation.
- Brief written reflection after every class accelerates improvement.
:::

::: reflect
1. What is the intention behind the class you most like to practise?
2. When have you seen a teacher adapt well to an unexpected class?
3. What will your reflection routine be after teaching?
:::

::: practice
1. Write a 60-minute lesson plan using the template, and exchange it with a partner for feedback.
2. Take your partner’s plan and teach 15 minutes of it exactly as written; discuss what was unclear.
3. Adapt your plan in two minutes for a class in which half the students are complete beginners.
:::
