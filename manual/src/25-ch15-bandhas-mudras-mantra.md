## Bandhas: the locks

A ***bandha*** (“lock”, “bond”) is a sustained muscular contraction that, in the traditional understanding, seals or directs the flow of prāṇa [[T]]. The Haṭha texts teach three principal bandhas, usually combined with breath retention (*kumbhaka*), and a fourth, *mahā bandha*, in which all three are held together.

!fig(bandhas)

### Jālandhara bandha — the chin lock

**Technique.** Sitting in a steady cross-legged posture with hands on knees, inhale; lift the sternum slightly, lengthen the back of the neck and lower the chin towards the notch between the collarbones, without forcing. Hold for the duration of the retention. To release, lift the head *before* exhaling.

**Physical action.** Flexion of the cervical spine with elevation of the chest, compressing the front of the throat. **Traditional purpose:** to prevent prāṇa escaping upward during retention and to prevent pressure rising into the head (HYP 3.70–73) [[T]].

### Uḍḍīyāna bandha — the abdominal lock

**Technique.** Standing with knees bent and hands on thighs, or sitting: exhale completely. Holding the breath out, close the throat and make a “mock inhalation” — expand the ribs as if breathing in, but let no air enter. The abdomen is drawn in and up under the rib cage. Hold for a few seconds only, release the abdomen, and then inhale gently.

**Physical action.** With the glottis closed after full exhalation, the expansion of the rib cage lowers the pressure inside the chest; the diaphragm, relaxed, is drawn upward and the abdominal wall is sucked inward. **Traditional purpose:** *uḍḍīyāna* means “flying up”; the text says it causes prāṇa to “fly up” through suṣumṇā (HYP 3.55) [[T]].

::: safety Uḍḍīyāna bandha
Practise only on an **empty stomach**. Commonly advised contraindications [[C]]: pregnancy; high blood pressure or heart disease; hernia; recent abdominal or chest surgery; peptic ulcer or other active abdominal conditions; glaucoma and conditions aggravated by straining. Many traditional teachers also advise against it during menstruation. Never practise it after an inhalation, and never combine it with vigorous breathing before the course has taught you both.
:::

### Mūla bandha — the root lock

**Technique.** Sitting steadily, draw up the muscles of the pelvic floor — the area between the anus and genitals — as if gently stopping the flow of urine and wind at the same time, and hold. The contraction should be moderate and should not tense the buttocks, abdomen or face.

**Physical action.** Voluntary contraction of the pelvic-floor muscles (principally levator ani). **Traditional purpose:** to draw apāna upward to unite with prāṇa, awakening kuṇḍalinī (HYP 3.61–64) [[T]].

::: evidence Pelvic floor and bandhas
The pelvic floor muscles support the pelvic organs and work together with the diaphragm and deep abdominal muscles in regulating intra-abdominal pressure [[S]] [@hodges2000]. Pelvic-floor muscle training is an established physiotherapy approach for some continence problems, but that is a specific clinical protocol; mūla bandha as taught in yoga has not itself been tested as a treatment. Some people have pelvic floors that are *overactive* (too tense) rather than weak, and for them repeated strong contraction may not help. Teach mūla bandha gently, and refer students with pelvic pain or continence problems to a pelvic-health physiotherapist.
:::

### Mahā bandha

With the breath held out after exhalation, all three locks are applied together, held briefly and released in reverse order (jālandhara last) before inhaling (HYP 3.19–25). This is an advanced practice, introduced only after each lock is secure.

## Mudrās: seals and gestures

*Mudrā* means “seal” or “gesture”. The word covers two quite different things:

- **Haṭha mudrās** — whole-body techniques combining posture, breath and locks, such as the ten in HYP chapter 3 (@ch:hatha);
- **Hasta mudrās** — hand gestures used in meditation, prāṇāyāma, ritual and Indian classical dance.

Table: Mudrās used in a classical-style class {#tab:mudras}

| Mudrā | How it is formed | When it is used | Traditional meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Cin mudrā* | tip of index finger touches tip of thumb; other fingers extended; palms down on knees | meditation, prāṇāyāma | the individual self (index finger) united with the universal (thumb); palms down: grounding |
| *Jñāna mudrā* | same finger position, palms facing up | meditation | receptivity; knowledge |
| *Viṣṇu mudrā* | right hand: index and middle fingers folded into the palm; thumb and ring (and little) fingers extended | alternate-nostril breathing | a practical hand position for closing each nostril |
| *Añjali mudrā* | palms pressed together at the heart | greeting, prayer, *namaste* | reverence; the meeting of opposites |
| *Dhyāna mudrā* | hands resting in the lap, right on left, palms up, thumb tips touching | seated meditation | stillness, receptivity |
| *Ṣaṇmukhī mudrā* | thumbs close the ears, index fingers rest on the eyelids, middle fingers beside the nostrils, ring and little fingers above and below the lips | pratyāhāra; listening to inner sound | “closing the six gates” of the senses |
| *Viparīta karaṇī* | inverted posture with hips supported (@ch:twelve-asanas) | as a Haṭha mudrā | “reversing” — traditionally said to preserve vitality |

::: myth “This mudrā treats your thyroid.”
Books and websites sometimes claim that particular hand mudrās cure specific diseases. There is no scientific evidence for such claims [[S]]. Hand mudrās are traditional aids to attention, feeling and devotion; they can be valuable exactly as that. Teach their traditional meaning — and do not make health promises.
:::

## Mantra

### What mantra is

A ***mantra*** is a sacred sound, syllable, word or verse, repeated as a meditative or devotional practice. A traditional etymology divides the word into *man* (“to think”) and *tra* (“instrument”, “protection”): a mantra is an instrument of the mind, or that which protects the one who reflects on it [[I]]. The tradition teaches that the sound itself has power, which is why Sanskrit mantras are preserved in their original form rather than translated [[T]].

### Oṃ

*Oṃ* (also written *AUM*) is the most important mantra, the *praṇava*. The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad analyses it into three sounds and silence (@fig:om); Patañjali calls it the expression of Īśvara and recommends its repetition with reflection on its meaning (YS 1.27–28). Chanting Oṃ at the start and end of class is a tradition in almost every school of yoga.

::: tip Teaching Oṃ
Divide it into three parts: *A* (open mouth, sound felt in the abdomen), *U* (lips rounding, sound in the chest), *M* (lips closed, a hum felt in the head), followed by silence. A common proportion is roughly 1 : 1 : 2, with the humming *M* longest. Let students listen first, and invite rather than require them to join.
:::

### Kinds of mantra

- ***Bīja*** (seed) **mantras** — single syllables such as *oṃ*, *hrīṃ*, or the elemental bījas of the cakras (*laṃ, vaṃ, raṃ, yaṃ, haṃ*).
- ***Saguṇa*** **mantras** — invoking a personal form of the divine, e.g. *Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya*, *Oṃ Namo Nārāyaṇāya*, *Oṃ Namo Bhagavate Vāsudevāya*.
- ***Nirguṇa*** **mantras** — pointing to the formless absolute, e.g. *Soham* (“I am That”) or *Oṃ* alone.
- **Vedic verses** — such as the Gāyatrī (@ch:vedas-upanishads) and the *Mahāmṛtyuñjaya* mantra (Ṛgveda 7.59.12), traditionally chanted for healing and protection.
- **Peace chants** (*śānti pāṭha*) — such as *Oṃ saha nāvavatu* and *Asato mā sad gamaya*.

### Japa

***Japa*** is the repetition of a mantra. The tradition describes three modes [[T]]:

1. ***Vaikharī*** — aloud; easiest for a restless mind.
2. ***Upāṃśu*** — whispered or with moving lips.
3. ***Mānasika*** — mental; the subtlest and traditionally the most powerful.

A **mālā** of 108 beads, with a larger *meru* bead that is not crossed, is used to count repetitions: the beads are moved with the thumb and middle finger, and at the meru the mālā is turned back. Traditionally a personal mantra is given at initiation (*mantra dīkṣā*) by a teacher; students without initiation can practise with Oṃ or a universal mantra [[T]].

::: evidence Chanting and breathing
In a small experimental study, reciting the Ave Maria in Latin and a yoga mantra (*Om mani padme om*) both slowed breathing to around six breaths per minute and increased cardiovascular rhythms associated with baroreflex sensitivity [[S]] [@bernardi2001]. This suggests one physiological pathway by which rhythmic chanting may be calming: it structures and slows the breath. Humming, as in *M* of Oṃ and in bhrāmarī, also greatly increases nasal nitric oxide levels [@weitzberg2002], though the practical significance of this is not established.
:::

## Mantra, mudrā and inclusion

Chanting and devotional gestures are among the most culturally rich parts of yoga — and among the most sensitive. Some students love them; others feel they are being asked to participate in a religion not their own. Good practice:

- **Translate** what you chant, briefly, so no one repeats words they do not understand.
- **Invite** rather than require: “Join in if you wish, or simply listen.”
- **Pronounce** Sanskrit carefully; learning correct pronunciation is a form of respect.
- **Give context**: “This is a traditional Hindu chant for peace.”
- **Never** use sacred symbols or chants as decoration or as a sales device.

::: takeaways
- The three bandhas are the chin lock (jālandhara), the abdominal lock (uḍḍīyāna, only after exhalation on an empty stomach) and the root lock (mūla); together they form mahā bandha.
- Uḍḍīyāna bandha has important contraindications, including pregnancy, hypertension, heart disease and hernia.
- Hand mudrās are aids to attention and devotion; claims that they treat disease are unsupported.
- Mantra is a sacred sound repeated aloud, in a whisper or mentally (japa), often with a 108-bead mālā.
- Chanting slows and structures breathing, one plausible reason for its calming effect; offer it with translation and freedom to participate.
:::
