## A map of experience

Alongside the physical body, the yoga traditions describe a **subtle body** (*sūkṣma śarīra*) made of vital energy and mind. It is not an object that can be dissected; it is a map of experience used to guide practice [[T]]. Practitioners feel the breath “move” through the body, sense warmth or tingling in certain regions, notice that attention can be gathered at a point. The subtle-body maps give these experiences names and a structure, so that a teacher can say “take your awareness to the space between the eyebrows” and the student knows where to go.

::: keyconcept Two languages, both valuable
Anatomy describes the body from outside — what can be measured and dissected. The subtle-body teachings describe it from inside — what can be felt and directed with attention. Neither replaces the other. A cakra is not the solar plexus; the solar plexus is not a cakra. The teacher’s task is to use each language accurately and never to present one as the other.
:::

::: evidence What science says about subtle anatomy
No anatomical structures corresponding to nāḍīs or cakras have been identified [[S]]. Attempts to equate them with nerves, nerve plexuses or endocrine glands are modern interpretations, not findings, and the correspondences proposed differ from author to author [[I]]. This does not make the traditional maps useless: as maps of attention and felt experience, they have guided practitioners for centuries. It does mean that they should not be taught as physiology.
:::

## Three bodies and five sheaths

Vedānta describes three “bodies” (*śarīra*) [[T]]:

- The **gross body** (*sthūla śarīra*) — the physical body, made of food.
- The **subtle body** (*sūkṣma śarīra*) — vital energy, mind and intellect.
- The **causal body** (*kāraṇa śarīra*) — the seed-state of deep sleep and latent impressions.

The five sheaths (*pañca-kośa*) of the Taittirīya Upaniṣad map onto these: the food sheath is the gross body; the vital, mental and intellectual sheaths form the subtle body; and the bliss sheath is the causal body.

!fig(koshas)

Table: The five sheaths and practices associated with them {#tab:koshas}

| Kośa | Made of | Body | Practices that work with it (traditional view) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Annamaya* | food | gross | āsana, diet, cleansing |
| *Prāṇamaya* | vital energy, breath | subtle | prāṇāyāma, bandhas, mudrās |
| *Manomaya* | mind, emotion, senses | subtle | pratyāhāra, mantra, relaxation, devotion |
| *Vijñānamaya* | intellect, discernment | subtle | study, self-enquiry, meditation |
| *Ānandamaya* | bliss | causal | deep meditation; glimpsed in deep sleep |

## Prāṇa and the five vāyus

*Prāṇa* means both “breath” and the vital force of which breath is the most obvious expression. The Upaniṣads already treat it as the chief of the bodily faculties (@ch:vedas-upanishads). Later texts divide it into five functions, the **vāyus** (“winds”), each with a direction and a region [[T]]:

!fig(vayus)

Table: The five vāyus (placements vary between texts) {#tab:vayus}

| Vāyu | Region | Direction | Functions traditionally attributed |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Prāṇa* | chest, heart | inward, upward | inhalation, taking in (food, impressions) |
| *Apāna* | pelvis, lower abdomen | downward, outward | elimination, exhalation, childbirth, menstruation |
| *Samāna* | navel region | centring, balancing | digestion, assimilation |
| *Udāna* | throat, head | upward | speech, effort, growth; the departure of the life-force at death |
| *Vyāna* | whole body | outward, pervading | circulation, co-ordination |

Haṭha texts describe the union of prāṇa and apāna in the navel region, drawn together by the bandhas, as a key step in awakening kuṇḍalinī (@ch:bandhas-mudras-mantra). Five minor vāyus (*upaprāṇa*s) are also named in some texts, associated with belching, blinking, sneezing, yawning and the persistence of the body after death.

## The nāḍīs

*Nāḍī* means “channel” or “tube”. The texts say the body contains many thousands — the figure 72,000 is most often given, though some texts give other numbers — through which prāṇa flows [[T]]. Three are primary:

- ***Suṣumṇā***, the central channel, running from the base of the spine to the crown of the head.
- ***Iḍā***, on the left, associated with the moon, coolness, the left nostril and a calm, receptive quality.
- ***Piṅgalā***, on the right, associated with the sun, heat, the right nostril and an active quality.

Iḍā and piṅgalā are said to begin at the base of the spine and end at the left and right nostrils. In the traditional view, prāṇa usually flows through one of them at a time, which is experienced as the alternation of nostril dominance; when it flows equally, or enters suṣumṇā, the mind becomes still and meditation deepens. Alternate-nostril breathing (*anuloma viloma*, *nāḍī śodhana* — “purification of the channels”) is the principal practice for balancing them.

::: evidence The nasal cycle
It is well established that in most people the nostrils alternately congest and decongest over a period of hours — the **nasal cycle** — so that one nostril is usually more open than the other; in 24-hour recordings the dominant side switched roughly every two hours while awake [@kahanazweig2016] [[S]]. Traditional yogic observation of this alternation is accurate. Whether breathing through one nostril selectively affects the two halves of the brain or the branches of the autonomic nervous system is less clear: small studies report such effects, but results are inconsistent and have not been firmly established [@raghuraj2008; @saoji2019].
:::

## Kuṇḍalinī

In tantric and Haṭha teaching, a dormant power, ***kuṇḍalinī*** (“the coiled one”), lies at the base of the spine, coiled like a serpent and blocking the entrance to suṣumṇā. Through prāṇāyāma, bandhas and mudrās, and through devotion and meditation, she is awakened, pierces the cakras and unites with Śiva at the crown — the symbolic image of liberation [[T]]. The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* devotes much of its third chapter to this process.

::: safety Speaking about kuṇḍalinī
Traditional teachers treat kuṇḍalinī practices as advanced and potentially destabilising, to be undertaken only under experienced guidance. Some modern practitioners report intense physical and emotional experiences they attribute to kuṇḍalinī. In a general class: do not promise awakenings, do not teach forceful practices aimed at them, and if a student reports distressing experiences — sleeplessness, intense anxiety, unusual perceptions — reduce or stop intensive breath work and meditation and encourage them to seek appropriate support, including medical and mental-health care where needed (@ch:meditation-practice).
:::

## The cakras

*Cakra* means “wheel” or “circle”. In the subtle-body maps, cakras are centres along the central channel where nāḍīs converge, visualised as lotuses with a given number of petals, each associated with an element, a seed syllable (*bīja mantra*), a deity and qualities.

### A history of the cakra systems

The cakra system most students know — six cakras plus sahasrāra — is one of many [[H]]. Earlier tantric texts describe systems with four, five, six, nine, twelve or more centres, differently placed [@mallinson2017; @samuel2008]. The six-cakra system became dominant largely through the *Ṣaṭcakranirūpaṇa* (“Description of the six cakras”), written by Pūrṇānanda in 1577, which reached the English-speaking world in Arthur Avalon’s (Sir John Woodroffe’s) translation *The Serpent Power* (1919) [@avalon1919].

Several popular associations are much more recent. The rainbow sequence of colours (red at the base to violet at the crown) and the correspondence of each cakra with an endocrine gland are twentieth-century Western innovations, not found in the traditional texts [[H]]. So are many of the psychological meanings now attributed to each cakra (“the heart cakra is about love”, “the throat cakra is about self-expression”) — useful teaching metaphors, perhaps, but modern ones [[I]].

Table: The cakras as commonly taught, following the Ṣaṭcakranirūpaṇa {#tab:chakras}

| Cakra | Meaning of name | Location | Petals | Element | Bīja |
|---|---|---|---:|---|---|
| *Mūlādhāra* | root support | base of spine / perineum | 4 | earth | *laṃ* |
| *Svādhiṣṭhāna* | one’s own seat | genital region / sacrum | 6 | water | *vaṃ* |
| *Maṇipūra* | city of jewels | navel | 10 | fire | *raṃ* |
| *Anāhata* | unstruck (sound) | heart | 12 | air | *yaṃ* |
| *Viśuddha* | purified | throat | 16 | space (ether) | *haṃ* |
| *Ājñā* | command | between the eyebrows | 2 | (mind) | *oṃ* |
| *Sahasrāra* | thousand-petalled | crown of the head | 1000 | (beyond elements) | — |

### Using cakras in teaching

The most traditional and least problematic use of cakras is as **points of concentration**: “Bring your awareness to the space between the eyebrows” is a clear, safe instruction that does not depend on any belief. Chanting the bīja mantras while focusing on each region is a traditional practice many students enjoy. Claims that a posture “opens” or “balances” a particular cakra, or that cakras cause physical or psychological conditions, should be presented — if at all — as traditional or interpretive, never as fact.

::: tip A safe, honest cakra cue
“In the tradition, this region is associated with *maṇipūra*, the navel centre, linked to fire. Let your attention rest there and notice any warmth or movement of the breath.” This teaches the tradition, invites experience, and claims nothing.
:::

::: takeaways
- The subtle body is a traditional map of felt experience used to guide practice; it is not physical anatomy.
- Vedānta describes three bodies (gross, subtle, causal) and five sheaths (food, vital, mental, intellectual, bliss).
- Prāṇa is divided into five vāyus: prāṇa, apāna, samāna, udāna and vyāna, whose placements vary between texts.
- The three main nāḍīs are suṣumṇā, iḍā and piṅgalā; alternation of nostril dominance (the nasal cycle) is real, but lateralised brain or nervous-system effects are unproven.
- The popular six-cakra system derives mainly from a 1577 text; rainbow colours and gland correspondences are modern additions.
:::
