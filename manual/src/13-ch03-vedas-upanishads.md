## The Veda

*Veda* means “knowledge”. The Vedic literature is regarded in the Hindu tradition as *śruti*, “that which was heard” — revealed truth received by ancient seers (*ṛṣi*) — as distinct from *smṛti*, “that which is remembered”, the later body of epics, law books and Purāṇas composed by human authors [[T]]. There are four Vedas:

Table: The four Vedas {#tab:vedas}

| Veda | Contents | Relevance to yoga |
|---|---|---|
| *Ṛgveda* | about 1,000 hymns to the deities | oldest layer; Gāyatrī mantra; the *keśin* ascetic; speculation on origins (10.129) |
| *Sāmaveda* | Ṛgvedic verses set to melody for chanting | the tradition of sacred sound and chant |
| *Yajurveda* | ritual formulae for the sacrifice | *Kaṭha*, *Taittirīya* and *Śvetāśvatara* Upaniṣads belong to its branches |
| *Atharvaveda* | hymns and charms for healing, protection and daily life | early ideas on breath and the body; *Māṇḍūkya*, *Muṇḍaka*, *Praśna* Upaniṣads |

Each Veda grew in layers. The **Saṃhitā** is the core collection of hymns or formulae. The **Brāhmaṇas** explain the meaning and performance of ritual. The **Āraṇyakas**, “forest books”, reflect on ritual symbolically. The **Upaniṣads**, the final layer, are philosophical. For this reason they are also called *Vedānta*, “the end of the Veda” — both its concluding portion and, as the tradition understands it, its culmination.

::: verse Gāyatrī mantra · Ṛgveda 3.62.10
ॐ भूर्भुवः स्वः । तत्सवितुर्वरेण्यं भर्गो देवस्य धीमहि । धियो यो नः प्रचोदयात् ॥

*oṃ bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ · tat savitur vareṇyaṃ bhargo devasya dhīmahi · dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt*

We meditate on the excellent radiance of the divine Savitṛ (the sun as impeller); may it inspire our minds.
:::

The Gāyatrī is the most famous Vedic verse. The opening “oṃ bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ” (earth, atmosphere, heaven) is a later ritual prefix, not part of the Ṛgvedic verse itself. It is chanted at dawn and dusk by many practitioners and is often sung at the opening of traditional yoga classes.

## What the Upaniṣads are

The word *upaniṣad* is traditionally explained as *upa* (near), *ni* (down) and *ṣad* (sit) — “sitting down near” a teacher to receive secret teaching [[I]]. Śaṅkara, the great commentator, preferred to explain it as “that which destroys ignorance”. Both readings capture something true: the Upaniṣads are records of intimate dialogue between teacher and student about the deepest questions.

Over two hundred texts carry the name *Upaniṣad*, composed over more than two thousand years. A traditional canon lists 108. Scholars distinguish a group of about thirteen early or “principal” Upaniṣads, composed roughly between the seventh century BCE and the first centuries CE [[H]] [@olivelle1996]. These, together with the Bhagavad Gītā and the Brahma Sūtras, form the threefold foundation of Vedānta philosophy (@ch:vedanta).

## The central teachings

### Ātman and brahman

The Upaniṣads ask: what is the true Self? And what is the ultimate reality behind the changing world? Their answer — or rather, the answer of their most influential passages — is that these are the same. *Ātman*, the innermost Self, is *brahman*, the ground of all being [[T]]. Four short statements, the *mahāvākyas* or “great sayings”, became the classic formulations:

Table: The four mahāvākyas {#tab:mahavakya}

| Mahāvākya | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| *prajñānaṃ brahma* | Consciousness is brahman | Aitareya Up. 3.3 (Ṛgveda) |
| *ahaṃ brahmāsmi* | I am brahman | Bṛhadāraṇyaka Up. 1.4.10 (Yajurveda) |
| *tat tvam asi* | You are that | Chāndogya Up. 6.8.7 (Sāmaveda) |
| *ayam ātmā brahma* | This Self is brahman | Māṇḍūkya Up. 2 (Atharvaveda) |

In the *Chāndogya*, the sage Uddālaka teaches his son Śvetaketu this truth through a series of demonstrations: salt dissolved in water cannot be seen but can be tasted everywhere in it; so the subtle essence pervades all things, “and you are that” (*tat tvam asi*).

### Karma and rebirth

The *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* gives one of the earliest statements of the law of action: “As a person acts, as a person behaves, so does he become. Doing good, he becomes good; doing evil, he becomes evil” (4.4.5) [@olivelle1996]. Actions leave impressions; impressions shape desire; desire shapes further action and future birth. Liberation (*mokṣa*) is release from this cycle through knowledge of the Self.

### The breath as life

Many Upaniṣadic passages place *prāṇa* at the centre of life. In a famous story, the bodily faculties — speech, sight, hearing, mind and breath — quarrel over which is most important. Each leaves the body in turn, and the body carries on, impaired. When breath begins to leave, all the others are torn from their places and beg it to stay (*Bṛhadāraṇyaka* 6.1; *Chāndogya* 5.1) [@olivelle1996]. The story explains why yoga gives such weight to the breath: it is the faculty on which all others depend.

## Yoga in the Upaniṣads

### The chariot of the Kaṭha Upaniṣad

The *Kaṭha Upaniṣad* frames its teaching as a dialogue between a boy, Naciketas, and Yama, the lord of death. Offered every pleasure the world can give, Naciketas refuses them all and asks only what happens after death. Yama, impressed, teaches him about the Self. His central image is the chariot:

::: trad The chariot · Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.3.3–9
Know the Self as the rider in a chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know the intellect (*buddhi*) as the charioteer and the mind (*manas*) as the reins. The senses are the horses, and the objects of the senses the roads they run on. When the charioteer lacks understanding and the reins are slack, the senses are like wild horses. When the charioteer is wise and the reins held firm, the senses are like good horses — and the rider reaches the end of the journey.
:::

A little later the Upaniṣad defines yoga: “When the five perceptions are still, together with the mind, and the intellect does not stir — that, they say, is the highest state. This firm holding of the senses is what they consider yoga” (2.3.10–11) [@olivelle1996]. Every later definition of yoga stands in the shadow of this image.

### Instructions in the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad

The *Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad* gives the oldest practical instructions for yoga practice we have. It advises:

- holding the body steady with the three upper parts — chest, neck and head — erect (2.8);
- restraining the breath gently and breathing out through the nose when the breath is subdued (2.9);
- controlling the mind “as a wise man controls a wagon yoked to unruly horses” (2.9);
- practising in a level, clean place, free from pebbles, fire and gravel, pleasant to the mind and sheltered from wind (2.10).

It describes forms seen in meditation, such as mist, smoke, sun, wind and fire, and the first fruits of yoga: “lightness, health, freedom from desire, a clear complexion, a pleasant voice, a sweet smell and scanty excretions” (2.11–13) [@olivelle1996]. These are traditional statements of what practitioners experienced [[T]], and it is striking how closely the practical advice — erect posture, gentle breath, quiet surroundings — matches what a meditation teacher would advise today.

### The sheaths and the states of consciousness

Two further Upaniṣadic teachings are essential for teachers:

- The ***Taittirīya Upaniṣad*** describes the person as a series of sheaths (*kośa*) — made of food, breath, mind, understanding and bliss — each subtler than the last (2.1–5). This model is studied in @ch:subtle-body.
- The ***Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad***, only twelve verses long, analyses the syllable *Oṃ* as A-U-M plus silence, corresponding to waking, dreaming, deep sleep and *turīya*, “the fourth”, pure awareness underlying all three.

## Peace invocations

Each Upaniṣad is traditionally chanted with a peace invocation (*śānti pāṭha*). Two are widely used in yoga classes:

::: verse Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.3.28
असतो मा सद्गमय । तमसो मा ज्योतिर्गमय । मृत्योर्माऽमृतं गमय ॥

*asato mā sad gamaya · tamaso mā jyotir gamaya · mṛtyor mā’mṛtaṃ gamaya*

Lead me from the unreal to the real; lead me from darkness to light; lead me from death to immortality.
:::

::: verse Īśa Upaniṣad (invocation)
ॐ पूर्णमदः पूर्णमिदं पूर्णात् पूर्णमुदच्यते । पूर्णस्य पूर्णमादाय पूर्णमेवावशिष्यते ॥

*oṃ pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idaṃ pūrṇāt pūrṇam udacyate · pūrṇasya pūrṇam ādāya pūrṇam evāvaśiṣyate*

That is whole; this is whole. From the whole the whole arises. Taking the whole from the whole, the whole alone remains.
:::

The threefold *śānti* that closes these chants is traditionally explained as peace from three sources of disturbance: from within oneself (*ādhyātmika*), from other beings (*ādhibhautika*) and from forces beyond control (*ādhidaivika*) [[T]].

::: tip Using the Upaniṣads in class
A single line well chosen — “lead me from darkness to light” before relaxation, or the chariot image before prāṇāyāma — can give a class depth without turning it into a lecture. Always give the source, translate the Sanskrit, and never ask students to affirm a belief. Offer chanting as an invitation: “Join in if you wish, or simply listen.”
:::

::: takeaways
- The Veda has four collections, each in layers ending in the Upaniṣads, also called Vedānta.
- The central Upaniṣadic teaching is the identity of the Self (*ātman*) with ultimate reality (*brahman*), expressed in the four *mahāvākyas*.
- The Upaniṣads introduce karma and rebirth, the primacy of *prāṇa*, the five sheaths and the analysis of Oṃ.
- The Kaṭha Upaniṣad gives the chariot image and the first definition of yoga; the Śvetāśvatara gives the first practical instructions.
- Chants from the Upaniṣads can enrich a class when offered with translation, context and freedom to participate.
:::
