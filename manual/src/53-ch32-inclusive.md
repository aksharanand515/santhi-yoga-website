::: objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- identify barriers that exclude people from yoga and ways to reduce them;
- use language, demonstration and props that include students of different bodies, ages, abilities, genders, cultures and languages;
- explain the principles of a trauma-informed approach and apply them to a yoga class;
- adapt classes for students with common disabilities and chronic conditions within your scope;
- recognise when a student needs specialist support.
:::

## Who is not in the room?

Look at the images used to advertise yoga and you will see a narrow range of bodies — young, slim, flexible, able-bodied. Many people who would benefit from yoga assume it is not for them: larger people, older people, disabled people, people with chronic illness, men who feel stiff, people who cannot afford studio prices, people who fear being judged or touched. Inclusive teaching begins by asking who is *not* in the room, and why.

## Inclusive teaching in practice

### Language

- **Offer options without hierarchy.** Say “option one… option two…” rather than “the full pose” and “the easy version”. Let students choose without feeling demoted.
- **Describe actions, not ideal shapes.** “Reach the arms up” works for everyone; “touch your toes” excludes many.
- **Avoid body-shaming or diet language**: no “burn off last night’s dessert”, no “work off the belly”.
- **Avoid assumptions** about gender, family, religion, ability or lifestyle. Use students’ names and pronouns as they give them; avoid gendered group terms (“ladies”, “guys”) in favour of “everyone”.
- **Speak plainly** for international students: short sentences, common words, Sanskrit translated, key words demonstrated.

### Demonstration and props

- Demonstrate modifications as normal options, not as concessions; sometimes demonstrate the modified version first.
- Make props available to everyone, and use them yourself.
- Invite students to adapt freely: “If your body says something different, listen to your body.”

### The environment

- Accessible entry, toilets and practice space where possible; chairs available.
- A range of class times and prices, including concessions where feasible.
- A clear, friendly welcome for newcomers; information in advance about what to expect.

::: keyconcept Universal design
Rather than designing a class for an imagined “standard” student and then adapting it for others, **design every class from the start to include a range of bodies**: offer options, have props ready, give time for transitions. Adaptations then become part of the class for everyone rather than special treatment for a few.
:::

## Teaching students with disabilities and chronic conditions

A 200-hour training does not make you a yoga therapist, but you will meet students with a wide range of conditions. General principles:

- **Ask, don’t assume.** The student is the expert on their own body. “What works for you? What should I know?”
- **Work with their clinician’s advice**, within your scope.
- **Adapt the form, keep the purpose**: a chair-based sun salutation can serve the same aims as the standing one.
- **Know when to refer** to a specialist teacher or yoga therapist.

Table: Examples of common adaptations {#tab:adaptations}

| Situation | Adaptations |
|---|---|
| Wheelchair users, limited mobility | chair-based practice; upper-body and breath-focused sequences; transfers only when the student chooses |
| Visual impairment | precise verbal cues; describe the room; hands-on guidance only with consent; consistent layout |
| Hearing impairment | position so the student can see you; demonstrate; visual signals; written notes |
| Chronic pain or fatigue | shorter holds, more rest, gentler sequences; emphasise breath and relaxation |
| Arthritis | gentle movement through comfortable range; padding under joints; avoid sustained end-range weight-bearing |
| High blood pressure | avoid breath-holding and straining; keep the head above the heart for longer holds; gentle inversions only with medical approval |
| Larger bodies | wider stances; blocks and straps; space for the abdomen in forward bends (legs apart); side-lying options |

## Trauma-aware teaching

Many people carry the effects of trauma — experiences that overwhelmed their capacity to cope, from accidents and violence to war, abuse or medical procedures. You will not know who they are. Yoga can support people living with trauma: a randomised trial found that ten weeks of trauma-sensitive yoga reduced symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder in women with chronic, treatment-resistant PTSD more than a health-education control [@vanderkolk2014]. But yoga classes can also include triggers — being touched without warning, lying with eyes closed in a darkened room, a commanding voice, being watched from behind.

A **trauma-informed approach**, as described by the US Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, rests on six principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; and attention to cultural, historical and gender issues [@samhsa2014]. Trauma-sensitive yoga, as developed by @emerson2011, applies these through invitational language, choice, predictable structure, an emphasis on noticing sensation, and very careful (often no) hands-on assistance.

Table: Trauma-aware practices in a general class {#tab:trauma}

| Principle | In class |
|---|---|
| Safety | predictable structure; explain what will happen; clear view of the door; students choose their place; no surprises |
| Trustworthiness and transparency | say what you are doing and why; ask before touching and respect the answer |
| Choice | invitational language (“if you like”, “you might…”); options for every posture; eyes open or closed |
| Collaboration | the student is the authority on their own experience; invite feedback |
| Empowerment | emphasise noticing and choosing rather than achieving a shape |
| Cultural and gender awareness | awareness of how gender, culture and history shape students’ experience of touch, space and authority |

::: tip Invitational language
Compare: “Close your eyes. Relax completely.” with “You might like to close your eyes, or let your gaze rest softly on the floor. Notice what it’s like to rest here.” The second offers choice, invites noticing, and does not command. It is also simply better teaching for everyone.
:::

::: safety Scope and trauma
Trauma-aware teaching is a way of teaching everyone more safely. It is **not** trauma therapy. Do not invite students to recall or process traumatic experiences, do not interpret their reactions, and do not promise that yoga will heal trauma. If a student is distressed, help them feel grounded and safe (@ch:meditation-practice) and encourage them to seek professional support.
:::

::: takeaways
- Inclusion starts by asking who is not in the room and removing barriers of image, language, cost, access and assumption.
- Offer options without hierarchy; describe actions not ideal shapes; avoid body-shaming and assumptions; speak plainly.
- Design classes from the start for a range of bodies (universal design), with props and time for transitions.
- Ask students with disabilities or chronic conditions what works for them; adapt the form and keep the purpose; refer when needed.
- A trauma-informed approach emphasises safety, transparency, choice, collaboration and empowerment; it is good teaching, not therapy.
:::
