By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- teach śavāsana and a complete systematic relaxation, at the beginning, between postures and at the end of class;
- teach the twelve positions of the classical Sūrya Namaskār with correct breath, alignment and mantras;
- analyse the sun salutation anatomically and offer modifications for common limitations;
- teach single and double leg raises safely;
- explain why these practices precede the twelve basic postures.
:::

## The shape of the opening

A traditional class does not begin with effort. It begins lying down, with a few minutes of relaxation, followed by prāṇāyāma (@ch:pranayama), and only then moves into sun salutation and leg raises. This order is deliberate: the student arrives, the breath settles, the body warms gradually, and the abdominal and back muscles are prepared for the inversions that follow.

## Śavāsana: the relaxation posture

**Śavāsana** (“corpse posture”) is the most important posture in the class and, many teachers say, the hardest to master.

!sequence(savasana=Śavāsana/lying relaxed on the back)

**Technique.** Lie on the back with the feet a little more than hip-width apart and falling outward; the arms about 45 degrees from the body with the palms facing up; the head in line with the spine (a thin folded blanket under the head if the chin tilts up). Close the eyes. Let the breath become slow and natural, moving mostly in the abdomen.

**Modifications.** A bolster or rolled blanket under the knees relieves the lower back; a blanket over the body keeps students warm; students in later pregnancy, and anyone uncomfortable lying flat, rest on the side (usually the left) with a cushion between the knees; those who become anxious lying with the eyes closed may keep them open with a soft downward gaze.

### Relaxation between postures

After each posture the classical method returns to rest — śavāsana after supine and seated postures, and **makarāsana** (the crocodile: lying on the front, forehead resting on the stacked hands or with the cheek to one side) after prone backbends. A few breaths of rest let the heart rate and breath return towards resting levels and allow students to notice the effects of the posture — an essential part of the practice, not an interruption of it.

### Final relaxation

The final relaxation lasts ten to fifteen minutes in a 90-minute class. In the classical class taught here it has three stages:

1. **Tensing and relaxing** the body part by part: lift the right leg a little off the floor, tense it, hold, and let it drop; then the left leg; each arm; the buttocks; the chest (by filling it with air); the shoulders (drawn up and released); the face (squeezed and then opened wide). Gently roll the legs, arms and head from side to side.
2. **Autosuggestion**: the teacher guides attention slowly through the body from the feet upward, suggesting relaxation of each part in turn — “relax the toes… relax the soles of the feet…” — including the internal organs and the face.
3. **Relaxation of the mind**: attention rests on the breath, then on the awareness that observes body and mind. The student remains still in silence for several minutes.

To come out, deepen the breath, move the fingers and toes, stretch, roll to one side and come up slowly.

::: cue A short relaxation script (opening of class)
Lie down and let the body become heavy. Let the floor take your weight… Feel the back of the head, the shoulder blades, the backs of the hands, the backs of the heels… Let the breath come and go by itself… With each exhalation, let a little more tension go… Notice the belly rising as you breathe in, falling as you breathe out… There is nothing to do. Just rest, and breathe.
:::

::: safety Relaxation is not always relaxing
For some students — particularly those with trauma histories, anxiety or panic — lying still with the eyes closed can be uncomfortable or distressing. Always offer choices: eyes open or closed, lying on the back, side or with knees bent, or sitting. Keep your voice steady and your guidance concrete (@ch:inclusive). If a student becomes distressed, speak calmly, invite them to open the eyes, feel their feet or hands, and sit up if they wish.
:::

## Sūrya Namaskār: the sun salutation

The sun salutation is a flowing sequence of twelve positions, each coordinated with one breath. The form taught in the tradition taught here was widely popularised in the 1920s and 1930s, though salutations of the sun are of course far older as a devotional practice [@singleton2010] [[H]]. It warms the whole body, links breath and movement, flexes and extends the spine in alternation, and prepares the shoulders, hips and legs for the postures to come.

!fig(surya)

Table: The twelve positions of Sūrya Namaskār as taught in this course {#tab:surya}

| # | Position | Breath | Mantra (optional) | Key actions | Common modifications |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prayer (standing, palms together at the chest) | exhale | *Oṃ Mitrāya Namaḥ* | stand evenly; feet together or hip-width | feet apart for balance |
| 2 | Arms raised, gentle backbend | inhale | *Oṃ Ravaye Namaḥ* | reach up; lift the chest; hips slightly forward; arch from the upper back | arms shoulder-width; minimal backbend; hands on the lower back |
| 3 | Hands to feet (forward bend) | exhale | *Oṃ Sūryāya Namaḥ* | fold from the hips; hands beside the feet | bend the knees freely; hands on shins or blocks |
| 4 | Lunge: right leg back, knee down | inhale | *Oṃ Bhānave Namaḥ* | front knee over the ankle; hands beside the front foot; lift the chest and look forward or up | hands on blocks; back knee cushioned |
| 5 | Plank (both legs back) | retain the breath in, or breathe out | *Oṃ Khagāya Namaḥ* | one straight line from heels to head; hands under shoulders | knees on the floor |
| 6 | Knees, chest and chin to the floor | exhale | *Oṃ Pūṣṇe Namaḥ* | lower knees, then chest and chin; hips raised; elbows close to the body | lower the whole body to the floor |
| 7 | Cobra | inhale | *Oṃ Hiraṇyagarbhāya Namaḥ* | slide forward and up; elbows bent and close; shoulders down | low cobra; sphinx |
| 8 | Inverted V (downward dog) | exhale | *Oṃ Marīcaye Namaḥ* | press hips up and back; lengthen the spine; heels towards the floor | knees bent; heels lifted |
| 9 | Lunge: right foot forward, knee down | inhale | *Oṃ Ādityāya Namaḥ* | step the foot between the hands; chest lifted | help the foot forward with the hand |
| 10 | Hands to feet | exhale | *Oṃ Savitre Namaḥ* | bring the back foot forward; fold | knees bent |
| 11 | Arms raised, gentle backbend | inhale | *Oṃ Arkāya Namaḥ* | come up with a long spine, arms alongside the ears | bend the knees and come up with the back rounded, head last |
| 12 | Prayer | exhale | *Oṃ Bhāskarāya Namaḥ* | return to stillness | — |

The second half-round repeats the sequence with the **left** leg stepping back in position 4 and forward in position 9. Beginners start with two to four rounds at a slow pace and gradually increase; the classical class allows up to twelve rounds. The mantras are twelve names of the sun, one for each position; they may be chanted aloud, mentally, or omitted.

::: tip Pace and breath
The breath sets the pace, not the teacher’s count. If students are gasping, slow down or add a breath in positions 5 and 8. For beginners, “step” rather than “jump”, and move through positions 4–9 with an extra breath where needed.
:::

### What the sun salutation does, anatomically

The sequence moves the spine alternately into extension (positions 2, 4, 7, 9, 11) and flexion (3, 6, 8, 10); flexes and extends the hips (the back leg of the lunge extends the hip and lengthens the hip flexors; the forward bends flex it); loads the arms and shoulders in weight-bearing (5–8); and strengthens the trunk in plank. The upward flow of positions 1–2 and 11–12 involves shoulder flexion and scapular upward rotation. Repeated rounds raise heart rate moderately. It is therefore a whole-body warm-up — and, practised briskly for many rounds, a meaningful cardiovascular exercise.

## Leg raises

After the sun salutation, the classical class includes **leg raises** lying on the back, to strengthen the abdominal and hip-flexor muscles in preparation for headstand, shoulderstand and plough.

!sequence(savasana=Start/on the back, palms down; leg_raise=Single leg raise/inhale up, exhale down; double_leg_raise=Double leg raise/both legs together)

**Single leg raises.** Lie on the back with the legs together and the palms flat on the floor beside the body (or under the buttocks for support). Inhale and raise one leg as high as comfortable, keeping it straight and the other leg on the floor; exhale and lower it slowly. Alternate legs, several times each.

**Double leg raises.** Keeping the lower back in contact with the floor, inhale and raise both legs together to vertical; exhale and lower them slowly and with control. Repeat several times.

::: safety Leg raises and the lower back
When both legs are lowered, the hip flexors, including psoas, pull strongly on the pelvis and lumbar spine; if the abdominal muscles cannot hold the pelvis, the lower back arches off the floor. Beginners and students with low-back pain should raise one leg at a time, bend the knees, keep the hands under the buttocks, or lower the legs only as far as the back stays grounded. Leg raises are best avoided or modified in pregnancy and after recent abdominal surgery.
:::

::: takeaways
- The classical class opens with relaxation and prāṇāyāma before movement; śavāsana is taught with care at the beginning, between postures and at the end.
- Final relaxation has three stages: tensing and relaxing, autosuggestion through the body, and relaxation of the mind.
- The twelve positions of Sūrya Namaskār are each linked to a breath and, optionally, to one of twelve names of the sun; the stepping leg alternates between half-rounds.
- The sun salutation alternates spinal extension and flexion and warms the whole body; its modifications should be offered freely.
- Leg raises strengthen the abdomen and hip flexors; the lower back must remain supported.
:::
