## Context

The **Bhagavad Gītā**, “the Song of the Lord”, is a poem of 700 verses in eighteen chapters, forming part of the sixth book (*Bhīṣma Parvan*) of the *Mahābhārata*. It was probably composed between the second century BCE and the second century CE [[H]] [@flood1996]. Though technically *smṛti*, it has been treated in the Hindu tradition with the authority of an Upaniṣad, and it is one of the three foundational texts of Vedānta.

The setting is the eve of a catastrophic war between two branches of one royal family. Arjuna, the finest archer of his age, asks his charioteer Kṛṣṇa to drive between the armies. Seeing teachers, cousins and friends ranged against him, he is overcome: his bow slips from his hand and he refuses to fight. The Gītā is Kṛṣṇa’s reply. By its end Arjuna has recovered his clarity and resolve, and the poem has laid out one of the most comprehensive syntheses of Indian spiritual teaching.

::: note Reading the battlefield
Commentators have read the battlefield both literally and symbolically. Mahatma Gandhi, committed to non-violence, read the war as an allegory of the struggle within every person between higher and lower tendencies [[I]]. Others insist on the literal setting: the Gītā is a teaching on how to act rightly when every choice has painful consequences. Both readings are ancient in spirit, and both are useful for teachers.
:::

## Structure

Table: An overview of the eighteen chapters (titles vary between editions) {#tab:gita}

| Chapters | Theme | Key teachings |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Arjuna’s despair | the crisis that prompts the teaching |
| 2 | Knowledge and the Self | the eternal Self; duty; *yoga* as evenness (2.48) and skill in action (2.50); the person of steady wisdom (2.54–72) |
| 3–5 | Action | *karma yoga*; acting without attachment; action as offering; renunciation *in* action |
| 6 | Meditation | instructions for sitting, moderation, the restless mind (6.10–36) |
| 7–12 | Devotion and the divine | the nature of the Lord; the cosmic vision (ch. 11); *bhakti yoga*; qualities of the devotee (12.13–20) |
| 13–15 | Knowledge | the field and the knower of the field; the three guṇas (ch. 14); the cosmic tree (ch. 15) |
| 16–18 | Discernment and surrender | divine and demonic qualities; threefold faith, food, austerity and giving (ch. 17); renunciation and surrender (ch. 18) |

## Karma yoga: the yoga of action

The Gītā’s most distinctive teaching is that action itself can be yoga. Arjuna’s instinct is to withdraw — to renounce the world and avoid the harm of acting. Kṛṣṇa answers that no one can avoid action altogether, even for a moment (3.5), and that withdrawing from one’s duty is itself a choice with consequences. The solution is not to stop acting but to change the relationship to action:

::: verse Bhagavad Gītā 2.47
कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन ।

*karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana*

Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits.
:::

This is ***niṣkāma karma***, action without craving for results. It does not mean indifference to outcomes — Kṛṣṇa wants Arjuna to fight well — but freedom from the anxiety and pride that attach to success and failure. Hence the definitions of yoga in chapter 2: *samatvaṃ yoga ucyate*, “evenness of mind is called yoga” (2.48), and *yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam*, “yoga is skill in action” (2.50). The Gītā also teaches that each person should follow their own path of duty (*svadharma*): “Better one’s own duty, even imperfectly performed, than the duty of another well performed” (3.35) [@easwaran2007].

::: tip Karma yoga for teachers
Teaching is karma yoga in its purest modern form. You prepare carefully, teach with full attention — and then let go of whether students praise you, return or perform the poses beautifully. Teachers who teach for applause burn out; teachers who teach as an offering tend to last.
:::

## Jñāna yoga: the yoga of knowledge

In chapter 2 Kṛṣṇa begins not with action but with the nature of the Self: it is not born and does not die; weapons do not cut it, fire does not burn it (2.20–23). Wisdom is to know oneself as this unchanging awareness rather than as the body and mind that change. Chapter 13 develops this as the distinction between the *field* (*kṣetra*: body, mind and all that can be known) and the *knower of the field* (*kṣetrajña*). Jñāna yoga is the path of discrimination between the two (@ch:vedanta).

### The person of steady wisdom

At the end of chapter 2, Arjuna asks what a person established in wisdom looks like — how they speak, sit and walk. Kṛṣṇa’s answer (2.54–72) is one of the great portraits of the goal of yoga: one who is not shaken by sorrow nor elated by pleasure, free from craving, fear and anger; who can withdraw the senses from their objects “as a tortoise draws in its limbs” (2.58) — an image of *pratyāhāra* centuries before Patañjali.

## Dhyāna yoga: meditation

Chapter 6 contains detailed instructions for meditation that remain practical today [@easwaran2007]:

- Sit in a clean place, on a seat neither too high nor too low (6.11).
- Hold the body, head and neck erect and still, gazing as if at the tip of the nose, not looking around (6.13).
- Practise **moderation**: “Yoga is not for one who eats too much, nor for one who eats too little; not for one who sleeps too much, nor for one who stays awake too long” (6.16). It is for one moderate in food, recreation, effort, sleep and waking (6.17).
- When the mind wanders, bring it back again and again (6.26).

Arjuna objects that the mind is as restless and hard to hold as the wind (6.34). Kṛṣṇa agrees — and gives the answer Patañjali would also give: through **practice** (*abhyāsa*) and **detachment** (*vairāgya*) it can be held (6.35).

## Bhakti yoga: the yoga of devotion

From chapter 7 onward the Gītā turns increasingly to devotion. Kṛṣṇa reveals himself as the Lord present in all beings, and in chapter 11 grants Arjuna a terrifying vision of his universal form. The path of love and surrender is presented as the most accessible: whatever is offered with devotion — “a leaf, a flower, a fruit, water” — is accepted (9.26). Chapter 12 describes the devotee dear to the Lord: without hatred, friendly and compassionate, free from possessiveness and ego, even-minded in pain and pleasure, forgiving (12.13–20). The Gītā closes with the call to surrender: “Abandoning all duties, take refuge in me alone” (18.66).

## The three guṇas

The Gītā adopts from Sāṃkhya the teaching that all of nature is composed of three qualities, the **guṇas**: *sattva* (clarity, harmony), *rajas* (activity, passion) and *tamas* (inertia, darkness) (ch. 14). Every experience, food and activity is a mixture of the three. Chapter 17 applies this to daily life, and it remains the basis for the yogic approach to diet and lifestyle taught in the tradition taught here.

!fig(gunas)

Table: The guṇas applied to food, practice and teaching (after Bhagavad Gītā 17) {#tab:gunas}

| | Sattva | Rajas | Tamas |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Food** (17.8–10) | fresh, wholesome, nourishing, agreeable; promotes life, strength, health | very bitter, sour, salty, hot, pungent, dry; causes discomfort | stale, tasteless, spoiled, leftover, impure |
| **Practice** (interpretation) | steady, attentive, breath-led, ending in rest | competitive, rushed, pushing into pain | sluggish, distracted, habitual |
| **Teaching** (interpretation) | clear, calm, responsive to students | performing, showing off, over-talking | unprepared, disengaged |

The first row follows the text [[T]]; the second and third are applications, not quotations [[I]]. The Gītā’s ultimate teaching goes beyond cultivating sattva: the liberated person transcends all three guṇas (14.19–26).

::: keyconcept The Gītā’s synthesis
The Gītā does not ask you to choose one path. Action, knowledge, meditation and devotion are presented as mutually supporting — the foundation of the integrated practice of the four paths (@ch:four-paths).
:::

::: takeaways
- The Bhagavad Gītā is a 700-verse dialogue within the Mahābhārata, composed around the turn of the Common Era.
- It teaches action without attachment to results (*niṣkāma karma*) and defines yoga as evenness of mind and skill in action.
- It presents the paths of knowledge, meditation and devotion alongside action, and portrays the goal in the *sthitaprajña*.
- Chapter 6 gives practical meditation instructions and the principle of moderation; practice and detachment steady the mind.
- The three guṇas provide a framework for diet, lifestyle and the quality of practice and teaching.
:::

2. Teach a five-minute seated meditation using only the instructions in Gītā 6.11–13 and 6.26, in your own words.
