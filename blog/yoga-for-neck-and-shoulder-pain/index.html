On the mat

Yoga for neck and shoulder pain at a desk

The neck rarely causes its own trouble. It is usually doing the work of a mid-back that has stopped moving and shoulders that have crept up and stayed there.

By Master Aksharanand A S, founder of Santhi School of Yoga & Vedanta Studies Fort Kochi, Kerala Updated 24 September 2026 6 min read

In short

Desk-related neck and shoulder pain usually comes from a fixed position rather than a weak neck: the head drifts forward, the upper back stiffens and the shoulders lift. The useful work is opening the chest, restoring movement in the upper back, and releasing the muscles from ear to shoulder. Move every hour rather than stretching hard once a day, and be cautious with shoulderstand, headstand and deep backbends while the neck is sore.

Of all the complaints people bring to a first class, this is the most common, and it arrives in the same words almost every time: I carry everything in my shoulders.

The neck is usually not the culprit. It is the part that ends up paying.

What desk work actually does

Sit for a few hours and three things happen together. The head drifts forward of the shoulders, and every centimetre forward multiplies the load the neck muscles have to hold. The upper back — the part between the shoulder blades that is meant to extend and rotate — stops moving and stiffens in a slight rounding. And the shoulders lift towards the ears, especially when you are concentrating, and then simply stay there.

So the muscles from the base of the skull to the top of the shoulder are working all day, isometrically, without rest. They ache because they are tired, not because they are short.

That distinction matters, because it explains why aggressive neck stretching gives twenty minutes of relief and then nothing. The fix is to give the head somewhere to sit, get the upper back moving again, and let the shoulders down.

The hourly minute

This is more valuable than any sequence you do in the evening, because it interrupts the position before it sets.

  • Shoulder rolls, five backwards, slowly and fully.
  • Chin tuck, five times: draw the chin straight back, as if making a double chin, stacking the head over the spine. Hold three seconds. This is the single most useful movement on the list.
  • Ear to shoulder, twenty seconds each side, without pulling. Let the opposite shoulder drop.
  • Open the chest: hands behind you on the chair back or interlaced, lift the breastbone, three slow breaths.

Sixty seconds. Set a reminder, because you will not remember on your own.

A ten-minute sequence

On the floor, at the end of the day:

  1. Cat and cow, ten rounds, with attention on the upper back rather than the lower.
  2. Thread the needle, one minute each side. From all fours, slide one arm under the other, shoulder and ear towards the floor. This reaches the space between the shoulder blades that nothing else does.
  3. Child's pose with arms walked to one side, one minute each side, for the side of the ribs and the lat.
  4. Sphinx, one minute. Forearms down, chest lifted, shoulders drawing back. Gentle extension for a back stuck in flexion all day.
  5. Eagle arms, thirty seconds each side, seated, for the space between the shoulder blades.
  6. Reclining twist, one minute each side.
  7. Savasana, five minutes, with a folded towel under the head if the chin points upward.

Nothing here should be sharp. A stretch that makes you hold your breath is too much — in a class this is exactly where a teacher would stop you, which is one reason small classes matter for injuries.

Postures to be careful with

Yoga has a few postures that put real load through the neck, and a sore neck is the wrong time for them.

  • Shoulderstand and plough. These place the full weight of the body on the cervical spine. Skip them entirely while the neck is painful, and learn them with a teacher and folded blankets when it is not.
  • Headstand. Done correctly the arms carry most of the weight, but "done correctly" takes months. Not while the neck hurts. There is a proper progression here.
  • Deep backbends with the head dropped back. Keep the back of the neck long instead.
  • Holding the head up in cobra or locust. Look at the floor a little ahead of you, not at the ceiling.

Your desk, briefly

Screen top at eye level so the chin stays level. Elbows supported, so the shoulders are not holding the arms up all day — this is the one people miss, and it is often the whole problem. Phone calls on speaker or headphones, never wedged against the ear. And stand up every hour, whatever the reminder says you are in the middle of.

When to see someone

Stretching is not the answer to everything. See a doctor or physiotherapist if there is numbness, tingling or weakness down an arm, if pain follows a fall or accident, if it wakes you at night, or if it has not shifted in six weeks of sensible movement. Nerve pain in particular can be made worse by exactly the stretching that helps muscular tightness.

For ordinary desk stiffness, though, the prescription is dull and it works: move often, open the front of the chest, stop lifting your shoulders, and give the tired muscles a real rest at the end of the day. The same principles apply to backs.

Common questions

Can yoga fix neck and shoulder pain from desk work?

It helps most ordinary desk-related tightness, because the cause is a fixed position rather than a weak neck. The most effective part is not the evening sequence but moving briefly every hour so the position never sets.

Should I stretch my neck if it hurts?

Gently, and never to the point where you hold your breath. Most desk-related neck muscles are tired rather than short, so opening the chest and restoring upper-back movement usually helps more than pulling on the neck itself.

Which yoga poses should I avoid with neck pain?

Shoulderstand and plough, which load the cervical spine directly, and headstand until a teacher has taught you the progression. Also avoid dropping the head back in backbends, and keep the gaze down in cobra and locust.

When should I see a doctor about neck pain?

If there is numbness, tingling or weakness in an arm, if the pain followed a fall, if it wakes you at night, or if it has not improved after about six weeks of sensible movement.
