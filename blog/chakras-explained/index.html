Behind the practice

The seven chakras, explained simply

The chakras are a map drawn by practitioners of the inner body, not a diagram of anatomy. Understood that way, they are useful. Understood literally, they lead to some very silly places.

By Master Aksharanand A S, founder of Santhi School of Yoga & Vedanta Studies Fort Kochi, Kerala Updated 23 September 2026 6 min read

In short

The chakras are seven centres described in tantric and hatha yoga texts, running from the base of the spine to the crown of the head. They are a traditional model of experience — where attention gathers, where the breath seems to catch — rather than organs that can be scanned, blocked or unblocked for a fee.

Chakra means wheel. The system as most people meet it today comes largely from tantric texts and later hatha yoga manuals, and it was always a practitioner's map: a way of describing what is felt during long practice, in a vocabulary shared by teacher and student.

What a chakra is, and is not

It is not an organ. Nobody has ever removed one, photographed one or measured one, and the tradition never claimed you could. It is a location where practitioners consistently report sensation, and a peg on which the tradition hangs a set of associations: an element, a sound, a quality of mind.

Treated as a shared language for experience, the system is genuinely useful. Treated as physiology, it falls apart the moment anyone asks a straight question.

The seven, from the ground up

  • Muladhara, the root, at the base of the spine. Associated with earth, stability, and the plain business of being housed in a body. The bija sound is lam.
  • Svadhisthana, the sacral centre, low in the pelvis. Associated with water, appetite, creativity and change. Vam.
  • Manipura, at the navel. Associated with fire, digestion, effort and will — the centre most obviously worked by breathing practice. Ram.
  • Anahata, the heart. Associated with air, breath and affection. Its name means unstruck — a sound made without two things touching. Yam.
  • Vishuddha, the throat. Associated with space, speech and listening. Ham.
  • Ajna, between the eyebrows. Associated with perception and attention; the place a teacher will tell you to rest your eyes behind in meditation. Om.
  • Sahasrara, the crown. Described less as a centre than as an opening, and traditionally left alone until the rest is steady.

Where they show up in practice

You do not need to believe anything for the map to be useful.

  • In pranayama, attention is often placed at the navel or between the eyebrows. Both are chakra locations, and both are simply places attention rests easily.
  • In asana, backbends open the front of the chest and are traditionally spoken of as heart practices. Forward folds turn attention inward and downward.
  • In meditation, giving attention a specific place to sit is more effective than telling it to be quiet.
  • In mantra, the bija sounds above are used as anchors for repetition.

Where to be careful

The chakra system attracts more nonsense than any other part of yoga. A short list of things to walk away from:

  • Anyone diagnosing an illness as a blocked chakra.
  • Anyone selling a course, crystal or session to clear or balance one.
  • Any claim that a colour chart is ancient. The rainbow correspondence is largely a twentieth-century Western addition.
  • Any suggestion that forcing kundalini upward is a beginner's project. The tradition is emphatic that it is not.

A teacher worth studying with will tell you where the model comes from, what it is for, and where it stops.

If you want to go further

Read the eight limbs first — the chakras make more sense once the ethical and practical ground is in place. Our plain-language version is here, and what Vedanta is covers the philosophical side of the same tradition.

On our 200-hour training, philosophy is taught alongside daily practice rather than as a lecture bolted on at the end, which is the only way any of this becomes more than trivia.

Common questions

What are the seven chakras?

Muladhara at the base of the spine, Svadhisthana in the lower pelvis, Manipura at the navel, Anahata at the heart, Vishuddha at the throat, Ajna between the eyebrows and Sahasrara at the crown. They come from tantric and hatha yoga texts and describe centres of experience rather than physical organs.

Can a chakra be blocked?

Not in any measurable sense. The language of blockage is metaphorical within the tradition. Anyone offering to unblock a chakra for a fee, or explaining an illness that way, is best avoided; see a doctor for medical problems.

Are the chakra colours traditional?

The rainbow colour scheme is mostly a twentieth-century Western addition. Traditional texts describe each centre with a number of petals, a seed sound and an element rather than a colour from a chart.

Do I need to believe in chakras to practise yoga?

No. The system is a map for attention. You can practise asana, pranayama and meditation perfectly well without holding any view about it.
