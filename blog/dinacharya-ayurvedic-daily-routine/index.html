Dinacharya: the Ayurvedic daily routine

Ayurveda’s central claim about health is not about herbs or treatments. It is that doing the same things at the same times every day does more for you than anything you might take.

By Master Aksharanand A S, founder of Santhi School of Yoga & Vedanta Studies Fort Kochi, Kerala Updated 26 September 2026 8 min read

In short

Dinacharya is the Ayurvedic daily routine: wake before sunrise, scrape the tongue, clean the teeth, drink warm water, empty the bowels, oil the body, practise, bathe, eat the largest meal at midday, eat lightly in the evening and sleep early. Its logic is that the body runs on cycles and that keeping to them prevents illness. If you take only three things from it, take these: a consistent wake time, the largest meal in the middle of the day, and a light early dinner. Those three have the most support outside Ayurveda as well as within it.

Dina is day; charya is conduct or routine. The classical texts — the Charaka Samhita, the Ashtanga Hridayam — treat this daily order as preventive medicine, and give it more space than they give to treating disease.

Read cold, the full routine is intimidating and most of it will not survive contact with a normal working life. Read as a set of principles, several parts of it are the most practical health advice in the whole Ayurvedic system.

The idea behind it

Ayurveda holds that the qualities of the three doshas rise and fall through the day in a predictable cycle, roughly in four-hour blocks:

The Ayurvedic clock
TimeDominantQualities, and what it suits
6am - 10amKaphaHeavy, slow, stable. Why waking at eight can leave you groggier than waking at six. Good for exercise.
10am - 2pmPittaHot, sharp, transforming. Digestion at its strongest — hence the large midday meal. Good for demanding work.
2pm - 6pmVataLight, mobile, quick. Good for creative work, conversation, movement.
6pm - 10pmKaphaSlowing, settling. The window in which sleep comes easily. Miss it and you catch a second wind.
10pm - 2amPittaThe body’s repair and processing hours — which is the argument for being asleep through them.
2am - 6amVataLight, subtle. Dreams, and the window in which waking is easiest.

Whether or not the doshas are your preferred vocabulary, the underlying observation — that the body runs on a daily cycle and works better when you stop fighting it — is exactly what modern circadian research describes.

The morning, in order

The classical sequence, with an honest note on each.

The morning routine
StepWhat it is
Wake at brahma muhurtaThe hour and a half before sunrise, roughly 4:30 to 6. Considered the clearest, quietest time of day. More on this below.
Drink warm waterA glass, sometimes with lemon. Encourages the bowels to move.
Empty the bowelsAyurveda regards a regular morning bowel movement as a basic sign of health, and it is not wrong.
Scrape the tongueSeven to fourteen strokes, back to front, with a metal scraper. Takes thirty seconds.
Clean the teethTraditionally with a neem or khadira twig, which is astringent and mildly antibacterial.
Oil pulling — gandushaSwishing sesame or coconut oil in the mouth for several minutes, then spitting it out. Not down the sink.
NasyaA drop or two of oil in each nostril. Often preceded by jal neti.
AbhyangaSelf-massage with warm oil — sesame in cool weather, coconut in Kerala’s heat. Ten to twenty minutes, long strokes on the limbs, circles at the joints.
PractiseAsana and pranayama, to about half your capacity rather than to exhaustion. Classical Ayurveda is firm about the half.
BatheAfter the oil has been on the skin twenty minutes or so.
Meditate, then eatBreakfast light, and only if you are hungry.

That is a two-hour morning, which is why almost nobody does all of it. Our teacher training runs something close to it for twenty-eight days, and the thing students report afterwards is not that they kept the whole routine — it is that they kept two or three pieces of it for years.

About waking at 4:30

Brahma muhurta is the period of about ninety-six minutes before sunrise, and the texts are enthusiastic about it. In Kerala, where the sun comes up around six all year, that means waking between 4:30 and 5.

The honest position: the quality of that hour is real — it is quiet, cool, nothing is asked of you, and it is the easiest time to sit and not be interrupted. But waking early at the cost of sleep is a bad trade, and Ayurveda agrees; the texts assume you went to bed at nine.

If you cannot sleep by ten, do not set an alarm for 4:30. Shift the bedtime first and let the waking follow. A person sleeping five hours to be virtuous at dawn is not practising dinacharya, just being tired on purpose.

The rest of the day

  • The largest meal at midday, when digestive fire — agni — is strongest. This is the single most distinctive instruction in the routine and the one most at odds with modern habit.
  • A light evening meal, ideally before sunset, and at minimum two or three hours before bed.
  • No snacking between meals; let the previous one finish.
  • A short walk after eating — a hundred steps, the texts say, which is a charmingly precise instruction.
  • Wind down after dark. Light, screens and stimulation after sunset are the modern obstacle the texts could not have anticipated but plainly describe the opposite of.
  • Sleep by ten, in the kapha window when sleep comes easily, rather than pushing into the pitta hours and catching a second wind.

The food itself is a separate subject — see sattvic food, which covers what is eaten as opposed to when.

What holds up, and what is traditional

Worth separating, because this site does not sell the mystical version of anything.

Well supported outside Ayurveda:

  • Consistent sleep and wake times. Circadian research is unambiguous that regularity matters, arguably as much as duration.
  • Eating earlier in the day. A growing body of work on meal timing points the same way the Ayurvedic instruction does.
  • Not eating close to bedtime, for sleep quality and for reflux.
  • Tongue scraping, which measurably reduces the volatile sulphur compounds behind bad breath. A small claim, but a real one.
  • Morning light exposure, which is a side effect of being up at dawn and one of the strongest levers on the body clock.

Traditional, with thin or mixed evidence:

  • Oil pulling. Some small trials suggest a benefit for plaque and gum health; the quality is poor and it is no substitute for brushing.
  • Nasya. Pleasant in dry air; little studied.
  • Abhyanga. No good evidence for the detoxification claims. As a daily twenty minutes of unhurried touch and attention, it may well do something anyway.
  • The dosha clock itself, which is a model rather than a measurement.

None of the second list is harmful. The distinction matters only so you know which claims to repeat.

If you only take three things

This is what I actually suggest to students, rather than the full programme.

  1. Wake at the same time every day, including weekends. Earlier if it suits you, but consistent above all. This one does the most work.
  2. Make lunch the main meal and supper light and early. Expect a fortnight of feeling odd about it, then better sleep.
  3. Scrape your tongue. Thirty seconds, costs a few rupees, and it is the easiest habit on the list to keep.

Add abhyanga on the days you have twenty minutes. In Kerala, coconut oil, warmed, on the days when it is not already thirty degrees before breakfast.

And do not start all eleven steps on a Monday. The routine that survives is the one you barely notice doing.

Dinacharya in Kerala

Kerala is where Ayurveda is still ordinary rather than exotic — coconut oil in the kitchen and on the skin, warm water as the default drink, the big meal at midday, and a general suspicion of cold food.

If you are here and curious, our piece on Ayurveda and yoga in Kerala covers how to find a genuine practitioner rather than a spa treatment, and what a consultation actually involves. On the teacher training, the daily rhythm is the routine in practice: early practice, the main meal at midday, a light supper, early nights. Most people sleep better by the second week.

Common questions

What is dinacharya?

The Ayurvedic daily routine: waking before sunrise, scraping the tongue, cleaning the teeth, warm water, emptying the bowels, oil massage, practice, bathing, the largest meal at midday, a light early supper and an early night. The classical texts treat it as preventive medicine.

What time is brahma muhurta?

The period of roughly ninety-six minutes before sunrise — in Kerala, about 4:30 to 6. It is considered the quietest and clearest time of day for practice.

Do I have to wake up at 4am to follow Ayurveda?

No. Waking early at the cost of sleep is a bad trade, and the texts assume you went to bed at nine. Shift the bedtime first and let the waking time follow. A consistent wake time matters more than an early one.

Does tongue scraping actually do anything?

Yes, modestly: it measurably reduces the volatile sulphur compounds responsible for bad breath. It takes thirty seconds and is the easiest part of the routine to keep. It does not replace brushing.

Why is lunch the biggest meal in Ayurveda?

Because digestive fire, agni, is held to be strongest in the middle of the day, in the pitta hours between roughly 10am and 2pm. Eating the main meal then and keeping supper light and early is the instruction, and research on meal timing broadly points the same way.
