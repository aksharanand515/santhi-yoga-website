# Chandra Namaskar: the *moon salutation*

Everyone learns the sun salutation. Almost nobody is taught its counterpart, which is a shame on a Kerala afternoon when the last thing your body wants is more heat.

By **Master Aksharanand A S**, founder of Santhi School of Yoga & Vedanta Studies Fort Kochi, Kerala Updated 26 September 2026 7 min read

![Three students practising beside tall windows looking out onto trees](https://santhiyogaindia.com/assets/img/handstand-practice-yoga-retreat-800.webp)

The moon sequence is practised slowly, and takes longer than it looks

In short

Chandra Namaskar is a flowing sequence of fourteen positions, one for each phase of the moon, practised as a cooling counterpart to Surya Namaskar. It follows a similar shape but adds lateral movement — a crescent bend to each side and a wide-legged squat — and is done slowly, with a long exhalation. Practise it in the evening, in hot weather, when you are depleted, or on days when a heating practice does not appeal. It is a much younger and less standardised sequence than the sun salutation, and versions differ between schools.

Chandra is the moon; namaskar is a salutation. Where [Surya Namaskar](https://santhiyogaindia.com/blog/surya-namaskar-steps/) builds heat, this releases it.

One honest thing before the sequence, because you will not read it in most descriptions: the moon salutation is not ancient. The sun salutation itself is younger than people assume, and the moon salutation is younger still — a twentieth-century development, with several competing versions and no single canonical form. What follows is the version we teach. Another school will show you a different one, and neither of us is wrong.

## How it differs from the sun salutation

|  | Surya Namaskar | Chandra Namaskar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Effect** | Heating, stimulating | Cooling, settling |
| **Direction** | Forward and back, in one plane | Adds sideways movement |
| **Pace** | Can be brisk; often repeated many times | Slow, one or two rounds is plenty |
| **Breath** | Even, one movement per breath | Long exhalations, pauses allowed |
| **Time of day** | Morning, facing the sunrise | Evening, or any time heat is unwelcome |
| **Positions** | Twelve, for the solar year | Fourteen, for the waxing or waning phases |

The fourteen positions are matched to the fourteen phases between new moon and full, which is the kind of correspondence traditional systems enjoy. Treat it as a mnemonic rather than astronomy.

## The sequence

Start standing at the front of the mat, feet together, palms at the chest. Move at about half the speed you think is right.

| # | Position | Breath |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Prayer pose** — pranamasana. Standing, palms together at the heart, eyes closed for a breath or two. | Exhale |
| 2 | **Raised arms** — hasta uttanasana. Arms overhead, a gentle arch, chest lifting. | Inhale |
| 3 | **Crescent to the right** — ardha chandrasana. Hands still joined overhead, bend sideways to the right. Hips stay level. | Exhale |
| 4 | **Crescent to the left**. The same to the other side. This pair is what makes the sequence lunar rather than solar. | Inhale, exhale |
| 5 | **Standing forward bend** — padahastasana. Fold from the hips, knees softly bent. | Exhale |
| 6 | **Low lunge, right foot back** — ashwa sanchalanasana. Back knee down, chest lifting, gaze forward. | Inhale |
| 7 | **Crescent lunge** — anjaneyasana. From the lunge, arms sweep overhead and the chest opens. The moon shape again. | Inhale |
| 8 | **Wide squat** — malasana. Step forward, turn the feet out, sink into a low squat with the palms at the chest. | Exhale |
| 9 | **Side lunge** — skandasana. From the squat, straighten one leg to the side and shift the weight over the bent knee. | Inhale |
| 10 | **Side lunge, other side**. Shift the weight across, slowly, keeping the hips low. | Exhale |
| 11 | **Wide squat again**. Return to centre. | Inhale |
| 12 | **Crescent lunge, left foot back**. Rising out of the squat into the lunge on the second side. | Exhale |
| 13 | **Standing forward bend**. Step the feet together and fold. | Inhale |
| 14 | **Raised arms, then prayer**. Rise, arch gently, and return the palms to the heart. | Exhale |

That is one half round. A full round repeats it leading with the other leg, so that the body is treated evenly — which matters more here than in the sun salutation because of the asymmetric lunges.

Two full rounds is a complete practice. Six is a great deal. If you find yourself doing twelve to get warm, do sun salutations instead; you are asking the wrong sequence to do a different job.

## The breath

The breath pattern above is a guide rather than a rule. The principle is simpler than the table: **lengthen the exhalation**.

In Surya Namaskar the breath drives the movement and the pace is brisk enough that you take what you get. Here, let each exhalation run longer than the inhalation — roughly four counts in, six out — and allow a pause at the bottom of the breath. A long out-breath is the most reliable way to shift the nervous system towards its calming side, which is the whole purpose of the sequence.

If you lose the pattern, breathe normally and keep moving slowly. A lost count is not worth stopping for.

## When to practise it

- **Evening.** The obvious one. It works well before a [sleep sequence](https://santhiyogaindia.com/blog/yoga-for-better-sleep/), and far better than sun salutations at nine at night.
- **Hot weather.** In April in Fort Kochi, a room at thirty-two degrees does not need twelve rounds of anything heating. This is when we teach it most.
- **When you are depleted** — after illness, travel, a poor night. It gives you a practice on days when the alternative is no practice.
- **During menstruation**, if a heating practice does not appeal. See [yoga and your period](https://santhiyogaindia.com/blog/yoga-and-your-period/).
- **As a warm-up for sitting.** Two slow rounds before [meditation](https://santhiyogaindia.com/blog/how-to-sit-for-meditation/) settles the body without waking it up.

Some teachers practise it on the full moon, and there is a pleasant ritual in that. It is not required.

## Practical notes

- **The crescent bends are not deep.** Lengthen up before you lean over. A side bend that collapses is a compression of the lower ribs, not a stretch.
- **Pad the back knee** in the lunges. A folded blanket costs nothing and changes the pose from endurance to practice. See our guide to [props](https://santhiyogaindia.com/blog/yoga-props-blocks-straps/).
- **The squat is the hard one** for most Western-trained bodies. Heels on a rolled blanket if they lift, and keep the chest up rather than rounding forward.
- **The side lunge needs warm hips.** If it is the first thing you do in the day, do a few rounds of something gentler first, and read [yoga for tight hips](https://santhiyogaindia.com/blog/yoga-for-tight-hips/).
- **Knees.** Deep squats and side lunges load the knee at an angle. If you have knee trouble, reduce the depth and keep the knee tracking over the foot.

## Is it traditional?

Worth being straight about, because the word "ancient" gets attached to everything in this field.

Surya Namaskar as we practise it was assembled and popularised in the early twentieth century, drawing on older material. Chandra Namaskar came later still, and versions differ substantially: some open with a wide-legged stance throughout, some use the standing half-moon posture, some run to fourteen positions and others to fifteen or seventeen.

None of that makes it less useful. A sequence is a tool, and this one does something the classical repertoire does not do as neatly — it gives you a flowing practice that cools rather than heats. But if a school tells you it descends unchanged from the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, they are embroidering.

## Common questions

**What is the difference between sun and moon salutation?**

Surya Namaskar heats and stimulates, moves forward and back in one plane, and is often repeated many times briskly. Chandra Namaskar cools and settles, adds sideways movement through crescent bends and side lunges, and is practised slowly with long exhalations — one or two rounds is a complete practice.

**When should I practise chandra namaskar?**

In the evening, in hot weather, when you are tired or recovering, or on days when a heating practice does not appeal. It also works as a gentle warm-up before sitting meditation.

**How many rounds of moon salutation should I do?**

Two full rounds is a complete practice, and six is a great deal. If you find yourself doing twelve to feel anything, do sun salutations instead — you are asking a cooling sequence to do a heating job.

**Is chandra namaskar traditional?**

Less than it is usually presented as. Surya Namaskar in its current form was assembled in the early twentieth century, and the moon salutation is later still, with several competing versions and no canonical sequence. That does not make it less useful, but it is not an ancient form.

**Why fourteen positions?**

One for each phase of the moon between new and full. It is a traditional correspondence and a helpful way to remember the count rather than a piece of astronomy.
