# 200-hour vs 300-hour teacher training: *the real difference*

The numbers describe length, not level. Here is what a 200-hour course covers, what a 300-hour adds, and which one you should be looking at.

By **Master Aksharanand A S**, founder of Santhi School of Yoga & Vedanta Studies Fort Kochi, Kerala Updated 20 September 2026 5 min read

In short

**A 200-hour course is the foundation: it teaches you to practise properly and to lead a general class. A 300-hour course is what you take afterwards, to go deeper into anatomy, philosophy, sequencing and teaching itself.** You cannot substitute one for the other, and nobody should start with a 300-hour.

The hour counts come from Yoga Alliance, an American registry that set 200 and 300 as its training standards. Almost every school worldwide now uses the same numbers, whether or not it registers with them.

## The short version

- **200-hour:** your first training. Practice, technique, the basics of teaching, philosophy, anatomy. Usually three to four weeks residential, or several months part-time.
- **300-hour:** the advanced course for people who already hold a 200-hour. Deeper study, more teaching practice, more subtle work.
- **500-hour:** not a separate course but the two added together, 200 plus 300.

## What a 200-hour actually covers

A good 200-hour changes your own practice first and your teaching second. Expect:

- **Asana** in detail — alignment, sequencing and how to hold a posture rather than pass through it.
- **Pranayama** taught carefully, without strain.
- **Meditation** as a daily habit rather than a lecture.
- **Philosophy** — where the practice comes from and why it is shaped the way it is.
- **Teaching methodology** — how to cue, watch a room, and hold space for people.
- **Anatomy** enough to keep people safe.

Twenty-eight days is not long. Anyone who tells you that you will leave a 200-hour as a finished teacher is selling something; you leave able to teach a careful beginners' class, and with a practice you can keep building.

## What a 300-hour adds

A 300-hour assumes the foundation and goes further: advanced asana and the mechanics behind it, deeper pranayama and kriya, more philosophy read at length, the business of teaching, and much more time teaching under supervision. Many people take it two or three years after their first course, once they know what they actually want to study.

## Which one you should be looking at

- **You have never trained:** a 200-hour, without exception.
- **You trained years ago and feel rusty:** another 200-hour is a perfectly respectable choice, and often more useful than an advanced course.
- **You teach regularly and want depth:** a 300-hour, ideally with a teacher whose own practice you admire.
- **You want to deepen your practice with no plan to teach:** a 200-hour. Most people on ours are in exactly this position.

## Where Yoga Alliance fits, plainly

Yoga Alliance is a voluntary registry, not a licensing body. Registering with them as RYT 200 or RYT 500 is useful mainly if you intend to teach at studios or gyms that ask for it, which is common in North America, Europe and Australia, and less common elsewhere.

We are direct about this: **Santhi School of Yoga & Vedanta Studies is not a Yoga Alliance Registered Yoga School.** Graduates of our 200-hour course receive our own certificate and cannot register as RYT 200. If that credential matters for where you intend to teach, choose a registered school — we will tell you so if you ask. ## Common questions

**What is the difference between 200-hour and 300-hour yoga teacher training?**

A 200-hour is the foundation course that qualifies you to teach a general class. A 300-hour is the advanced course taken afterwards, going deeper into anatomy, philosophy, pranayama and teaching practice. Together they make up what is often called a 500-hour training.

**Can I do a 300-hour teacher training first?**

No. A 300-hour course assumes you already hold a 200-hour certificate and have been practising and usually teaching. Schools will ask for proof of the earlier training.

**Is a 200-hour training enough to teach yoga?**

It is enough to begin teaching a careful beginners’ class, and it is the standard most studios ask for. It is not enough to make anyone an experienced teacher; that comes from teaching, continued study and your own daily practice.
