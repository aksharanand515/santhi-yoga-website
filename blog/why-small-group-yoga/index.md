# Why we keep classes small, and *what that changes*

A yoga class of a dozen people and one of forty are different activities. What changes when a group stays small matters more than the music, the lighting or the mat.

By **Master Aksharanand A S**, founder of Santhi School of Yoga & Vedanta Studies Fort Kochi, Kerala Updated 23 September 2026 4 min read

![A teacher adjusting a student’s posture by hand during a class in Fort Kochi](https://santhiyogaindia.com/assets/img/hands-on-yoga-adjustment-fort-kochi-800.webp)

Hands-on correction is only possible when the teacher can see everyone

In short

In a small yoga class the teacher can see every student, which is what makes individual correction possible. Misalignments get caught early rather than repeated hundreds of times, beginners are given a version of each posture that suits them instead of copying the back row, and experienced students get specific feedback on a practice that may have run on autopilot for years.

There is a simple reason the classes at Santhi have always stayed small: the teacher needs to be able to see everyone.

That sounds obvious. It is also the thing that quietly disappears when a class gets bigger. Here is what changes when a group stays small — and why it matters more than the music, the lighting or the mat.

## The teacher actually sees you

In a large class, instruction has to be general. “Lengthen your spine.” “Engage your core.” The teacher demonstrates at the front and hopes. In a small class the teacher walks the room, watches each person breathe into a posture, and speaks to *your* spine, *your* knee, *your* habit of holding your breath.

This is not a luxury. It is how Hatha yoga has always been passed on — from teacher to student, one correction at a time.

## Corrections arrive early, not late

Most yoga injuries do not come from one dramatic moment. They come from repeating a small misalignment hundreds of times: a knee drifting inwards in a lunge, a lower back taking the load of a backbend, a neck compressed in a shoulderstand.

A teacher with a dozen students can catch those patterns on day one. With forty, they may never see them. It is the same reasoning behind the advice in [yoga for back pain](https://santhiyogaindia.com/blog/yoga-for-back-pain/): what protects you is attention, not caution alone.

## Beginners stop hiding

In large classes, beginners often go to the back row and try to copy the person in front. In a small room there is no back row. That can feel exposing for about ten minutes, and then it becomes the opposite: you realise you will be given a version of each posture that suits you, and that nobody is watching except the one person whose job is to help.

## Advanced students go deeper

Small groups are not only for beginners. Experienced practitioners get something rarer: specific, honest feedback on a practice that may have run on autopilot for years. The long holds and slow pace of [a traditional two-hour class](https://santhiyogaindia.com/blog/inside-a-two-hour-hatha-class/) give room to work on the details that large classes rush past.

## The room becomes quiet

A small group settles faster. There is less shuffling, less waiting, less noise. Pranayama is easier to hear yourself in. The final relaxation goes deeper. It is hard to overstate how much the texture of a class changes when there are fewer bodies in it.

## What it costs the school

Honestly: income. A studio that fills every mat makes more money. We have chosen to stay small anyway — for the daily classes, the [private sessions](https://santhiyogaindia.com/services/#private), and especially the [teacher training](https://santhiyogaindia.com/teacher-training/), where a small batch means every trainee is corrected, and teaches, every single day.

If you have only practised in large classes, try one small one. Come to a [daily class in Fort Kochi](https://santhiyogaindia.com/services/#daily-classes) and tell the teacher what you would like help with. You will know within the first half hour whether it makes a difference.

## Common questions

**Are small yoga classes better for beginners?**

Generally, yes. In a small group the teacher can see every student, offer an easier version of a posture before strain sets in, and correct alignment early — which is exactly what beginners need most.

**How many people are in a class at Santhi?**

Numbers vary by season and by whether it is the morning or afternoon class, but the class is deliberately kept to a size where the teacher can watch and correct each person. Message us if you would like to know what a particular day looks like.

**Is a private session better than a small class?**

They do different jobs. A private session is the fastest way to build foundations or work around an injury, because the whole hour is yours. A small group adds the rhythm of practising alongside others, which most people find easier to keep up.

**Do experienced practitioners get anything from a small class?**

Often more than beginners do. Long holds and a slow pace leave room to work on details, and a teacher who can see you will name habits you stopped noticing years ago.
